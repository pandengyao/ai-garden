## 6 Trace Generation: Teachers, Self, and Search

Section 5 discussed where training questions come from; this section discusses who writes the reasoning traces (our assessment). By the source of the supervision signal, existing approaches fall into five classes: a stronger teacher writes traces offline; the model samples on its own and filters by answer; tree search or evolutionary algorithms improve traces on top of sampling; RL mixes teacher traces into the model's own rollouts; and on-policy distillation has the student generate on its own while the teacher scores it token by token (our assessment). This section is organized around three questions: which signal is more effective under controlled comparison, whether a stronger teacher is always better, and how to trade off generation cost against data efficiency (our assessment). Retention criteria are covered in Section 7, selection and mixing ratios in Section 8, and agentic trajectories in Section 9 (our assessment). Table 6-1 subdivides these five classes into eight approaches and summarizes their signal sources, retained content, costs, and representative numbers (our count; each row is based on the references it cites).

### 6.1 Offline Teacher Distillation and Teacher Selection

**Lineage and mechanism.** In offline teacher distillation, a teacher writes a trace for each question, and after filtering the traces are used for the student's SFT (our assessment). Distilling Step-by-Step uses rationales generated by an LLM as additional supervision in a multi-task framework[^73]. Its 770M T5, using only 80% of a benchmark's data, surpasses the 540B PaLM with few-shot prompting, whereas the same T5 under standard fine-tuning still struggles to catch up even with the full 100% of the data[^73]. Orca has a 13B student learn GPT-4's explanation traces and step-by-step thought processes, with ChatGPT as a teacher assistant[^67]. It is first trained on ChatGPT responses for FLAN-5M and then on GPT-4 responses for FLAN-1M[^67]. The authors' reason for adding an intermediate teacher is the capacity gap: the 13B student is much smaller than GPT-4, and an intermediate teacher with a smaller capability gap can improve imitation learning[^67].

Offline distillation in the long-CoT stage can be illustrated with DeepSeek-R1 (our assessment). Its reasoning data comes from rejection sampling on the checkpoint of the first RL stage: multiple responses are sampled per question, only the correct ones are kept, and chains of thought with language mixing, long paragraphs, or code blocks are filtered out[^80]. This yields about 600k reasoning samples and about 200k non-reasoning samples, and these 800k examples are used to fine-tune base models of the Qwen2.5 and Llama series for 2–3 epochs[^80]. On AIME 2024, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B has a pass@1 of 72.6, whereas Qwen2.5-32B-Zero, which applies more than 10K steps of RL directly to the same base model, reaches only 47.0, comparable to the 50.0 of QwQ-32B-Preview[^80]. The authors therefore argue that large-scale RL on small models is enormously costly and may still fall short of distillation[^80]. Qwen3 applies two-stage strong-to-weak distillation to the dense models from Qwen3-0.6B to 14B and to Qwen3-30B-A3B[^70]. The first stage combines the teacher's outputs in the /think and /no_think modes for off-policy response distillation; in the second stage the student generates sequences, whose logits are then aligned with those of Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B[^70]. Starting from the same 8B checkpoint after off-policy distillation and using only math and code questions, on-policy distillation raises AIME24 from 55.0 to 74.4 in 1,800 GPU hours, while RL reaches only 67.6 in 17,920 GPU hours[^70].

**Evidence: a stronger teacher is not necessarily better.** RevisitCapacityGap points out protocol flaws in existing capacity-gap studies, including the lack of a comparison with the student before distillation, and cross-teacher filtering that keeps only questions all teachers answer correctly, which deprives strong teachers of the correct data unique to them[^22]. On five math benchmarks, with teachers such as Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, the distilled student is in most configurations actually worse than before distillation[^22]. When Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct are used instead as the small and large teachers and 15 BBH tasks are re-evaluated under the practical protocol, the capacity-gap effect appears in 8 of the 11 tasks where distillation is effective[^22]. With Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and QwQ-32B-Preview as the short and long teachers, it appears in only 2 of 10 tasks[^22]. Cross-teacher filtering reduces the large teacher's data from 1,928 to 1,684 examples (−12.7%) but the small teacher's only from 1,769 to 1,684 (−4.8%), and the average accuracy across four student sizes drops by 3.61 and 1.78 respectively[^22]. The authors therefore argue that the advantage of strong teachers comes mainly from more usable data rather than from higher CoT quality[^22].

TeacherGRPO calls the opposite phenomenon the Gap Curse: the more complex the teacher, the further its distribution departs from what a capacity-limited student can approximate[^49]. With Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as the teacher and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct as the student, aligning the teacher toward the student with KD for 40 steps on Date Understanding raises the student's accuracy from 30.2% to 38.5%[^49]. When the alignment continues to step 80, the teacher drops from 59.2% to 43.8%, and the student also drops to 29.0%[^49]. When the alignment uses GRPO with reverse KL as the reward instead, the teacher is still at 58.5% at step 80[^49]. The full TeacherGRPO is on average 7.4 and 3.6 points higher than the original distillation methods on the Qwen and Gemma families[^49]. Strong Teacher Not Needed performs logit-level distillation in the pretraining stage, with a 1.7B student model trained on 50B tokens[^30]. When all teachers are trained on 300B tokens, the smaller the teacher, the better the student: 1.7B, 3.8B, and 8.0B teachers bring downstream improvements of +4.3%, +3.5%, and +2.8% respectively[^30]. The 8.0B teacher yields the largest gain at 30B tokens (+4.3%), which falls to +2.8% after it is trained to 300B tokens[^30]. Knowledge Distillation During Mid-Training uses OLMo-2 1B as the student and 1B to 13B Instruct models as teachers, and finds that forward-KL distillation slows the acquisition of factual recall during mid-training, while reasoning still improves[^44]. Among these, the 7B teacher is generally better than the 13B teacher[^44]. After routing by the teacher's predictive entropy and distilling only on tokens where the teacher is confident, the reasoning macro-average with the 7B teacher rises from 26.1% under NTP to 44.7%, while factual recall is 29.3%, versus 30.3% for NTP[^44]. There is similar evidence in on-policy distillation: in the code experiments of Ask to Distill (A2D), with Qwen3-4B as the student, the teacher-gap recovery rate is 82.9% with a 4B teacher but only 32.8% with the stronger 30B-A3B teacher[^56]. With the stronger teacher, the same student's post-distillation average score is actually lower[^56].

**Limitations and disputes.** These conclusions are not necessarily contradictory; they vary different variables (our inference). RevisitCapacityGap's conclusion addresses the case where candidate teachers differ widely in performance, Strong Teacher Not Needed varies the teacher's parameter count and number of training tokens, and TeacherGRPO targets reasoning distillation between small models (our inference, based on [^22][^30][^49]). RevisitCapacityGap also acknowledges that the capacity gap may matter more when candidate teachers perform similarly[^22]. Teacher selection depends at least on the gap between candidate teachers, the teacher-student capacity ratio, and the training stage (our assessment, based on [^22][^44][^56]). RevisitCapacityGap's practical protocol covers only BBH and mostly uses single runs, and TeacherGRPO leaves 7B→1.5B and larger teacher-student pairs to future work[^22][^49].

### 6.2 Self-Generation and Rejection Sampling

**Lineage and mechanism.** Self-generation has the model being trained write its own traces, which are then filtered by final answer or by tests (our assessment). STaR has the model generate rationales for a large number of questions from a few examples; when an answer is wrong, it rewrites the rationale given the correct answer, and it fine-tunes only on rationales whose final answer is correct, iterating the process[^82]. ReST-EM builds the same kind of loop on an expectation-maximization framework: generate samples, filter them by binary feedback, fine-tune on the retained samples, and repeat for several rounds[^81]. TRICE treats the CoT as a latent variable and uses MCMC-EM to sample from the posterior over rationales conditioned on the correct answer[^90]. Compared with STaR, it ignores hard questions less often and can make use of incorrect rationales[^90]. Following these two lines of work, Inverse Reasoning Models trains an inverse model that generates a CoT conditioned on the answer, for use on hard questions where rejection sampling yields no signal[^52]. In the long CoT stage, this loop is still one component of mainstream recipes (our assessment). DeepSeek-R1's reasoning data, about 600k samples, came from rejection sampling on its own first-stage RL checkpoint[^80].

**Evidence.** With GPT-J as the base model, STaR with rewriting reaches 72.5% on CommonsenseQA, comparable to the 73.0% of a fine-tuned model 30 times larger[^82]. On GSM8K, STaR without rewriting outperforms all baselines while using only 25.0% of the training data[^82]. ReST-EM's gains on MATH and APPS with PaLM 2 grow with model size and significantly exceed those of fine-tuning on human data alone[^81]. TRICE performs prompt tuning on PaLM 2-M and matches its cost per iteration to that of rejection sampling[^90]. With bootstrapped initialization on 27 BBH tasks, the greedy accuracies of STaR, rejection sampling and TRICE are 62.0, 64.6 and 72.8, respectively[^90]. On GSM8K, rejection sampling scores 77.9, higher than TRICE with bootstrapped initialization (74.7) and on par with TRICE without bootstrapped initialization (77.7), while STaR scores 53.5[^90]. The authors state overall that TRICE outperforms rejection sampling, which the GSM8K numbers do not support (our assessment, based on [^90]). Xiong et al. compare rejection sampling with RL, using about 860k Numina-Math questions as the question bank, with 1024 questions per round and 4 responses per question[^151]. The averages of Qwen2.5-Math-7B-base over Math500, Minerva and OlympiadBench are: base model 23.6, RAFT (which learns only from positive samples) 52.3, PPO 52.5, RAFT++ 56.1 and GRPO 56.3[^151]. Ablations show that GRPO's main advantage comes from discarding questions on which every response is wrong, not from reward normalization[^151]. One round of offline distillation with the inverse model on Qwen3-0.6B, on which GRPO has already saturated, raises pass@1 on hard Countdown questions from 0.08 to 0.19[^52]. On the 17% of new questions that the model had never solved, every rollout in each GRPO group is wrong and the gradient stays at zero, yet the inverse model produces forward-consistent CoTs for 30% of them[^52].

**Limitations and controversies.** ReST-EM's gains diminish after more than a few iterations, which the authors attribute to overfitting on the small number of training questions[^81]. Learning only from positive samples makes policy entropy fall faster; RAFT++ then stalls and is overtaken by GRPO, and the authors argue that negative samples help sustain exploration[^151]. This comparison is evaluated with avg@16 under a 4096-token cap[^151]. There is no evidence yet on whether this conclusion holds in long CoT settings (our assessment). When trained with NLL alone, the inverse model ignores the given answer and needs an added contrastive loss[^52]. The authors also acknowledge that an inverse model that merely restates the answer would pass the forward-consistency check as well[^52]. On GSM8K, its pass@32 gain over rejection sampling on hard questions is at most 0.03, and at high mixing ratios its pass@1 even falls below that of rejection sampling and of the initial model[^52]. For self-verification approaches that do not rely on reference answers (such as Self-Verified Distillation), see Section 7 (our assessment). Apriel-Reasoner applies RL post-training across five domains to the 15B Apriel-Base using only public data, and under a 16K output budget its reasoning traces become 30–50% shorter[^20]. However, its base model went through continued pretraining and instruction tuning that included explicit reasoning traces, and the authors leave a same-base comparison with distillation baselines such as DeepSeek-R1-Distill to future work[^20]. It therefore cannot serve as evidence that approaches without teacher traces beat distillation (our assessment).

### 6.3 Traces from Search and Evolutionary Generation

**Lineage and mechanism.** TS-LLM treats tree search as a policy-improvement operator: search finds better traces, which are then distilled back into the policy while a value function is trained at the same time[^135]. It replaces prompting an LLM for scores with a learned value function and can handle search trees of depth 64[^135]. What later work mainly changes is what to keep from the tree (our assessment). AlphaMath uses only 15k question-answer pairs from GSM8K and MATH, running MCTS with DeepSeekMath-Base-7B combined with a Python interpreter and a value head[^137]. Each round it builds 10 trees per question and obtains 57k to 59k positive examples; the policy learns only from correct paths, while the value head learns from both correct and incorrect paths[^137]. MCTS-DPO pairs the candidate steps with the highest and lowest Q values at the same depth into DPO preference pairs[^138]. ReST-MCTS\* uses the reference answer to infer a process reward for each step, which serves both as the PRM training target and as the basis for filtering self-training traces[^136]. rStar-Math runs four rounds of self-evolution on 747k questions, with 16 MCTS rollouts per question per round (limited to 8 in the first round), and keeps only nodes whose Python code executes successfully[^78]. From the correct traces it takes the two with the highest average Q value as SFT data[^78]. O1 Replication Journey instead weaves the erroneous steps in the search tree, together with backtracking and reflection, into long CoTs[^91]. Rather than searching within a single model's sampling space, CoT-Evo performs novelty-driven selection, recombination and mutation on traces from multiple teachers such as Qwen3-32B-think and DeepSeek-R1[^8]. Its fitness considers answer correctness, coherence and use of knowledge[^8]. MIMIC has the language model articulate the reasoning while deterministic code execution guarantees the computation, anchoring the natural-language CoT to the execution traces of instrumented programs[^46].

**Evidence.** On GSM8K, after one round of updates the greedy accuracy of TS-LLM's LLaMA2-7B policy rises from 41.4 to 47.9, while the rejection-sampling baselines RFT-50 and RFT-100 reach 47.0 and 47.5[^135]. After three rounds of AlphaMath, greedy-decoding MATH accuracy rises from 33.2 to 53.6, still below the 57.4 of DeepSeekMath-Instruct, which was trained on 776k GPT-4 and human samples[^137]. With step-level beam search it reaches 66.3[^137], and the excess comes from inference-time search (our assessment). Starting from Mistral-7B after SFT on Arithmo (about 540K math and code problems), MCTS-DPO raises GSM8K from 75.9 to 81.8 and MATH from 28.9 to 34.7[^138]. After two rounds of ReST-MCTS\*, the average of LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct over MATH, GPQA-Diamond and CEval-Hard rises from 24.78 to 29.02, against 26.83 for ReST-EM and 27.73 for Self-Rewarding[^136]. The pass@1 of rStar-Math's Qwen2.5-Math-7B policy model on MATH rises over four rounds from 58.8 to 78.4, higher than the 75.3 of the bootstrap model[^78]. With deepseek-math-7b-base fine-tuned on Abel and on PRM800K, respectively, as the base, training on 327 long traces constructed from the reasoning tree scores 8.4% and 8.0% higher on MATH500 than training on 327 shortcut traces derived from the same tree[^91]. CoT-Evo's relative gains over single-teacher and multi-teacher distillation are 12.6% and 8.4% on BioProBench and 27.0% and 19.3% on ChemCoTBench[^8]. It also beats Best-of-K, which samples 21 traces from each teacher and then picks the best, a candidate count equal to the maximum number of traces CoT-Evo produces for a single question[^8]. In a comparison of problem-statement formats on Qwen3-8B, original Codeforces statements make the model switch to writing code in 38.7% of cases, MIMIC's narrative-fused statements cut this to 0.7%, and accuracy rises from 23.8% to 42.2%[^46]. In a controlled ablation, with SFT on Qwen3-4B in both arms and identical test cases, training on original Codeforces problems instead of MIMIC data scores 16.5 points lower on general reasoning and 21.6 points lower on math[^46].

**Limitations and controversies.** Search methods generally depend on reference answers (our assessment, based on [^136][^137][^138]). With answer guidance, the accuracy of MCTS-DPO's training signal rises from 81.2% to 97.9%; without it, MATH is 32.2, close to the offline variant's 31.9[^138]. The gains of ReST-MCTS\* are also limited: untrained few-shot prompting of LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct already reaches 28.99, and Mistral-7B's MATH score falls from 31.06 in the first round to 24.40 in the second[^136]. rStar-Math claims not to rely on distillation from stronger models, yet in its first round it runs MCTS with the 236B DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct to collect SFT data[^78]. Its headline figure of 90.0% also adds 64 test-time search traces on top[^78]. To isolate the contribution of the training data, the policy model's 78.4 is the number to use (our assessment, based on [^78]). The cost-matched evidence is also inconsistent (our assessment). ReST-MCTS\* reports outperforming Best-of-N and Tree-of-Thought under the same search budget[^136]. Yet TS-LLM's own token-matched comparison shows that on GSM8K, CoT-SC with ORM voting gets 52.3 with 500 tokens, while MCTS-α gets 51.9 with 561 tokens[^135]. On the other four tasks most tree-search variants still come out ahead, and the authors acknowledge that the overhead of node expansion and value evaluation makes them hard to scale[^135]. The most likely explanatory variable is the search depth a task requires (our inference). In their single-path comparison, the authors note that MCTS-α and MCTS-Rollout achieve only comparable accuracy on shallow-search tasks such as GSM8K, Game24 and ProntoQA, but come out ahead on deep-search tasks such as RLHF and chess endgames[^135]. TS-LLM performs only one round of updates, and its RFT baseline samples 10 traces per question and keeps the top 5; there is no evidence yet on how the gap between the two changes over multiple iterations (our assessment, based on [^135]). CoT-Evo's fitness is scored by an LLM, which the authors acknowledge may introduce bias[^8]. The students in this section's training experiments are all 8B or smaller (our count). The reported numbers of iterations are also small: one round for TS-LLM, two for ReST-MCTS\* and three for AlphaMath[^135][^136][^137]. rStar-Math uses four rounds[^78].

### 6.4 Off-Policy Guidance in Reinforcement Learning

**Lineage and mechanism.** LUFFY argues that purely on-policy RLVR can learn only from the model's own outputs and cannot acquire reasoning beyond its initial capability[^140]. It puts teacher traces into the rollout group, with 1 DeepSeek-R1 trace plus 7 of the model's own rollouts per question, so that the model imitates the teacher when its own attempts fail and keeps exploring when they succeed[^140]. The training questions are 94k questions from OpenR1-Math-220k, of which 45k remain after filtering out traces longer than 8192 tokens or judged wrong by Math-Verify[^140]. To avoid entropy collapse and superficial imitation, it uses regularized importance sampling to amplify the learning signal of low-probability key actions[^140]. Hindsight Hint Distillation (HHD) targets SWE-bench tasks with about 100 turns of interaction and needs only question-answer pairs without CoT[^24]. It distills a one-sentence hint from the model's own failed rollouts and injects it only at the start, so the whole trajectory is still generated by the model itself[^24]. The successful trajectories from its own rollouts and from the hinted rollouts are then pooled for SFT[^24].

**Evidence.** With Qwen2.5-Math-7B as the student, LUFFY averages 50.1 on six competition benchmarks, 6.4 higher than previous RLVR methods and 4.6 higher than on-policy RL in the same setting[^140]. Compared with SFT followed by RL, it is 1.9 higher in-distribution and 16.1 higher out-of-distribution, using only 59% of the GPU time (77×8 vs. 130×8 hours)[^140]. On a subset of hard questions, the training reward of on-policy RL for LLaMA-3.1-8B collapses to zero, while LUFFY still improves steadily[^140]. With Qwen2.5-72B-base as the backbone, pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified is: rejection-sampling fine-tuning 44.0, Agent-RLVR-Plan (in which an expert takes over midway) 45.6, Dense Expert Judge (step-by-step expert rejection sampling) 45.8 and HHD 51.2[^24]. HHD-M, which injects the same hint midway through the trajectory instead, reaches only 47.2[^24]. The expert providing guidance in the baselines is DeepSeek-V3, which is weaker than the rollout model on this benchmark; the authors argue that this weakens the confound from knowledge distillation[^24].

**Limitations and controversies.** The HHD authors argue that expert takeover forces the model to predict the expert's continuation on its own prefix, which violates autoregressive consistency[^24]. The off-policy baselines have slightly higher rollout success rates, yet the data they produce are harder to learn[^24]. LUFFY likewise observes that SFT directly on teacher traces leads to superficial, rigid imitation and hurts out-of-distribution performance[^140]. The two works appear to take opposite stances on off-policy traces, but the difference lies in how the traces are used: LUFFY uses teacher traces in the policy gradient after importance-sampling correction, whereas HHD's baselines use trajectories of mixed origin directly for SFT (our inference, based on [^24][^140]). In addition, LUFFY's teacher is stronger than the student while HHD's expert is weaker than the student, and the number of interaction turns also goes from a single turn to about a hundred (our inference, based on [^24][^140]). The LUFFY authors state that their experiments mainly target models of 7B and below and math questions with reference answers, using only one teacher trace per question[^140].

### 6.5 On-Policy Distillation

**Lineage and mechanism.** On-policy distillation (OPD) has the student receive token-level supervision from the teacher on prefixes it samples itself, so the training data are no longer a fixed set of traces (our assessment). Denote the student by $`\pi_\theta`$, the teacher by $`\pi_T`$, and the privileged context additionally visible to the teacher by $`c`$; the objective can then be written as follows (our assessment):

```math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{Q},\;y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t} D\big(\pi_T(\cdot\mid x,c,y_{<t})\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\big)\Big]
```

where $`D`$ can be the forward KL, the reverse KL or the JSD[^141]. $`c=\varnothing`$ corresponds to plain OPD, and when $`c`$ is the final answer or the reference solution, it is privileged-context OPD[^145]. GKD points out that conventional distillation of autoregressive models suffers from a distribution mismatch between the training sequences and the sequences the student generates at inference time, and it has the student receive teacher feedback on its self-generated sequences[^139]. G-OPD and Rethinking OPD both cite GKD as a precursor of OPD[^142][^144]. G-OPD proves that OPD is a special case of dense KL-constrained RL in which the reward term and the KL term are always weighted equally; with a reward scaling factor greater than 1 it is called ExOPD, and the factor is fixed at 1.25 in the experiments[^142]. W2S-OPD drops the premise that the teacher is at least as strong as the student: it takes the logit difference between two models smaller than the student as a capability direction and adds it to the student's own base model to form a proxy teacher[^38]. OPSD has one model serve as both teacher and student: the teacher sees the reference solution in its prompt, while the student sees only the question[^141].

**Evidence.** With T5-XL as the teacher and T5-small/base/large as students, the improvements of on-policy GKD on summarization, translation and arithmetic reasoning are 2.1, 1.7 and 1.9 times those of baseline KD, respectively[^139]. On GSM8K, using only the student's self-generated CoTs works better than a fixed CoT dataset or a mixture of the two[^139]. With Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 as the teacher (math average 59.7), the four-benchmark math averages of Qwen3-4B-Non-Thinking are: base model 15.4, SFT on teacher traces 35.1, OPD 42.6 and ExOPD 45.3[^142]. With Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode as the student and the RL and original versions of Qwen3-4B as the positive-negative pair, the math averages are SFT 45.7, OPD 46.5 and W2S-OPD 51.8, the last exceeding the 48.8 of the 4B-RL domain teacher[^38]. On the code average, OPD scores 58.7, below SFT's 59.7[^38]. W2S-OPD requires forward passes through three frozen models, yet its per-step training time is only 20% higher than OPD's (1043 seconds vs. 868 seconds)[^38]. OPCD applies OPD to context distillation; after Qwen3-8B internalizes the experience distilled from its own traces, its math accuracy is 75.0 for the base model, 77.6 in context, 78.5 with off-policy context distillation and 79.7 with OPCD[^150].

**Failure modes and fixes.** The paper Revisiting On-Policy Distillation identifies three kinds of failure in OPD computed on sampled tokens: imbalanced token-level supervision, unreliable teacher guidance once the student's prefix drifts, and mismatched tokenizers and special tokens[^143]. They switch to a truncated reverse KL over the teacher's top-K support set[^143]. With Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the student and OpenThinker3-7B as the teacher, the five-benchmark math average rises from the student's 28.2 to 36.4 with sampled-token OPD, versus 41.7 with local support matching and 56.0 for the teacher[^143]. Rethinking OPD (Li et al.) proposes two conditions for OPD to succeed: the teacher's and student's thinking patterns are compatible, and the teacher has new capabilities the student has not seen[^144]. Successful OPD shows up as progressive alignment on the states the student visits, with a shared token set concentrating 97%–99% of the probability mass[^144]. Reverse-distilling JustRL-1.5B toward its pre-RL checkpoint erases all of the RL gains, and switching to the larger R1-Distill-7B as the teacher brings the student back down to the same level[^144]. An SFT cold start on 200K teacher responses followed by OPD clearly outperforms pure OPD[^144]. The dense signal decays with depth: the accuracy advantage of the teacher continuing the student's prefix falls from +0.37 at a 1K prefix to +0.02 at a 16K prefix[^144]. The training-free diagnostic of Armandpour et al. shows that on BoolQ the mean cosine between the distillation gradient and the ideal gradient is only +0.027 (Qwen3-0.6B) and +0.026 (Qwen3-1.7B)[^149]. Alignment is significantly higher on incorrect rollouts, and the optimal teacher varies with student capacity[^149]. G-OPD finds that when the reward weight is raised to 1.5, the student exploits the implicit reward and becomes unstable[^142]. W2S-OPD observes that on IFBench plain OPD drops the student below its initialization, and the authors argue that the student absorbed the small expert's limitations[^38]. In OPCD a continuously updated self-teacher diverges, which the authors attribute to the high variance of using a constantly changing model as the teacher[^150]. On Sokoban, self-distillation scores 18.8, versus 53.9 for the frozen teacher-student configuration[^150]. OPCD's checkpoints are selected by highest test accuracy, so the reported values may be optimistic (our inference, based on [^150]).

**The contradiction in privileged self-distillation.** OPSD trains post-trained versions of Qwen3 (which the authors call the instruction-tuned versions) on at most 30K question-solution pairs from the math subset of OpenThoughts; the teacher is fixed as the initial policy, and thinking is disabled for the student but enabled for the teacher[^141]. The Avg@12 average of Qwen3-1.7B over AIME24, AIME25 and HMMT25 rises from 37.1 to 43.4, against 35.8 for SFT and 37.7 for GRPO[^141]. OPSD samples only 1 rollout of 1024 tokens per question and converges within 100 steps, whereas GRPO needs 8 rollouts of 16k tokens per question[^141]. Kaur et al. obtain the opposite result on thinking models: the avg@16 of all five Qwen3 and OLMo thinking models drops, with relative declines of up to 17%[^145]. On the Qwen3-1.7B thinking model, plain OPD with Qwen3-8B as the teacher rises from 0.372 to 0.392, but adding a gold demonstration for the teacher lowers it to 0.350[^145]. On this basis the authors argue that the problem lies in the privileged context on the teacher side, not in OPD itself[^145]. The losses concentrate at long budgets: at 32k–38k tokens the OPSD models match or fall below the base model, and their responses become markedly shorter[^145]. Kim et al. find on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B that GRPO raises AIME24 from 54.7 to 56.0, whereas SDPO conditioned on the full solution lowers it by about 40%[^146]. When generation is conditioned on the full solution, the score rises from 0.30 to 0.98, yet the average length falls from 13,054 tokens to 1,873 tokens and the number of epistemic markers from 182.5 to 8.8[^146]. Table 6-2 sets the conditions of the three studies side by side (our count, each row based on the references it cites).

The Qwen3-1.7B starting points of the two studies are almost identical (37.1 and 0.372), both evaluate with thinking enabled, and both use a generation cap of 38,912 tokens, so they are likely the same checkpoint (our inference, based on [^141][^145]). The divergence is most likely explained by whether the rollouts supervised during training are in thinking mode (our inference, based on [^141][^145][^146]). OPSD has the student generate rollouts with thinking disabled, while the teacher has thinking enabled[^141]. Kim et al. argue that this mixed configuration is closer to conventional teacher-student distillation[^146]. Kaur et al. also note that earlier evidence for self-distillation comes mostly from instruction-tuned models, short generation budgets or rollouts with thinking disabled[^145]. When Kaur et al. hold Qwen3-4B fixed, training on non-thinking rollouts maintains performance (0.591→0.590), whereas training on thinking rollouts lowers it to 0.532[^145]. Disabling thinking only preserved performance; it did not reproduce OPSD's gain on Qwen3-4B from 61.2 to 63.6 (our assessment, based on [^141][^145]). Thinking mode is not the only variable either: with thinking disabled, Qwen3-8B under SDPO still drops on AIME24 from 0.25 to 0.23[^146]. The divergence measure and checkpoint selection may be a second set of variables (our inference). In OPSD's divergence ablation forward KL works best, and the authors' subsequent experiments all use forward KL[^141]. In the same ablation, JSD reaches only 36.9 at step 50, against a baseline of 36.7[^141]. Kaur et al. and Kim et al. both use JSD[^145][^146]. When Kim et al. reproduce the mixed configuration, performance rises early on and then declines steadily[^146]. OPSD, by contrast, reports the best checkpoint from evaluations every 20 steps within 100 steps[^141]. The amount of privileged information and task coverage are two further moderating variables (our inference). The damage is milder when only the final answer is given as privileged context and more severe when the full solution is given[^145]. The chemistry data on which SDPO previously worked come from only six main problem types[^146]. In the Countdown training setting, OPSD also helps instruction models more consistently than thinking models[^145]. The four-benchmark average of Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 rises from 0.527 to 0.559, whereas that of Qwen3-4B-Think-2507 falls from 0.776 to 0.765[^145]. So far, OPSD's gains appear only in settings where the student has thinking disabled and training is short, and there is no positive evidence yet for supervising thinking rollouts (our assessment, based on [^141][^145][^146]).

**Data efficiency.** OPD needs very few questions (our assessment). Rethinking OPD II (Fu et al.), with R1-Distill-1.5B as the student and JustRL-1.5B as the teacher, trains on only one question; at step 300 its average over MATH-500, AIME25 and AMC23 is 68.5, against 69.8 for full-data OPD, recovering 87% of the full-data gain[^147]. A single question already reaches a state coverage of 71.5%, 16 semantically distinct questions reach 98.9% and match full-data training, and never-solved hard questions are just as effective[^147]. The authors argue that the input only needs to get the student's reasoning started, so the focus of data design shifts from collecting questions to choosing the teacher[^147]. Hou et al., studying the same teacher-student pair, draw 1,000 questions from DAPO-Math-17K as a candidate pool; on the six-benchmark average, the base model scores 42.4, the teacher 55.9, and 8 hard questions 53.6, close to the 53.7 of the full 17K questions, while 8 random questions score 52.1[^148]. Questions on which both teacher and student have zero training accuracy still improve validation accuracy[^148]. The authors attribute the benefit of hard questions to the longer CoTs they induce rather than to high token entropy[^148]. A2D goes further and trains a question generator with GRPO, with a reward computed from the disagreement of a frozen teacher-student pair and the teacher's probability mass on the student's top-K candidates, requiring no reference answers[^56]. On three students, Qwen3-1.7B, R1-Distill-1.5B and R1-Distill-7B, the six-benchmark math averages with synthesized questions are 46.2, 51.6 and 64.5, against 43.0, 50.5 and 62.9 for plain OPD on a real question bank[^56]. The authors also note that their token counts do not include the upstream cost of generating and scoring the questions[^56].

Whether hard questions carry a signal depends on who provides the signal (our inference). On questions that the student, and even the teacher, has never answered correctly, OPD can still improve validation accuracy[^147][^148]. This kind of signal comes from the teacher's distribution over the student's prefixes and does not depend on answering correctly (our inference). On-policy RL that relies on outcome rewards sees its reward collapse to zero on hard questions[^140], and rejection sampling likewise has no signal on questions that were never solved[^52]. The OPSD authors acknowledge that when a question is beyond the model's understanding, even a self-teacher that can see the reference solution cannot provide effective supervision[^141]. Conclusions about rollout length have to be read by range (our inference). Hou et al. observe that longer CoTs help within a generation cap of 7,168 tokens[^148]; Li et al. find that maximum lengths of 10K and 15K give flat or lower results, with late-stage collapse[^144]; and OPSD finds no consistent improvement of 4096 over 1024[^141].

### 6.6 Summary (our assessment)

- Offline teacher distillation is a cost-effective way for small models to acquire long CoT capability: on the same base model, distillation clearly outperforms pure RL, and following it with on-policy distillation can surpass RL with about one tenth of the GPU time, based on [^70][^80].
- A stronger teacher is not necessarily better: when candidate teachers differ widely the stronger one wins, though the advantage comes mostly from the amount of usable data, and when the teacher is too large, overtrained or incompatible with the student's patterns, the gains shrink or even reverse, based on [^22][^30][^144].
- Most of the self-generation and search methods in this section rely on filtering by reference answers; learning only from positive samples is simple and effective but compresses exploration, and search has a limited advantage on shallow tasks when compared at matched token counts, based on [^135][^138][^151].
- The effect of off-policy teacher traces depends on how they are used: used within RL groups with importance sampling they help, while naive SFT or midway takeover leads to imitation and memorization, based on [^24][^140].
- On-policy distillation turns the data from a set of traces into questions plus a teacher, and very few questions suffice to recover most of the gain; the risks are signal decay on long prefixes, teacher-student mismatch and a teacher that lacks new knowledge, based on [^144][^147][^148].
- Positive evidence for privileged self-distillation is limited to the mixed configuration in which the student has thinking disabled and to short training; when thinking rollouts are supervised, thinking models generally degrade on long-budget mathematical reasoning, based on [^141][^145][^146].
