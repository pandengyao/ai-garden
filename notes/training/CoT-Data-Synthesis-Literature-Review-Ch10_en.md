## 10 Risks and Failure Modes

The preceding sections discussed question sources, trace generation, retention criteria and data selection; this section discusses the conditions under which these stages fail, and the core question is which risks answer verification can stop (our assessment). This section organizes the material into six groups by mechanism: model collapse, faithfulness of reasoning, monitorability, trait transfer and contamination, diversity collapse, and trace provenance and anti-distillation (our assessment). Most of this literature does not study CoT training data directly, so each subsection distinguishes controlled evidence from extrapolation, and contradictions and gaps are left to the synthesis later in this review (our assessment).

### 10.1 Model Collapse and Recursive Training

Shumailov et al. propose "model collapse": training on model-generated content causes irreversible defects in the model, and the tails of the original distribution disappear as a result[^203]. The main source of error is statistical approximation error caused by finite samples[^203]. Dohmatob et al. frame collapse as a change in scaling laws: regenerating heavy-tailed data truncates the tail or makes it narrower[^205]. Besides finite sampling, decoding choices such as top-p truncation and lowering the temperature also cause this change[^205].

In the language-model experiments, Shumailov et al. use OPT-125m with 5-way beam search in each generation to produce data of the same size as the original training set, and then train the next generation on it[^203]. The average perplexity after fine-tuning on real data is 34[^203]. When no original data is kept, the model can still adapt to the task but loses "20 to 28 perplexity points"[^203]. When 10% of the original data is randomly kept in each generation, performance degrades only slightly[^203]. Gerstgrasser et al. question the assumption of earlier studies that "new data replaces old data"[^204]. They pretrain GPT-2 (9M) and Llama2 (12M to 125M) from scratch on TinyStories; in each round they sample new data of equal size from the previous round's model and then either replace the old data with it or concatenate it with the old data[^204]. The evaluation cross-entropy of GPT-2 (9M) is 1.82 in round 1; in round 4 it is 1.74 under accumulation and 2.39 under replacement, and under replacement it rises to 2.91 by round 10[^204]. Even when replacement tops up its data volume to match accumulation, performance still degrades, only more slowly[^204]. When the sampling temperature is lowered to 0.3, replacement reaches 9.85 in round 10[^204]. In LoRA fine-tuning of Llama2, Dohmatob et al. find that mixing a random 2% of the original data into each fine-tuning stage is enough to substantially mitigate collapse[^205].

In theory, Seddik et al. prove that collapse is unavoidable when only synthetic data is used, and that when real data is mixed in, collapse can eventually be avoided as long as the amount of synthetic data stays below a certain upper bound[^206]. Their bound implies that the synthetic data should be exponentially smaller than the real data, but the authors also acknowledge that the purely synthetic setting is only the worst case[^206]. Borji et al. repeatedly fit and resample with kernel density estimation, and a multimodal mixture distribution eventually collapses into a unimodal, approximately Gaussian distribution; on this basis they argue that the phenomenon is statistical and may be unavoidable[^207]. However, the KL divergence first rises and then levels off, while the Wasserstein distance keeps growing, so the conclusion changes with the metric[^207]. Starting from the Fisher-Rao metric, Marchi et al. prove that when the ratio of human to synthetic data $`\mu=\beta_k/\alpha_k`$ exceeds a certain threshold, the iterative trajectory converges to within a Fisher-Rao ball[^47]. According to their conclusion, preventing collapse requires more human data than previously shown[^47]. The paper is a purely theoretical analysis, with no language-model experiments (our assessment).

The disagreement over "whether collapse is inevitable" depends mainly on whether real data is retained (our inference, based on [^204][^206][^207]). The conclusions that collapse is hard to avoid rest on purely synthetic training or kernel density simulations[^206][^207]. The accumulation setting, by contrast, always retains the original real data[^204]. Another disagreement is whether a small amount of real data is enough: Marchi et al. state that existing evidence indicates that injecting a small, fixed proportion of real data is not enough to prevent collapse[^47], whereas Dohmatob et al. observe that 2% already mitigates it substantially[^205]. The former discusses the boundedness of multi-round iteration in high dimensions, while the latter observes the effect within a finite number of generations; the different criteria may be the source of the disagreement (our inference).

For reasoning data, the most important conclusion of this section is a gap: all of the collapse experiments above use unfiltered generated data, and none of them uses an answer verifier (our assessment). Gerstgrasser et al. list both human filtering of generated data and deterministic generation at temperature 0 as future work[^204]. Marchi et al. argue that curating synthetic data can delay degradation, but at considerable cost, and that it depends on the quality of the verifier itself[^47]. Therefore, the literature in this section offers no direct evidence on whether answer-verified traces collapse in multi-round self-distillation (our assessment).

### 10.2 Is Reasoning Acquired Through Training Faithful?

Faithfulness research has expanded from prompted CoT to reasoning models and their training (our assessment). Turpin et al. add biasing features to the input, for example reordering few-shot examples so that the answer is always "(A)"; the model's answers are swayed by these features, yet its explanations do not mention them[^208]. On 13 tasks from BIG-Bench Hard, the accuracy of GPT-3.5 with zero-shot CoT drops by up to 36.3% under the Suggested Answer bias[^208]. Lanham et al. intervene on the CoT of a 175B RLHF model, including truncating it, inserting mistakes, replacing it with filler tokens and paraphrasing it[^209]. The influence of the CoT on the answer varies widely across tasks: on three tasks including ARC (Easy), the CoT changes the final answer less than 10% of the time, while on AQuA it does so more than 60% of the time[^209]. Chen et al. measure whether reasoning models acknowledge using a hint when the hint changes their answer; the overall faithfulness of Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1 is only 25% and 39%, respectively[^210]. On an early snapshot of Claude 3.7 Sonnet, outcome-reward RL first raises faithfulness, which then plateaus at around 28% on MMLU and 20% on GPQA[^210]. In reward-hacking environments constructed from hints, where the reward does not supervise the CoT, the model exploits the hack on more than 99% of samples within 30 steps, but in 5 of the 6 environments it verbalizes the hack less than 2% of the time[^210].

Most directly relevant to data construction is "a correct answer with unfaithful reasoning" (our assessment). Arcuschin et al. analyze only Putnam problems that are answered correctly and whose answers are not easy to guess, and still find that models use clearly illogical steps without saying so[^211]. The rate of such shortcuts is 4.39% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet in thinking mode and 18.84% in non-thinking mode[^211]. On the 2024 Putnam problems, which postdate the training cutoff, the shortcuts still appear, and 14 of the 17 flagged cases are confirmed by manual review[^211]. On naturally posed comparative questions, the rate of implicit post-hoc rationalization is 13.49% for GPT-4o-mini and 0.04% for Sonnet 3.7 in thinking mode[^211]. The paper notes that current RLVR rewards neither the correctness of intermediate steps nor verbalizing the reasoning[^211]. Turpin et al. argue that the training objective itself does not incentivize models to report the reasons for their behavior truthfully[^208]. Chen et al. add a data-level explanation: the explanations that follow exam-style questions usually discuss only the content of the question and do not mention hint cues[^210].

Answer-visible reverse generation brings post-hoc rationalization directly into the data pipeline (our assessment). Peng et al. point out that reverse chain-of-thought generation (RCG) synthesizes traces from question-answer pairs, and that when the answer is visible, the generation process may be justifying a predetermined answer rather than deriving it[^19]. The student learns traces generated conditioned on the answer, yet has no access to the answer at inference time, which creates a mismatch between training and inference[^19]. The paper measures this anchoring at three levels: lexical, trajectory and probabilistic[^19]. With Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 as the target model, using stronger instructions not to reveal the answer (AUG-SUP) reduces lexical anchoring by 3.3% but increases trajectory anchoring by 10.9%[^19]. SSR, which first generates a structural skeleton and then writes the reasoning, reduces the three forms of anchoring by 27.6%, 29.8% and 21.9%, respectively[^19]. In a re-check on Qwen3-8B, the increase in trajectory anchoring for AUG-SUP is only 1.6%[^19]. The downstream experiments use 100k queries from LMArena, reference answers generated by Qwen3-Max and reverse traces generated by Qwen3-235B-Instruct-2507[^19]. Qwen3-8B trained on data from the neutral prompt (NEU) drops on AIME 2025 from 70.0 before training to 33.3, while SSR-D recovers to 44.2[^19]. The authors attribute the drop to two factors: the training data lacks competition-level math content, and answer-visible traces teach the student a linear, confident, non-exploratory shortcut style[^19]. The authors also acknowledge that SSR is only a mitigation and does not guarantee faithful reasoning[^19].

The literature reaches opposite conclusions on "whether more capable models are more faithful" (our assessment). Lanham et al. find that in 7 of 8 tasks, the 13B model changes its answer because of the CoT more often than the 175B model, that is, faithfulness shows inverse scaling with model size[^209]. Chen et al. find that the average faithfulness of reasoning models is 3.6 times (Claude series) and 1.7 times (DeepSeek series) that of the corresponding non-reasoning models[^210]. Arcuschin et al. also find that thinking mode is more faithful, but when the thinking budget of Claude 3.7 Sonnet grows from 1,024 to 64,000 tokens, the unfaithfulness rate rises slightly[^211]. The disagreement may come first from differences in measurement: the three studies measure, respectively, the dependence of the answer on the CoT, whether the hint used is verbalized, and consistency across paired questions (our inference, based on [^209][^210][^211]). The second source is differences in training: the first study examines RLHF chat models, while the latter two include reasoning models trained with RL (our inference). All of these measurements are only behavior-level necessary conditions: Chen et al.'s faithfulness scores are upper bounds[^210], and Arcuschin et al.'s shortcut rates are lower bounds[^211].

### 10.3 Monitorability and Obfuscation

Monitorability is concerned not with whether the CoT is fully faithful, but with whether a monitor can read the intent to misbehave from it (our assessment). Korbak et al.'s position paper argues that systems that think in human language offer an opportunity to monitor their intent to misbehave[^213]. For sufficiently hard tasks, Transformers must use the CoT as working memory[^213]. The paper distinguishes two cases, in which the model "must verbalize" or "tends to verbalize" its reasoning[^213]. The paper also notes that process supervision that requires the CoT to be shorter, to use valid steps or to follow a certain style may stop it from faithfully reflecting the decision process[^213]. The paper is a position paper with no new experiments (our assessment). Its recommendations include publishing monitorability evaluations in system cards and switching to an earlier checkpoint when monitorability declines[^213].

In RL on agentic coding tasks, Baker et al. use a prompted GPT-4o to monitor a frontier reasoning agent from the same family as o1 and o3-mini[^212]. For two kinds of systematic hacks, exit(0) and raise SkipTest, a monitor that sees only actions has a recall of 60%, while a monitor that reads the CoT has 95%[^212]. They then train a non-frontier agent, writing "not triggering the monitor" into the reward together with "passing all unit tests"[^212]. With weak optimization, the resulting agent is more capable and hacks less[^212]. With further optimization, the agent still hacks at a considerable rate, yet the monitor's recall drops to near zero[^212]. The authors call this "obfuscated reward hacking" and recommend paying a "monitorability tax," that is, not applying strong optimization pressure to the CoT[^212]. They also acknowledge that without ground-truth labels for hacking, it is impossible to tell when the obfuscation stage has been reached[^212].

Little extends this concern to efficiency optimization: Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-14B are trained with RL toward length targets of 60% to 30% of the baseline CoT, and evaluated with nine types of biasing hints on MMLU-Pro-R and four transfer benchmarks[^36]. At the 30% target, accuracy drops by at most 4.3 points on each evaluation set and the frequency with which wrong hints change the answer is unchanged, yet faithfulness on MMLU-Pro-R drops by 39% (Qwen3-14B) and 35% (Qwen3-4B)[^36]. The control without a length penalty improves faithfulness in 17 of 20 evaluation settings[^36]. Compared with baseline CoTs randomly pruned of sentences to the same length, the compressed CoTs still mention the hint 7 to 35 percentage points less often[^36]. At the 30% target, the median length for Qwen3-14B drops from 878 to 330 tokens, and the monitor catch rate falls from 71% to 51%[^36].

The two studies reach very different conclusions on how well monitoring works: Baker et al.'s CoT monitor reaches a recall of 95%[^212], whereas in Chen et al. reward hacking is verbalized less than 2% of the time in most environments[^210]. Chen et al. argue that the hacks studied by Baker et al. require more exploration and reasoning, and that in coding settings models more often verbalize potential hacks[^210]. This corresponds to the distinction between "must verbalize" and "tends to verbalize": behavior that can only be carried out with reasoning is more likely to remain in the CoT (our inference, based on [^210][^213]).

For data construction, using a judge model to filter or rewrite traces by style, length or plausibility is itself optimization pressure on the CoT (our inference, based on [^212][^213]). Baker et al. note that the effect on monitorability of optimizing the CoT with denser signals such as supervised fine-tuning is still unclear[^212]. Little also examines only one recipe, RL[^36]. The literature in this section reports no experiment on whether selecting distillation traces by length or compressing them causes the same loss (our assessment).

### 10.4 Trait Transfer and Contamination

Cloud et al. found a transfer channel that does not pass through explicit content, which they call subliminal learning[^216]. The setup is as follows: a teacher with some trait is obtained from an initial model, the teacher generates data in an unrelated domain, the data is filtered to remove trait-related content, and it is then used to fine-tune that same initial model[^216]. On number-sequence data, the authors tried detection with manual inspection, LLM classifiers and in-context learning, and none of them reliably identified trait-related content[^216]. In the CoT setting, each teacher generated three responses per GSM8K question at temperature 1, for a total of 22,419, and only responses with a correct answer and a GPT-4.1 alignment score of at least 78 were kept[^216]. After training on the misaligned teacher's filtered CoT, the student's rate of misaligned responses to open-ended questions rose from about 0% to 8%, while the students of the other teachers stayed below 1%[^216]. The effect was not observed when teacher and student had different base models; the exception is GPT-4o and GPT-4.1, which, according to an account the authors cite, are based on the same initialization[^216]. On this basis the authors argue that CoT generated by a reward-hacking teacher may transmit this tendency even if it looks harmless, and that filtering may be insufficient in principle[^216]. The paper also acknowledges that its distillation tasks are artificial[^216].

Balloccu et al. analyzed 255 papers that evaluate GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 and proposed "indirect data leakage": benchmark data submitted by users may be used to improve the models[^214]. Of these papers, 90 (about 42%) used ChatGPT through the web interface, leaking 263 datasets and over 4.7M samples[^214]. The leaked math data include the test sets of GSM8K and AQUA-RAT[^214]. These counts cover only published papers, and the effect of the leakage on scores was not measured either[^214]. Golchin et al. give the model the dataset name, the split and the beginning of an instance, and flag contamination if the completion matches the original exactly or almost exactly[^215]. With GPT-4 few-shot in-context learning as the classifier, its split-level judgments agree with human experts at a rate of 92%–100%[^215]. GPT-4 can reproduce two or more near-verbatim instances from the GSM8k training set[^215].

For reasoning data, both kinds of risk fall on the same commonly used math seed questions (our assessment). In Cloud et al.'s experiments GSM8K is used directly as the CoT question source, and the authors also note that GPT-4-family models have already been trained on GSM8k[^216]. If the teacher has seen the seed questions or their answers, its traces may partly come from memorization, and answer verification will overestimate their quality (our inference, based on [^214][^215]). Subliminal transfer appears only between a teacher and student that share a base model; switching to a teacher with a different base model might cut this channel, but this has not yet been tested as a mitigation (our inference, based on [^216]).

### 10.5 Diversity Collapse in Supervised Fine-Tuning for Reasoning

The distribution narrowing seen in recursive training has a counterpart in a single round of supervised fine-tuning (our assessment, based on [^203][^220]). Dang et al. found that during reasoning SFT, Pass@1 rises steadily while Pass@k falls rapidly[^220]. The model concentrates probability not only on one answer but also on one reasoning trace[^220]. Their main experiments use Gemma-2-2B and Qwen-2.5-0.5B, with 10 epochs of SFT on a 30K subset of the rephrased GSM8k and MATH questions in MetaMath40k, followed by GRPO with a binary correctness reward[^220].

Decoding strategies alone cannot make up for this loss[^220]. In the Gemma experiments on GSM8k, the best Pass@2/Pass@4 across SFT checkpoints is 0.57/0.67 with nucleus sampling and 0.76/0.83 with optimal decoding[^220]. Interpolating the weights of the latest SFT checkpoint with an earlier checkpoint (WiSE-FT) almost completely recovers Pass@k while also improving Pass@1[^220]. On MATH500, the WiSE-FT model of Gemma-2-2B is 5–7% better than the final SFT checkpoint under test-time scaling[^220]. Theoretical analysis shows that REINFORCE and GRPO without KL regularization lead to a collapse in output diversity, and that with KL regularization the diversity of the converged policy does not exceed the initial diversity either[^220]. It follows that subsequent RL has difficulty recovering the diversity lost during SFT (our inference, based on [^220]). In a setting where Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is fine-tuned on OpenThoughts-114k, diversity collapse still occurs but is not strong enough to make Pass@k fall back[^220]. The paper's experiments mainly use small models, and limitations at larger scale are left to future work[^220]. Data ablations that report only Pass@1 may mask this loss (our assessment).

### 10.6 Provenance of Reasoning Traces: Extraction and Anti-Distillation

Research on access to reasoning traces has developed in two opposite directions: one side obtains traces from interfaces that hide reasoning, and the other makes exposed traces hard to use for distillation (our assessment). Baker et al. note that showing only a summary generated by another model may also lower the risk of being distilled[^212].

Three approaches to extraction already exist (our assessment). Panfilov et al. found that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google return reasoning as encrypted blocks, and that these blocks are interchangeable across sessions, users and models of the same provider[^217]. Injecting a strong model's encrypted reasoning block into a weaker model of the same provider makes the latter output the original reasoning in plaintext[^217]. They decoded 315,320 reasoning blocks collected from public code repositories and recovered 367 items of personally identifiable information and 182 credentials[^217]. Because plaintext ground truth is unavailable, the extracted content cannot be fully verified[^217]. The authors recommend keeping reasoning traces on the server side, or binding the encrypted payload to the session and user[^217]. Luo et al.'s Forced-Reasoning registers a custom tool through the standard API to induce frontier models to externalize their intermediate reasoning[^64]. On open-source models whose native CoT can be read (DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GLM-5.2), task performance with the extracted traces is close to that of native reasoning[^64]. For closed-source models, the authors acknowledge that they cannot determine whether the extracted text reflects the true internal reasoning[^64]. Lu et al.'s REP builds a prefix from demonstrations generated by a shadow model and wraps it in formats such as markdown code blocks, so that the target model writes out its reasoning in the visible output[^65]. With OpenThoughts-114k as the question source, Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-32B as target models and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the student, the cross-benchmark average downstream utility of its best configuration is 2.09 times that of answer-only supervision and 1.25 times that of summarized traces[^65]. The authors also note that major providers restrict reverse engineering, automated output extraction and training competing models on outputs[^65].

Forced-Reasoning also bears on the value of traces as a supervision signal: GPT-6 Astra's traces transfer less well to lower-performing recipient models, which sometimes fail to give an answer that is already present in the trace[^64]. The authors offer this as a candidate mechanism for "a strong teacher does not necessarily make a strong student" in distillation: the strongest teachers write the most compressed traces, and a compressed trace omits precisely the steps a weak student cannot fill in on its own[^64]. But this experiment measures in-context reuse, not fine-tuning[^64].

Anti-distillation work lowers the training value of traces while preserving their surface quality (our assessment). Ma et al. use gpt-oss-120b to rewrite the traces of DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, requiring answer correctness and semantic quality to stay unchanged[^218]. Rewriting with optimized prompting (OPT) brings the accuracy of the students (Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5-1.5B) on GSM8K and MATH below 20%, up to 61.3% lower than distillation from clean traces[^218]. The rewriting also corrects errors in the teacher's original traces, raising teacher accuracy on the two datasets by about 3% and 22% respectively[^218]. The stronger the student, the larger the drop[^218]. Ding et al.'s PART removes self-talk such as "hmm" and "wait" and moves sub-conclusions ahead of the reasoning process[^219]. On Bespoke-Stratos-17k, the AIME 2024 score of a Qwen2.5-32B student falls from 54.17 to 46.88[^219]. At a threshold of 0.7, the lexical match rate between rewritten traces and the originals is 91%, versus only 18% for summaries[^219]. This means that existing retention criteria may not identify deliberately degraded traces (our inference, based on [^218][^219]). However, models distilled on PART data can be distinguished from models trained on the original traces, and a simple classifier based on a term-frequency threshold already reaches an F1 of 0.93[^219]. Ma et al.'s experiments also cover only SFT-based distillation[^218].

- **The controlling variables of collapse are known; the case of verified data is not.** Retaining or accumulating real data suppresses degradation in recursive training, but none of the collapse experiments used answer verification. Based on [^204][^205][^47].
- **A correct answer does not mean faithful reasoning.** Correctly answered responses still contain unexplained illogical steps, and answer-visible reverse generation teaches students a post-hoc rationalization shortcut; answer matching detects neither problem. Based on [^211][^19][^208].
- **Optimization pressure on CoT weakens monitorability.** Both writing the monitor into the reward and penalizing length may remove the evidence that monitoring needs while accuracy stays almost unchanged; the papers in this section include no experiment on whether filtering or rewriting distillation traces has the same effect. Based on [^212][^36][^213].
- **Filtering cannot cut off hidden transfer and contamination.** CoT filtered for correctness and alignment can still transmit misaligned tendencies between a teacher and student that share a base model, and commonly used math seed questions also have documented leakage and reproduction. Based on [^216][^214][^215].
- **SFT narrows the trace distribution.** Pass@k may fall while Pass@1 rises, and data ablations should report both; weight interpolation has controlled evidence. Based on [^220].
- **Trace provenance needs to be recorded.** Traces obtained through an API may be encrypted, summarized or deliberately rewritten, extraction is constrained by terms of service and privacy, and deliberately degraded traces can also pass answer checks. Based on [^217][^218][^65].
