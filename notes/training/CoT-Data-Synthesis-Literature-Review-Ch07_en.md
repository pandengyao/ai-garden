## 7 Verification and Labeling: What Counts as an Acceptable Trace

Sections 4 and 5 discuss where questions come from and who generates the traces; this section discusses which criteria decide whether a trace is retained, and where step-level labels come from. There are three core questions: which incorrect traces outcome checking lets through, how noisy and costly the various label sources are, and why the same set of verifiers produces opposite rankings at different evaluation scales. The 19 papers in this section focus mainly on mathematical reasoning, with some coverage of code, multidisciplinary question answering and scientific computing (our count). The selection and mixing ratios in Section 8 presuppose the criteria of this section.

### 7.1 Outcome Checking: Answer Matching, Test Execution and Their Errors

Outcome checking is the cheapest and most widely used retention criterion (our assessment). Self-improvement methods of the STaR kind use test cases or reference answers to give each generated solution a binary correctness label[^69]. Math-Verify, used by several recipes in Sections 4, 5 and 7, is also a rule-based tool of this kind (our assessment).

Checks of this kind err in two directions[^152]. GenRM points out that answer checkers based on string matching or SymPy inevitably miss some correct solutions, that is, false negatives[^152]. A solution may also reach the correct answer through incorrect reasoning, producing a false positive[^152]. In The Lessons of Developing Process Reward Models in Mathematical Reasoning (hereafter Lessons), Zhang et al. use Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct to sample 8 responses per question on GSM8K, MATH, OlympiadBench and Omni-MATH, and manually annotate randomly sampled responses whose answers are correct[^153]. A considerable proportion of responses have a correct answer but a flawed process, and the harder the question, the more pronounced this is[^153]. DIVERSE likewise notes that bootstrapped exemplars may contain invalid reasoning yet happen to reach the correct answer, a problem that is more severe on StrategyQA, which has only two options[^155]. CRAFT finds that giving the correct answer in the prompt does not improve reasoning: in 11 of 12 comparisons of four models on step-wise error detection in PRMBench there is no significant difference[^23]. On GSM8K, giving the answer actually lowers the ROSCOE quality of the traces of all four models[^23]. Generating explanations conditioned on the reference answer is no substitute for checking the process (our assessment, based on [^23][^153]).

The coverage of rule-based checking is also limited: among real exam questions, only 60.3% of math questions have a single numerical answer that rules can check, and only 45.4% of complex multidisciplinary questions do[^161].

For code, outcome checking means executing tests, and the difficulty lies in where the tests come from[^158]. AceCoder has GPT-4o-mini rewrite 124K Python seeds into LeetCode-style questions and "imagine" about 20 tests for each question[^158]. These tests contain severe hallucinations, so the authors filter them by executing solutions from Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct, obtaining 87.1K questions and 1.38M tests[^158]. In a manual spot check of 200 tests after filtering, only 3 were invalid[^158]. In a controlled ablation, training the reward model on the filtered tests raises the Best-of-16 average score by 2.5 (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and 1.0 (Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct) compared with no filtering[^158]. The authors also point out that reference solutions may be wrong, tests may be too simple, and passing all tests does not mean a program is correct[^158]. "Tests pass, program wrong" and "answer right, process wrong" belong to the same category: a checker checks only the behavior it was designed to check (our assessment, based on [^152][^158]).

### 7.2 Process Supervision and PRM Data: Where the Labels Come From

Let's Verify Step by Step holds that process supervision has no simple automated solution, so it has annotators label each step of the model's solutions as positive, negative or neutral, labeling only up to the first incorrect step[^74]. The annotators can see the reference answer[^74]. PRM800K contains 800K step labels on 75K solutions to 12K questions[^74]. During annotation, the solutions most likely to fool the current best reward model are shown first[^74]. In synthetic experiments in which a large PRM replaces human annotators, this active learning is about 2.6 times as data-efficient as uniform labeling[^74]. In the large-scale setting with a GPT-4 base model, the Best-of-1860 solve rates on a representative test subset of MATH are 78.2% for the PRM, 72.4% for the ORM and 69.6% for majority voting[^74].

Among the works in this section, the earliest automatic step labeling comes from DIVERSE (our count, based on [^155]). It marks paths whose final answer is correct as positive examples; if a step appears in none of the positive paths (with equivalence judged by roberta-large-mnli), that step and every step after it are marked as negative[^155]. Math-Shepherd and the concurrent, independent MiPS instead estimate step labels with Monte Carlo (MC) continuations[^85][^163]. Math-Shepherd defines the quality of a step as its potential to lead to the correct answer, estimated by having a completer model continue N reasoning paths from that step, and it distinguishes hard and soft labels[^85] as follows:

```math
y^{\mathrm{HE}}_{s_i}=\mathbb{1}\left[\exists j:\ a_j=a^{*}\right],\qquad y^{\mathrm{SE}}_{s_i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}\left[a_j=a^{*}\right]
```

Here $`a_j`$ is the answer of the $`j`$-th continuation and $`a^{*}`$ is the reference answer[^85].

Math-Shepherd manually checked 160 steps from the GSM8K training set: with LLaMA2-70B trained on MetaMATH as the completer and N=4, hard estimation reaches an accuracy of 86%[^85]. As N increases further, accuracy actually decreases[^85]. In a comparison in the same section, the labeling accuracy of completion with LLaMA2-13B (N=4) is 85.0, while that of DIVERSE's NLI-based and rule-based labeling ranges from 61.3 to 75.6[^85]. MiPS uses the fraction of correct continuations as a soft label, with the same model used for generation and continuation[^163]. Its authors argue that an imperfect reasoning model underestimates the correctness of intermediate steps, so a verifier should focus on high scores, contrary to experience on human-annotated data[^163]. Predictions are of the worst quality for early steps[^163]. Taking PaLM 2-S on MBPP as an example, the process verifier scores 54.2, below the outcome verifier's 56.8, and 57.8 when only the last 3 steps are considered[^163].

OmegaPRM describes its idea as the same as that of MiPS and Math-Shepherd; in addition, it collects process data with MCTS and uses binary search to locate the first error, reducing the complexity from O(kM) to O(k log M)[^154]. With the same compute, brute-force MC produces 200K data points, while OmegaPRM produces 15M[^154]. The authors acknowledge that the labels are noisy: when a question is too hard, even a correct prefix cannot be continued to the correct answer, producing a false negative[^154]. When a question is too easy, even an incorrect prefix can be continued to the correct answer, producing a false positive[^154]. The method also depends on questions paired with reference answers, which makes it hard to apply to open-ended tasks[^154].

PAV points out that PRMs of the Math-Shepherd and OmegaPRM kind learn the probability of future success, approximating the value function in RL[^156]. Setlur et al. argue that a process reward should measure the progress a step makes, that is, its step-level advantage, computed under a prover policy distinct from the base policy[^156]. Earlier automatically labeled PRMs, when used as dense rewards in RL, beat ORMs by only 1-2%[^156]. An overly strong prover succeeds from any step, so the advantages of all steps approach 0 and good and bad steps cannot be told apart[^156]. On MATH, with Gemma-2B SFT as the base policy, the Bo4 prover comes out ahead for all N[^156]. For a 27B base policy, a 9B prover is actually better than a 27B one[^156]. Compared with ORMs, test-time search guided by PAV is more than 8% more accurate, and its compute-efficiency multiple is 1.5 to 5 in the abstract and 5 to 10 in its Section 4.1[^156]. In online RL, PAV improves the RFT policies of Gemma 2B and 9B by 11% and 15%, respectively, more than 7% above ORM-RL[^156].

Implicit PRM goes further and removes step labels altogether[^157]. It parameterizes the outcome reward as the log-likelihood ratio between the policy model and the reference model, and computing the same ratio on partial responses yields the process reward[^157] as follows:

```math
r_\theta(\mathbf{y})=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(\mathbf{y})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{y})},\qquad q_\theta^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf{y}_{<i})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i\mid\mathbf{y}_{<i})},\qquad r_\theta^{t}=q_\theta^{t}-q_\theta^{t-1}
```

The authors estimate that collecting step labels with look-ahead sampling in the Math-Shepherd manner requires 38.8 times the FLOPs of training an ORM[^157]. The training data are 33K math questions from UltraInteract, with 8 responses per question sampled by Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and labeled only by final answer[^157]. Averaged over Best-of-N with three generators and N of 4, 16 and 64, the DPO version of the implicit PRM scores 50.4, while Math-Shepherd reimplemented on the same data scores 47.8[^157]. Adding step labels for step-level KTO brings no gain, but the authors caution that MCTS-style step labels are themselves noisy[^157].

Formal verification provides another source of labels[^3]. FoVer uses Z3 and Isabelle to assign error labels to every step in formal logic and theorem-proving tasks, with no human annotation and no extra model calls[^3]. Math word problems are recast as theorem proving: the conditions serve as premises, the candidate answer serves as the hypothesis, and the model generates the proof[^3]. FoVer-40K contains 40K steps generated by Llama 3.1 8B and Qwen 2.5 7B, with the proportion of erroneous steps at 50% in every subset[^3]. Measured by AUROC on ProcessBench, FoVer training raises Llama 3.1 8B from 67.4 to 77.3 and Qwen 2.5 7B from 75.9 to 85.8, compared with the same backbones without fine-tuning[^3]. The authors also point out that formal labels may make the PRM overly strict, giving low scores to intermediate steps that are acceptable but not fully precise[^3]. To simplify the verification pipeline, the math portion uses only questions of GSM8K difficulty[^3].

Lessons gives the most direct controlled comparison of label sources[^153]. The authors use Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as a judge to relabel, step by step, the same 860k question-answer samples used for MC estimation[^153]. In average F1 on ProcessBench, MC labels score 40.1, judge labels 46.5, and the human labels of PRM800K, with only 264k samples, 56.5[^153]. The authors argue that the completer model can reach correct answers from incorrect steps and incorrect answers from correct steps, so MC labels in effect bring the principles of a value model into PRM training[^153]. Keeping only the samples on which MC and the judge agree on the error location leaves about 40% of the data, with ProcessBench performance comparable to that of judge labels[^153]. After filtering, hard labels clearly outperform soft labels, and a threshold of 0 (a step is judged correct if any continuation is correct) works best[^153]. Following this recipe, 1.5M samples were filtered from 3M MC samples, and the resulting Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B and 72B reach average F1 scores of 73.5 and 78.3 on ProcessBench[^153].

Step-DPO uses first-error localization to construct preference data[^159]. The authors argue that rejecting an incorrect response as a whole also discards the correct steps before the first error, which is itself a form of label noise[^159]. It has GPT-4 or a human locate the first incorrect step, then uses the correct steps before it as a prefix for the model to continue on its own, taking the first step of a continuation that reaches the correct answer as the positive example[^159]. In a controlled ablation with 5K pairs, Qwen2-72B-SFT (MATH 61.7) reaches 64.1 with Step-DPO and 62.5 with plain DPO[^159].

### 7.3 Generative Verifiers and LLM Judges

V-STaR uses the correct and incorrect solutions produced during self-improvement to train, with DPO, a verifier that judges whether a solution is correct[^69]. On LLaMA2 and CodeLLaMA, it outperforms existing self-improvement and verification methods by 6% to 17% on math reasoning (GSM8K, a MATH subset) and by 4% to 12% on code generation (MBPP, HumanEval)[^69]. GenRM takes a DPO verifier trained in the V-STaR manner as one of its baselines and instead trains the verifier with next-token prediction[^152]. The verifier answers "Is the answer correct?", takes the probability of the "Yes" token as the score, and is trained jointly with solution generation[^152]. Its CoT version requires verification rationales; for GSM8K, the rationales are written by Gemini 1.0 Pro under the guidance of a reference solution, which can be any model solution with a correct answer[^152]. The rationales for a solution are kept only when more than 50% of them agree with the verdict of the answer checker[^152].

With the generator fixed and only the verifier changed, Best-of-N with the Gemma2-9B GenRM-CoT over Gemini 1.0 Pro's GSM8K solutions raises the share of problems solved from 73% to 93.4%[^152]. Such gains depend on the starting point: on MMLU abstract algebra, the rise from 37.9% to 53.5% is counted from the base model's pass@1, whereas relative to a discriminative reward model it is only from 50% to 53.5%[^152]. Reference guidance is critical to rationale quality: the Gemma-7B verifier scores 91.7% on GSM8K with guidance and 87.8% without it[^152]. GenRM without CoT is only on par with or slightly better than discriminative verifiers[^152]. Rationales filtered only by the final "Yes/No" are still often of very poor quality, because random guessing also achieves 50% accuracy[^152].

ThinkPRM extends generative verification to long-chain process verification and points out that GenRM performs only outcome verification based on short CoT[^160]. It has QwQ-32B-Preview write step-by-step verification chains for solution prefixes from PRM800K and keeps only chains that are correctly formatted, whose step-by-step judgments agree with the PRM800K human labels, and whose length stays within the budget[^160]. About 20% of the samples meet the first two conditions, which finally yields 1K chains and 8K step labels[^160]. On subsets of GPQA-Diamond and LiveCodeBench, it outperforms a discriminative PRM trained on all 712K labels by 8% and 4.5%, respectively[^160]. In a controlled ablation, 65K chains filtered by process outperform 128K chains filtered by outcome[^160]. When an off-the-shelf reasoning model serves as the judge, slight changes in the instruction wording can change F1 by up to 3 to 4 points[^160]. Generative PRMs also suffer from overconfidence, with scores clustering near 0 or 1, and from label interference, a tendency to also judge the subsequent steps wrong once one step is judged wrong[^160]. Its out-of-domain evaluation uses only 86 physics questions from GPQA-Diamond and 200 questions from LiveCodeBench[^160].

Judges conditioned on reference answers can extend verifiable rewards to free-form answers[^161]. Su et al. find that, given expert reference answers, the binary judgments of Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct and GPT-4o agree almost completely, with Cohen's κ above 0.86 on math questions and above 0.88 on multi-subject college-level questions[^161]. On this basis, the authors argue that reference-based judgment is usually easier than reference-free verification, which is as hard as finding the first error in a response[^161]. They distill 160k binary judgments given by the 72B judge during the RL exploration phase into Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct[^161]. When Qwen2.5-7B is used as the base model and trained with RLOO plus binary rewards, the math average is 63.0 (distilled 7B verifier), 61.6 (72B judge) and 58.5 (rule-based reward)[^161]. This verifier outputs only 0 or 1 and writes no CoT; it is a form of outcome verification[^161].

VersaPRM uses an LLM judge for multi-domain step annotation[^162]. It draws 5,750 questions from MMLU-Pro and has Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct generate 16 CoTs per question[^162]. The annotator, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, is given definitions of step types and the reference answer in its prompt and identifies the earliest erroneous step in each CoT[^162]. In a human audit of 64 questions, the agreement rate is 80% for CoTs judged correct and 71% for those judged wrong, and the authors estimate that about 75% of CoTs are labeled correctly, close to the estimate of about 80% for PRM800K[^162]. After the step definitions and the reference answer are removed, the agreement rate for the "correct" class drops to 7%, and a PRM trained on the resulting labels improves over majority voting in Best-of-N by less than half as much as the full version[^162]. This ablation removes the step definitions and the reference answer at the same time, so their individual contributions cannot be separated (our assessment, based on [^162]). Math PRMs bring almost no benefit outside math: on the categories of MMLU-Pro other than math, the best math PRM is only 0.71 above majority voting[^162]. In the law category, VersaPRM's weighted majority voting is 7.9% above majority voting[^162]. The authors also raise the question of whether, since both the generator and the annotator are Llama models, VersaPRM is biased toward Llama-family generators[^162].

Reference-free verification is another route[^27]. Self-Verified Distillation takes only the questions from OpenThoughts and has Qwen3 models generate solutions and verify them themselves[^27]. A solution enters the training set only if it passes three levels of checks in order, for cycle consistency, factuality and correctness, and the judge votes are unanimous[^27]. In the math ablation on Qwen3-4B (8 candidates per question, verification strength 5), simple verification that only asks "Is it correct?" raises the average over AIME26 and HMMT by 4.9 points, and three-level verification raises it by 8.4 points[^27]. On code, training on unfiltered self-generated data does worse than the initial model on both LCBv5 and LCBv6[^27]. The authors also point out that self-verification may accept wrong solutions and reject useful ones; the weakest model, Qwen3-0.6B, drops from 12.3 to 11.4 on HLE[^27].

CRAFT replaces the judge with cross-trace consensus: it samples K traces per question and keeps the steps shared by multiple traces[^23]. The rationale is that erroneous steps tend to differ from sample to sample, whereas correct steps recur across independent samples[^23]. In eight settings with GPT-5.4-nano and Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite, using only one trace (K=1) lowers accuracy in seven of them, by up to 6.1%[^23]. The authors also acknowledge that on hard problems where many candidates share the same faulty reasoning (for example, a common post-hoc rationalization), consensus may amplify errors[^23]. The consensus filtering in Lessons rests on the same premise, namely that the errors of the two label sources are independent of each other (our inference, based on [^23][^153]).

### 7.4 Verification of Scientific-Computing and Neuro-Symbolic Traces

Residual errors in scientific-computing traces take a particular form, for example substituting the wrong variable into a correct physics formula and computing a quantity that is mathematically sound but irrelevant to the question[^42]. If model-generated code is syntactically valid but logically wrong, the Python interpreter executes it as usual[^42]. NS-PRM therefore splits step correctness into symbolic validity and semantic grounding: the former requires the step to be executable with consistent units and types, and the latter requires applying the correct principle to the correct variables[^42]. The former is guaranteed by hard filtering with a domain-specific language containing more than 120 mathematical primitives and a deterministic symbolic verifier, and the latter is assessed by a PRM trained only on steps that the verifier accepts[^42].

The contribution on the data side lies in the construction of negatives: in counterfactual symbolic perturbation (CSP), GPT-5.2 first proposes a semantic perturbation, such as replacing the initial velocity with a final velocity in the same units, and a deterministic algebraic solver then recomputes the values, so that each negative is guaranteed to pass the verifier while being logically wrong[^42]. For positives, GPT-5.2 judges step correctness[^42]. The training set uses PRM800K questions as seeds and contains 140,000 steps each of positives and negatives[^42].

On ProcessBench, the full NS-PRM has an average F1 of 74.2, and 68.8 without the symbolic verifier, while the Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B score that its table quotes from other work is 73.5[^42]. On 200 MATH problems, with the same search budget and a Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct backbone, ToRA-style Python execution reaches 82.0% and 67.0% accuracy on short and long chains, and long chains have 21.0% silent failures, that is, faulty logic that executes smoothly without raising an error[^42]. NS-PRM reaches 88.0% and 78.0%, with 5.0% silent failures on long chains[^42].

This set of evidence needs to be discounted (our assessment). The baseline numbers on ProcessBench are quoted from other papers[^42]. In the CSP ablation, joint perturbation has an F1 of 75.4, higher than the 74.2 of the full system in the main table, which the paper does not explain (our assessment, based on [^42]). The text states that OlympiadBench rises from 62.1 to 69.8, which does not match the 62.6 and 68.9 in the table (our assessment, based on [^42]). The authors acknowledge that an approach relying on a structured format may struggle with the implicit assumptions in real engineering problems[^42]. NS-PRM also points out that the concurrent FoVer cannot judge semantic problems such as mismatched physical variables[^42]. Deterministic tools can guarantee that steps are executable and provable, but not that the variables and principles are used correctly; the latter still requires a learned discriminator or an LLM judge (our assessment, based on [^3][^42]).

**Contradiction one: whether MC labels or human labels are better.** In OmegaPRM, with solutions generated by Gemini Pro and PRM-weighted majority voting over 64 samples on MATH500, the PRM trained on MC labels scores 69.4, the one trained on PRM800K 67.6, and majority voting 67.2[^154]. Math-Shepherd also reports that, when selecting the best of 256 candidates on MATH, its automatically annotated data outperform PRM800K[^85]. When ThinkPRM filters verification chains with Math-Shepherd's MC labels instead of human labels, the F1 on OlympiadBench and OmniMath is 75.8 and 76.5, versus 76.3 and 75.7 with human labels[^160]. On the other side is the controlled comparison in Lessons: on ProcessBench the three kinds of labels rank in the order human, judge, MC, and on Best-of-8 the ranking is exactly reversed (Table 7-2)[^153].

The most likely explanatory variable is the evaluation yardstick (our inference). Lessons points out that policy models often give responses with the right answer but a wrong process, while Best-of-N looks only at the answer, so the evaluation criterion is misaligned with the goal of process verification[^153]. The two-stage EurusPRM models, Math-Shepherd-PRM-7B and Skywork-PRM-7B place more than 40% of their lowest step scores on the last step, which usually contains the final answer, and the authors argue that PRMs optimized only for Best-of-N actually behave more like ORMs[^153]. The comparisons in OmegaPRM and Math-Shepherd both use answer-level yardsticks (our assessment, based on [^85][^154]). In ThinkPRM, the MC labels only decide which verification chains are kept and do not directly serve as training targets, so they are more tolerant of noise (our inference, based on [^160]). Math-Shepherd itself attributes its advantage to distribution differences and data volume: PRM800K annotates the outputs of GPT-4, which differ from the outputs of its open-source models[^85].

**Contradiction two: soft labels or hard labels.** OmegaPRM finds pointwise soft labels to be the best, with a per-step accuracy of 70.1%[^154]. In a replication on GSM8K with the same models as Math-Shepherd, MiPS finds soft labels with max aggregation better than hard labels with min aggregation, 54.7 versus 52.4 on Llemma[^163]. Math-Shepherd finds no substantive difference between the verifiers trained with the two kinds of labels[^85]. Lessons, by contrast, finds hard labels clearly better after consensus filtering, with the difference before filtering masked by noise[^153]. There are two possible explanatory variables: first, whether noisy labels were filtered out before training, and second, whether the aggregation method matches the evaluation yardstick (our inference). MiPS's conclusion is tied to max aggregation, whereas Lessons scores with the product of step scores and evaluates on ProcessBench[^153][^163].

**Contradiction three: whether step labels are needed.** The implicit PRM uses only outcome labels, outperforms a reimplementation of Math-Shepherd on the same data, and gains nothing from adding step labels[^157]. EurusPRM-Stage1 and Stage2, evaluated in Lessons, were trained with the implicit PRM method; their Best-of-8 averages are 61.6 and 62.0, below the 66.2 of maj@8, and their ProcessBench average F1 is only 31.2 and 31.3[^153]. In ThinkPRM, process filtering also outperforms outcome filtering[^160]. The most likely variables are baseline strength and the evaluation yardstick (our inference). The main table of the implicit PRM paper compares the Best-of-N of each PRM, with the three generators reaching accuracies of 9.6%, 44.6% and 63.2% at temperature 0.5[^157]. Its KTO and CE variants, used alone, do not exceed majority voting[^157]. Lessons, by contrast, uses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct as the policy model and maj@8 as the baseline, and adds a step-localization evaluation[^153]. EurusPRM is not the model from the original implicit PRM paper, so this comparison is only indirect evidence (our assessment, based on [^153][^157]).

**Contradiction four: rule-based rewards or learned rewards.** AceCoder runs RL starting from Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Base; with passing all tests as the reward, the average score rises from 44.4 to 52.3, whereas with a learned reward model it only reaches 49.1, which the authors attribute to reward hacking[^158]. On free-form math questions, RLOO training with the distilled 7B generative verifier yields 63.0, versus 58.5 with the rule-based reward[^161]. As the training data grow from 20k to 100k, the math average with the rule-based reward falls from 58.2 to 51.7, while with the distilled verifier it rises from 63.4 to 65.0[^161]. What decides the direction is how verifiable the answers are: executable tests make rule-based rewards nearly exact, whereas the share of free-form answers that rules can check is limited (our inference, based on [^158][^161]).

**Contradiction five: whether stronger annotators are always better.** MiPS expects a stronger continuation model to reduce MC noise[^163]. When used as an RL reward, an off-the-shelf 72B judge also outperforms a 7B judge, 62.7% versus 58.8% on math[^161]. But PAV finds that an overly strong prover cannot distinguish good steps from bad ones, and that a prover of intermediate strength is needed[^156]. Math-Shepherd's annotation accuracy declines as the number of continuations N increases, which the authors attribute to false positives[^85]. Judging whether a single step is right benefits from capability, but a contrastive step signal requires a continuation model whose success rate is not saturated, and both an overly large N and an overly strong prover saturate the success rate (our inference, based on [^85][^156]).

The strength of the generator likewise changes the value of a verifier (our assessment). With greedy decoding as the control, Best-of-N with AceCodeRM-7B improves Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct by 8.4 on average across four code benchmarks, but improves Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct by only 2.6[^158]. The only case in which DIVERSE's step-aware labels lower accuracy is code-davinci-002 on GSM8K, from 82.3 to 81.5, and the authors speculate that the quality of this model's reasoning paths is already high[^155]. The stronger the generator, the fewer distinguishable errors there are among the candidates, and the marginal value of a verifier declines accordingly (our inference, based on [^155][^158]).

### 7.6 Summary (our assessment)

- Outcome checking lets through traces with "right answer, wrong process" and covers only questions that rules can check; the counterpart problem in code is "tests pass, program wrong", based on [^153][^158][^161].
- In controlled comparisons, step-label quality ranks in the order human, LLM judge, MC continuation; consensus filtering reaches the judge's level with about 40% of the data; and labels from formal and symbolic tools are reliable but narrow in coverage and err on the strict side, based on [^3][^42][^153].
- The quality of automatic labels comes largely from reference answers, reference programs or human labels, but giving the answer alone does not improve step-by-step error identification, and the separate roles of reference information and annotation guidelines still lack controlled experiments, based on [^23][^152][^162].
- The evaluation yardstick drives the conclusions: Best-of-N favors ORM-like scoring, and only step-localization evaluation exposes label noise, so the yardstick and baseline must be aligned before comparing conclusions about PRMs, based on [^153][^154][^157].
- Stronger annotators are not always better: a contrastive step signal requires a continuation model whose success rate is not saturated, and the gains from a verifier also shrink as the generator gets stronger, based on [^85][^156][^158].
- Reference-free self-verification and cross-trace consensus suit open-ended tasks; the main risk is that systematic errors are unanimously accepted, and current evidence covers only a few models and benchmarks, based on [^23][^27].
