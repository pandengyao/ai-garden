## 8 Selection, Scale and Length

There are usually more verified candidate traces than training needs, and the same question often has multiple correct traces (our assessment). This section discusses the decisions that follow verification: how many to keep, which signal to keep them by, how to allocate the number of questions and the number of samples per question, how long traces should be, and how these decisions change with the student (our assessment). For retention criteria, see the chapter on verification; for the filtering of agentic trajectories, see the chapter on agentic trajectories (our assessment). The question running through this section is how much of conclusions such as "small but refined beats the full set" comes from the selection signal itself, and how much from uncontrolled training budget, trace length and student size (our assessment).

### 8.1 Small Curated Sets and Scaling Up

"Small but refined" has two origins (our assessment). The first is influence estimation in instruction tuning: on about 270K mixed instruction examples, LESS selects data by the similarity between low-rank gradient features and few-shot examples of the target task[^164], and reports that a 5% subset often outperforms training on the full set[^164]. The second is the curation of long CoT in early 2025 (our assessment). LIMO fine-tunes Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct with only 800 samples[^72] and proposes the hypothesis that, when domain knowledge is already thoroughly encoded in pretraining, a small number of deliberately designed demonstrations suffices to elicit complex reasoning[^72]. s1 collects 59,029 questions from 16 sources[^86] and then filters them down to 1,000 by three criteria, namely quality, difficulty and diversity[^86]. Later work turned curation into rankers or curricula: Select2Reason ranks by combining a difficulty reward model with trace length[^173], Light-R1 shrinks the question set stage by stage by pass rate[^174], and Phi-4-reasoning specifically selects "teachable" questions at the edge of Phi-4's capability[^175].

LIMO reaches 63.3% on AIME24 and 95.6% on MATH500, compared with 6.5% and 59.2%, respectively, for earlier fine-tuned models[^72]. Its sample-efficiency experiment ranks 2,125 candidate questions by a reasoning-chain quality score and trains on the top-ranked subsets[^72]. The first 400 samples alone raise AIME24 from 16.5 to 57.5[^72]; beyond 800 samples the returns diminish, and going from LIMO-800 to LIMO-1k2 adds only 0.9 on AIME24[^72]. In s1, with Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct as the base model and in an ablation with a thinking cap of about 30,000 tokens, three 1K subsets chosen at random, by diversity only, and by taking only the longest traces score 36.7, 26.7 and 33.3 on AIME24, respectively, while s1K scores 50.0[^86]. Training on all 59K samples gives 53.3, and the 95% bootstrap confidence interval of its difference from s1K is -13.3% to 20.0%[^86], yet the training cost is 394 versus 7 H100 GPU hours[^86].

LESS's conclusion varies with the base model[^164]. On Mistral-7B, LESS's 5% subset scores 61.8, 60.3 and 56.0 on MMLU, TydiQA and BBH, versus 60.4, 57.7 and 53.0 for the full set[^164]. On Llama-2-7B, it exceeds the full set only on TydiQA (56.2 versus 54.0) and falls below it on MMLU and BBH (50.2 versus 51.6, 41.5 versus 43.2)[^164]. Select2Reason uses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct as the student and selects 10% of the about 196k correct samples in OpenR1-Math-220k[^173]. This subset has a Pass@1 of 0.914 on MATH, higher than the 0.894 of the full set, and 0.433 on AIME24, lower than the 0.465 of the full set[^173].

Light-R1 first filters about 76k questions by the pass rate of DeepScaleR-1.5B-Preview and passes them to DeepSeek-R1 for generation, and then keeps about 3k hard questions on which DeepSeek-R1's samples are neither all correct nor all wrong[^174]. Through two SFT stages and DPO, the AIME24 score of Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct rises from 16.6 to 69.0, 73.0 and 75.8 in turn, and reaches 76.6 after model merging[^174]. The same 3k questions also raise the 32B DeepSeek-R1-Distill model from 72.6 to 78.1 on AIME24[^174]. Phi-4-reasoning generates demonstrations with o3-mini, and its SFT data exceed 1.4M examples and 8.3B tokens[^175]. Its 14B model scores 63.1 on AIME 25 and 78.0 after a further short phase of outcome-reward RL, while DeepSeek-R1-Distill-70B scores 51.5[^175]. The report also cautions that two AIME evaluations, each averaged over 5 runs, can differ by as much as 5 to 10 percentage points[^175].

Prompt selection for reinforcement learning has pushed "small" to the extreme (our assessment). With RLVR on just one example, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B rises from 36.0% to 73.6% on MATH500, matching the 73.6% obtained with the 1,209 examples of DSR-sub[^172]. But in the comparison table reported at each run's best checkpoint, a baseline that rewards only format already reaches 65.6 on MATH500, versus 75.2 for DSR-sub[^172]. The authors also note that 1-shot RLVR does not save RL compute[^172].

The scaling-up side also has consistent evidence (our assessment). RFT finds that SFT accuracy grows log-linearly with the amount of supervised data[^165]. Demystifying Long CoT runs SFT on Llama-3.1-8B with long CoT distilled from QwQ-32B-Preview, and MATH-500 exceeds 70% and is still not saturated at 3.5B tokens, whereas short CoT distilled from Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct converges below 55%[^83]. Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct keeps benefiting from more data when learning from DeepSeek-R1 data, and about 16k examples already raise its average score by 15.2%[^169].

The disagreement most likely comes from three uncontrolled variables (our inference). The first is the number of optimization steps. LESS trains every subset for 4 epochs, and the authors say that adding epochs for small subsets brings no gain[^164]; Select2Reason trains subsets of any size for 3 epochs[^173]. Under this setting, a 10% subset receives only about one tenth of the gradient steps of the full set (our inference, based on [^173]). Under a fixed update budget, by contrast, Olmo3-7B trained for 128 epochs on 400 long CoT samples scores 12 to 26 percentage points higher on AIME'24/25 and GPQA than when trained for 1 epoch on 51,200 samples[^18]. The second is the base model's knowledge. With the same LIMO data, Qwen1.5-32B-Chat reaches only 9.2% on AIME2024, while Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct reaches 63.3%[^72]. The third is evaluation difficulty. The Select2Reason subset wins on MATH but lags on AIME24[^173]; in the influence-function pruning paper, every method's score on AIME24 is negligible[^7]. The opposite conclusions of OpenThoughts and ZGCM-1 on scaling up and pruning should likewise be compared in light of these three variables (our assessment).

### 8.2 Selection Signals and Their Confounds

On top of answer-correctness filtering, the most common second signal is difficulty relative to some model; Light-R1's two-level pass-rate filtering belongs to this type (our assessment, based on [^174]). When there is no ground-truth answer, Phi-4-reasoning uses the majority answer of a strong model as a proxy and estimates difficulty by the rate at which a weaker model agrees with it[^175]. In prompt selection for reinforcement learning, such signals converge on the "neither all correct nor all wrong" range (our assessment, based on [^174][^178]). Light-R1 keeps only RL prompts with pass rates between 0.25 and 0.625[^174]. Online difficulty filtering, with Qwen2.5-3B after an SFT cold start on 1.1K examples as the policy, gives a five-benchmark mean of 30.1 for the balanced range (0.3, 0.7), versus 26.3 for GRPO without filtering and 24.9 to 25.9 for skewed filtering that cuts only one side[^178]. Its theoretical basis is that the expected policy improvement is lower-bounded by the variance of the task success rate[^178]. A pass rate of zero does not mean hard either: Light-R1's manual inspection found that for more than half of such questions the answers cannot be verified or are themselves wrong[^174]. 1-shot RLVR ranks examples by the variance of their historical training accuracy, but examples with medium or low variance can also improve MATH500 when used alone[^172].

Likelihood-based selection (naturalness selection) computes the average log probability that the model assigns to each sample and keeps those with high probability, assuming that they better match the model's inherent preferences[^21]. The Signal is in the Steps points out that the average log probability of the whole trace (GALP) works on short single-teacher responses but fails in settings with long traces and multiple teachers[^6]. On 817 LIMO questions with three teachers, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3-32B and QwQ-32B, the traces of Qwen3-32B have the highest GALP under both the 7B and the 32B student, yet yield the worst training results[^6]. The paper proposes LALP, which splits a trace into steps, scores each step only within a small local window, and then averages over the steps[^6]. The two scores can be written in the following form (our assessment, notation based on [^21]):

```math
s_{\mathrm{G}}(r)=\frac{1}{|r|}\sum_{t}\log p_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t},q),\qquad
s_{\mathrm{L}}(r)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{j}\frac{1}{|s_j|}\sum_{l}\log p_\theta\big(s_{j,l}\mid s_{j,<l},\,s_{j-k:j-1},\,q\big)
```

Here $`\mathcal{S}`$ is the set of steps, and $`k`$ is the number of steps in the window (our assessment). In the setting where one trace per question is selected from the three-teacher pool, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct trained on the data selected by LALP reaches a mean of 0.726, versus 0.632 for GALP and 0.651 for random selection[^6]; the authors argue that global likelihood here produces systematic misranking rather than noise[^6].

Likelihood-based signals are also confounded by step length[^21]. On the Step Length Confounding lists the Local LP of Just et al. as the current best naturalness method[^21]; this method is LALP[^21][^6]. It finds that such selection systematically favors samples with longer reasoning steps[^21]. The mechanism is that the first token of each step has a low probability, and the longer the step, the more thinly this token is spread in the average, so the average log probability is inflated accordingly[^21]; the effect of total response length is much weaker[^21]. On the 800 questions of LIMO-v2, with four models each generating 5 traces per question for 20 in total, the samples selected by several naturalness methods all have markedly longer steps[^21]. When four models of different sizes are trained on LIMO-v2 and AceReason-1.1-SFT, two variants, one discarding the first-token probabilities and one removing the first-token confound with causal debiasing regression, achieve average accuracies 6.28% and 9.08% higher than Local LP[^21]. The consequence of the bias is that the selector concentrates excessively on a single data source[^21].

Gradient-based methods use "influence on the target" as the signal (our assessment). LESS finds that unnormalized gradient inner products favor short sequences with larger gradient norms, and therefore switches to cosine similarity[^164]. Influence-function pruning scores and trains with Llama3-8B-Instruct on LIMO; keeping 90% gives 0.8113 on GSM8k, versus 0.7839 for randomly keeping 90% and 0.7722 without pruning[^7]. But that paper selects checkpoints with MATH500 as the validation set[^7], and when the selected subsets are used for Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, no strategy consistently beats the baseline[^7]. GRACE measures, at the step level, alignment with the gradient in the answer direction and consistency with the preceding trace; when a 20% subset of multimodal math CoT is selected with Qwen3-VL-2B, removing the answer-alignment term lowers the relative mean from 108.8 to 97.5[^26]. DeGMatch offers a stronger diagnosis: the candidate gradients of long reasoning traces are dominated by a few group-level directions, and the top 50 principal components explain 86.7% to 89.4% of the centered gradient variance[^55]. It first centers the gradients with the group mean and removes the principal components, and then performs target matching[^55]. When ranking with only the top 50 principal components, the accuracy of identifying five target classes is 56%, and 71% after removing the mean and these principal components[^55]. With a model derived from Qwen2.5-Math-7B as the student and 10K traces selected by each method from the 93K traces of OpenR1-Math-220k, the mean over eight math benchmarks is 55.03 for random selection, 58.73 for selection by length, 56.98 for LESS and 60.73 for DeGMatch[^55]. The paper also finds that target-aware methods such as LESS, RDS+, GIST and TAROT cannot consistently beat the length baseline in reasoning settings[^55]. On a 3K subset targeting AIME, compared with a length-stratified random subset with a similar total token count (36.1M versus 35.3M), its mean is 34.83 versus 33.70[^55].

Signals that do not rely on gradients are cheaper (our assessment). HES sums the entropy of only the 0.5% of tokens with the highest entropy[^28]. With SFT of Qwen3-8B-Base on Mixture-of-Thoughts, the top-20% HES subset in the math domain has a mean of 31.34, versus 31.83 for the full set, 29.05 for selecting the same proportion by length, and 23.86 for random selection[^28]. Within every length group, high-HES subsets beat low-HES subsets[^28]. TEMP judges difficulty only from the loss of the first 100 tokens of a reasoning trace on perturbed checkpoints[^35]. When selecting 1k examples from M23k, it beats the second-best baseline by up to 1.1% and 1.7% on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct[^35]. TEMP also points out that loss-, embedding- and gradient-based metrics are mainly dominated by differences in trace length, verbosity and style[^35].

RSR measures a trace by the ratio of its average token rank to its average negative log-likelihood under the student, and takes the one with the smallest ratio[^13]. Across 5 students and 11 teachers, its average Spearman correlation with post-training performance is 0.86, whereas average token length as the metric gives only 0.53[^13]. When 1 trace is selected per question from 33 candidates by 11 teachers, for 5k questions in total, Qwen-3-14B has a four-benchmark mean of 78.6, versus 70.2 for random selection and 71.4 for taking the longest[^13]. But in DeGMatch's setting of selecting 10K out of 93K traces, the subset selected by RSR has only 34.9M tokens and a mean of 51.24, below the 55.03 of random selection[^55]. The difference between the two most likely lies in whether selection happens within a question or across questions (our inference, based on [^13][^55]). The RSR authors also acknowledge that cross-question selection introduces additional confounds[^13]. MI-Distillation ran a comparison that changes only the ranking criterion: with the same candidate pool and the same correctness filtering, on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, SeqLSS has a mean of 47.14, versus 44.90 for PPL, 43.95 for random selection and 42.10 for reverse ranking[^43].

The evaluation target of diversity signals is shifting from SFT itself to subsequent RL (our assessment). DiverseSFTTraces characterizes sequences of step events with rule-based "path fingerprints" and performs farthest-point selection after clustering[^48]. Compared with "similar" selection under the same budget, OLMo3-7B, after the same RL, scores 16.9 points higher in pass@8 on environments unseen during SFT[^48]. The path-diverse checkpoint has slightly lower average accuracy, but prompts with mixed success and failure make up 54.7% of its prompts, versus 46.9% for similar selection[^48]; under binary outcome rewards, a rollout group that is all correct or all wrong has zero within-group advantage[^48]. The cluster diversity of synthetic text correlates positively with pretraining and SFT performance, but this evidence comes only from 350M and 1.4B models[^179] and does not involve reasoning traces (our assessment).

The common problem of these signals is length (our assessment, based on [^21][^35][^55]). Methods that explicitly control for length include LESS's gradient normalization[^164], HES's length-grouped tests[^28] and DeGMatch's token-matched comparison[^55]; Select2Reason's ranker itself contains a length term[^173]. The cross-model stability of selection is also insufficient: when subsets selected with Llama are used to train Qwen, no strategy consistently wins[^7], and S2L ranks second on Llama3.1 but is only slightly better than random on Qwen2.5[^35]. The cost of selection itself differs by several orders of magnitude (our assessment). The regression fit for step-length debiasing usually finishes in a few seconds[^21], and path-fingerprint selection on Nemotron-Cascade 2 needs only about three hours on one CPU node[^48]. RSR's scoring of 5,000 traces takes less than 1 hour on a single H200[^13], whereas DeGMatch needs about 58.3 GPU hours to compute gradients for the math candidate pool[^55]. ### 8.3 Dimensions of Scale

Scale has at least three dimensions: the number of questions, the number of traces per question and the number of training epochs (our assessment). RFT was one of the earlier works to examine the relationship between the first two systematically (our assessment). On the 7,473 training questions of GSM8K, it has the SFT model sample from itself, up to 100 samples per question, keeps only the paths whose answers are correct and whose calculations have been rechecked with Python, and then deduplicates them by their lists of equations[^165]. The maj1@1 of LLaMA-7B rises from 35.9 with SFT to 41.7, while the 33B model gains nothing (SFT 54.6, RFT 54.5)[^165]. With rejection-sampling results pooled from multiple models, LLaMA-7B reaches 49.3[^165]. The gain grows with the number of distinct reasoning paths rather than the number of samples[^165]. Stronger generators actually produce less diversity: LLaMA-33B-SFT generates 88.7 correct paths per question on average, and only 6.5% of the paths are unique to it[^165].

The same team then used this data to study data mixing ratios (our assessment, based on [^165][^166]). Dong et al. take subsets of GSM8K RFT, Code Alpaca and ShareGPT at proportions from 1 to 1/256 and train on them either separately or as a mixture[^166]; the GSM8K RFT data comes from Yuan et al.'s RFT paper[^166]. Math and code abilities keep improving as the amount of data grows, while general abilities plateau after about 1k examples[^166]. Mixing is mutually beneficial in low-resource settings but causes conflicts when data is plentiful, and the absolute amount of each component matters more than its proportion[^166]. Compared with mixed sequential training, their dual-stage mixed fine-tuning raises the GSM8K score of LLaMA-7B from 32.6 to 41.92 and that of 33B from 44.24 to 56.36[^166].

On the trade-off between the number of questions and the number of samples per question, the two kinds of evidence disagree (our assessment). RFT finds that doubling the samples per question gains less than doubling the training questions, because new paths do not bring new questions[^165]. BehavioralEssence, by contrast, compares at a fixed total number of traces, and 128 traces for each of 500 questions give almost the same average results as 4 traces for each of 16K questions[^54]. The most likely explanatory variable is the form of the traces (our inference). RFT works on GSM8K, where the number of distinct reasoning paths for the same question has an upper limit[^165]; BehavioralEssence studies long-CoT distillation and argues that what it transfers is mainly reusable reasoning behaviors[^54].

DataRepetition treats the number of training epochs as the third dimension[^18]. Under a fixed budget of 51200 updates, Olmo3-7B trained for 32 epochs on 1600 samples reaches a benchmark mean of 39%, but only 17% when trained for 1 epoch on 51200 samples[^18]. The gains saturate at around 32 to 64 epochs, and training-token accuracy reaching full memorization can serve as a stopping signal[^18]. Only 24% of the generations of the single-epoch model terminate properly, against nearly 89% for the 32-epoch model[^18]. As epochs increase, the validation loss rises markedly[^18], yet benchmark scores keep improving, so validation loss should not be used as the early-stopping criterion for long-CoT SFT (our inference, based on [^18]). Teacher strength determines whether scaling up helps: with Qwen3-0.6B as the teacher, the average score falls as the number of samples grows[^18].

Scaling up also has cross-domain costs (our assessment). In Light-R1, GPQA declines from the second stage onward, which the authors attribute to heavy math training weakening generalization[^174]. The influence-function paper also finds that math SFT hurts GPQA, and that pruning more actually does less harm[^7]. Serial scaling at the mid-training stage likewise first rises, then plateaus, and then declines[^51].

The evidence in this direction still has gaps (our assessment). RFT does not fit a scaling law, because many of the numbers are estimates and the settings across models are not aligned[^165]. DataRepetition finds no causal mechanism, and the optimal dataset size still cannot be determined in advance[^18]. Among the papers covered in this section, no work yet jointly compares the number of questions, the number of samples per question and the number of training epochs within a single controlled framework (our assessment).

### 8.4 What Matters in the Traces

A set of controlled studies shows that what students learn is mainly the structure and behavior of the traces, not their step-by-step content (our assessment, based on [^169][^170][^54]). With Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct as the student, Li et al. reach 56.7% (+40.0%) on AIME 2024 with just 17k long-CoT samples[^169]. In perturbation experiments with 4,618 correct QwQ-32B-Preview traces as the control, training only on samples with wrong answers gives an average score of 63.1%, only 3.2% below the all-correct set[^169]. With 70% of the numbers corrupted the score is still 62%, but with all numbers corrupted it falls to 2.7%[^169]. Shuffling the reasoning steps of 67% of the samples lowers AIME 2024 by 13.3%[^169]. Deleting steps also shortens the traces, whereas inserting steps largely keeps the original length[^169]. Valmeekam et al. use A\* search to generate formally verifiable maze traces and train 0.5B Qwen models from scratch, with 500K samples per model[^170]. When training and testing on Wilson mazes, planning accuracy is 10.0 when training on solutions only, 79.9 with correct traces, and 83.3 with traces randomly swapped across problems[^170]. On the open-book QA task CoTemp QA, DeepSeek-R1 traces give the best training results for three of the four students, yet participants rated them the hardest to understand[^182].

Traces with wrong answers are usable as well (our assessment, based on [^12][^54]). Shape of Thought uses Gemma-2-27B-IT to take, for each question, one trace with a correct answer and one with a wrong answer[^12]. On GSM8K, the best accuracy of Gemma-2-2B trained on human CoT is 0.29, and the wrong-answer and correct-answer traces are higher by a further 0.11 and 0.09, respectively[^12]. The initial training loss is 0.55 to 0.59 for synthetic traces and 0.86 for human traces, on which basis the authors argue that distributional closeness may matter more than correctness[^12]. BehavioralEssence uses Qwen3-4B to generate 16 traces for each of 3,000 DAPO-Math-17K questions, obtaining 22,222 correct and 25,778 wrong traces[^54]. Training only on wrong traces retains 97.7% of the absolute accuracy of correct traces on the math sets[^54]; with Qwen3-4B as the student, the linear CKA between the representations of the two models reaches 0.998[^54]. In DataRepetition, negative examples are even slightly better, at 40.0% versus 37.3% on AIME'24[^18]. Cognitive Behaviors finds that, with the same RL on Countdown, Qwen-2.5-3B reaches about 60% while Llama-3.2-3B reaches 30%[^87]. After Llama is primed with synthetic traces containing behaviors such as verification and backtracking, its RL performance catches up with Qwen[^87], and this also holds when the priming traces have wrong answers[^87]. Both controls, an empty chain of thought and length-matched placeholders, only match the Llama base model, at about 30% to 35%[^87].

Backtracking behavior is not always beneficial either[^183]. Qin et al. use DFS to generate search traces with backtracking on CountDown, and DFS solves only 57% of the problems[^183]. Comparing 17M and 38M models trained from scratch at equal inference compute, the models trained on backtracking traces do worse on CountDown than direct solutions plus parallel sampling, but better on Sudoku[^183]. The models copy the search process in the traces, including making mistakes first and then correcting them, and their performance is also limited by the quality of the trace generator[^183]. After GRPO, the backtracking model solves nearly 70% under a 4096-token budget, surpassing the 57% of the DFS teacher[^183].

The opposing evidence comes from interventions in the generation process (our assessment). Lee et al. hold the generator, the question set and the correctness filter fixed, and vary only whether, during generation, the reference answer is given along with an instruction to reason toward it[^184]. After Qwen3-8B is self-distilled on 935 questions, MATH-500 drops from 95.1 in the answer-blind condition to 78.9, and AIME 2024–2025 from 71.7 to 44.4[^184]. Answer-blind traces truncated to the same length as the leaked traces still score 95.0, which shows that the loss comes from content rather than length[^184]. On the subset where the answer appears too early, cutting only the line that first states the answer (about 1.4% of the trace) recovers 24.6 of the 29.8-point gap[^184]. A STaR-style remedy that leaks the answer only on failed questions still loses 4.0 points[^184]. This loss does not appear on multiple-choice benchmarks such as MMLU and GPQA-Diamond[^184].

The two bodies of evidence are most likely reconciled by "whether the perturbation changes the generation process" (our inference, based on [^169][^184]). Corrupted numbers, wrong answers and cross-problem swaps all preserve the form of forward derivation or search, whereas shuffled steps and answer-conditioned rationalization destroy it (our inference, based on [^169][^170][^184]). Task type is a second variable: on Countdown, which requires an exact sequence of operations, the gain from correct traces is clearly larger than from wrong traces, with absolute gains of up to 20%[^12], while the loss from answer conditioning disappears on multiple-choice questions (our inference, based on [^12][^184]). BehavioralEssence also restricts its conclusion to local factual errors, excluding wholesale breakdowns of reasoning[^54]. In addition, the teacher CoT of Chen et al. was generated with the reference answer given in the prompt and a requirement to agree with it[^171]. In light of the results of the answer-conditioning study, their comparisons of teachers and of granularity may carry this confound (our inference, based on [^171][^184]).

### 8.5 Length and Compression

Wu et al. have the Qwen2.5-Instruct series generate 60 solutions of varying length per question on MATH Level 5[^167]. Accuracy first rises and then falls with the number of CoT steps, and the optimal number of steps increases with question difficulty and decreases with model capability[^167]. The optimal length falls from 14 steps for the 1.5B model to 4 steps for the 72B model, and for the 72B model the accuracy gap between the optimal length and the longest CoT can reach 40%[^167]. On synthetic arithmetic involving only addition, a 6-layer GPT-2 trained on optimal-length CoT beats a 9-layer model trained on random lengths[^167]. The authors also acknowledge that the optimal length for real problems is hard to estimate[^167].

Whether long CoT helps depends on the size of the student (our assessment, based on [^71][^43]). On the 7,500 questions of MATH, Small Models Struggle generates long CoT with QwQ-32B-Preview and short CoT with Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct[^71]. With long CoT, Qwen2.5-0.5B, 1.5B and 3B average 14.8, 27.0 and 40.3, below their 19.5, 34.2 and 43.4 with short CoT[^71]. For 7B, 14B and 32B the pattern reverses, with 32B at 73.0 versus 59.3[^71]. MI-Distillation also reports that long CoT is often worse than short CoT for small student models[^43], and its gradient analysis shows that long CoT induces larger gradient magnitudes and more concentrated update directions, the more so the larger the student[^43]. PropsOfSupervision keeps the questions and answers unchanged and rewrites only the reasoning part[^25]. The Lengthy variant, about twice as long as the Detailed variant, causes catastrophic forgetting in PLLuM-8B, yet improves Bielik-11B by 12.3% and 15.0% on MoT-PL-eval and LightR1, respectively[^25].

There are three routes to constructing compressed data (our assessment). CoT-Valve finds a direction in parameter space that can be scaled to control generation length, and constructs several solutions, from long to short, for the same question[^168]. The chain length of QwQ-32B-Preview on GSM8K falls from 741 to 225 tokens, while accuracy goes from 95.07% to 94.92%[^168]. LLaMA-3.2-1B scores 52.69 (759.3 tokens) when trained on the long chains of QwQ and 55.50 (267.0 tokens) when trained on chains compressed by CoT-Valve[^168]. But "moderately short is best" is seen only with small models and simple datasets[^168]. The second route is self-training: Munkhbat et al. have the student run best-of-N sampling itself (optionally aided by concise examples as few-shot conditioning) and keep only the shortest correct path for each question[^176]. On GSM8K and MATH, the combined method cuts output tokens by 30% on average across five model families while keeping average accuracy unchanged[^176]. The shortest path is chosen per question rather than globally in order to keep the hard questions that need long reasoning[^176]. Using external concise data directly costs accuracy: fine-tuned on human CoT, the five models average only 65.73 on GSM8K, against a zero-shot baseline of 78.06[^176]. The third route is pruning and rewriting: Lyu et al. separately examine granularity, degree of rewriting and compression budget[^177]. In the short-CoT setting of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the MATH baseline is 75.3; token-level NLL pruning (ratio 0.7) drops it to 15.8, while step-level entropy pruning (same ratio) keeps it at 74.8[^177]. Structure-preserving summarization gives 76.8 at a ratio of 0.5, and semantic fusion 71.7[^177]. Long-CoT students keep their verbose habits under concise supervision, and the compression rate at training time is only an optimistic lower bound on deployment cost[^177]. ZippingThought finds, mainly on synthetic arithmetic tasks, that compressed CoT of coarser granularity needs more SFT data and also benefits more from data expansion than explicit CoT does[^31]. When data is limited, repeated training improves Composed CoT but hurts Implicit CoT[^31]. SFT cannot break reasoning down more finely than the compression granularity of the training data, whereas RLVR can[^31].

Length is also a confounding variable in selection (our assessment). Selecting by length is a strong baseline: in Select2Reason, the subset of the longest traces consistently beats the medium and shortest subsets[^173]. In DeGMatch, the 10K examples selected by length average 58.73, above the 55.03 of random selection, but use 142.3M tokens against only 60.3M for random[^55]. In s1, the 1K subset of only the longest traces improves GPQA but is still worse than s1K overall[^86]. Merely adding length brings no gain: after Level 1-granularity chains are padded to the length of Level 5, Gemma 2B scores 46.62 on GSM8K, versus 47.61 for the original chains and 52.92 for genuine Level 5[^171].

The disagreement between "longer is better" and "shorter is better" is mainly explained by two variables (our inference). The first is student capacity relative to question difficulty: the optimal length rises with difficulty and falls with capability, and the "shorter is better" of CoT-Valve disappears on complex datasets (our inference, based on [^167][^168][^71]). The second is the granularity of length selection: taking the longest globally likely also selects the hard questions, whereas taking the shortest per question keeps the hard questions (our inference, based on [^173][^176]).

### 8.6 Teacher-Student Fit

When teachers are compared on a fixed question set, a strong teacher can win clearly[^180]. On the same 1.89M queries, Tian et al. distill separately from AM-Thinking-v1, Qwen3-235B-A22B and DeepSeek-R1, resampling every response until its verification score is at least 0.9[^180]. With the Qwen2.5-32B base model as the student, the three score 84.3, 79.4 and 70.9 on AIME2024 and 72.2, 62.2 and 52.8 on AIME2025[^180]. The winning data is not the longest: the average token count is 4196.7 for Qwen3, 3784.8 for DeepSeek-R1 and 3757.3 for AM, and AM has the lowest average perplexity (2.5)[^180]. However, the study uses different perplexity thresholds for different teachers and trains only one student[^180], and AM-Thinking-v1 comes from a team that overlaps with the authors[^180].

The picture differs for small student models (our assessment). Across 4 teachers and 7 students, Chen et al. find that a stronger teacher does not always produce a better student, but that stronger students benefit more from stronger teachers[^171]. Small Models Struggle calls this the small-model learnability gap and proposes Mix Distillation, which mixes long and short CoT, or responses from large and small teachers[^71]. With Mix-Long, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct scores more than 8 points higher on MATH and AMC than when learning long CoT directly, and with Mix-Large more than 7 points higher on MATH, AIME and AMC than when learning only the CoT of the large teacher[^71]. PerSyn refines this idea down to individual questions and uses the above method as its Mix baseline[^181]. It measures learnability by the log-likelihood of the student and quality by a reward model, and trains a router to assign a teacher to each question[^181]. The average of Qwen2.5-3B over six benchmarks, IFEval, TruthfulQA, LiveBench, GSM8K, MATH and SVAMP, rises from 55.38 with the single strongest teacher to 58.09, versus 55.39 for Mix[^181]; Qwen2.5-0.5B rises from 28.51 to 34.13[^181]. More than 95% of the questions are routed to smaller teachers[^181], whereas the strongest-teacher baseline, which uses long CoT throughout, traps students in non-terminating repetitive reasoning[^181].

Teacher selection can also be done cheaply with student-side signals (our assessment). LALP needs only 200 questions to reproduce the teacher ranking on the full set of 817 questions, namely QwQ over DeepSeek-R1 over Qwen3, while GALP gives the reverse order[^6]. RSR can select a teacher by sampling only 200 traces per teacher[^13], and its per-question mixed selection exceeds the best score of any single teacher for 4 of the 5 students[^13].

"Whether a stronger teacher is better" is most likely explained by the size of the student relative to the teacher (our inference, based on [^171][^180][^181]). The student of Tian et al. is a 32B base model, the main students of PerSyn lie between 0.5B and 3B, and in Small Models Struggle the disadvantage of long CoT appears only for students no larger than 3B (our inference, based on [^180][^181][^71]). The issues noted above, the misaligned filtering thresholds and the teacher sharing an origin with the authors, also weaken the causal interpretation of the conclusion of Tian et al. (our assessment, based on [^180]).

### 8.7 Summary (our assessment)

- The gains from small curated sets are real, but the common "subset beats the full set" comparisons do not align the number of optimization steps (based on [^164][^173][^55]). Under a fixed update budget, Olmo3-7B gains 12 to 26 percentage points just by training repeatedly on a small amount of data, and the pretraining knowledge of the base model also determines whether curation works (based on [^18][^72]).
- Selection signals are commonly confounded by length, in forms that include step length, total length and group-level structure in the gradients, and selection conclusions without a length control should be treated as weak evidence (based on [^21][^35][^55]).
- When the errors are mostly local factual errors, answer correctness affects the value of a trace less than structure and behavior do (based on [^169][^54][^87]). Answer-conditioned generation, in contrast, damages the data in ways that correctness filtering cannot detect (based on [^184]).
- The optimal length varies with student capability and question difficulty, and small student models are better suited to short chains or a mix of long and short ones (based on [^167][^71][^168]). Compressed data needs more samples, and the compression rate at training time does not equal the savings at inference time (based on [^31][^177]).
- Teacher selection should be conditioned on the student: strong teachers win for 32B students, students no larger than 3B are better served by smaller teachers or by teachers routed per question, and low-cost student-side signals can already produce the teacher ranking (based on [^180][^181][^6]).
