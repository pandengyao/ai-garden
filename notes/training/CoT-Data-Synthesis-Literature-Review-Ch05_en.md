## 5 Question and Task Sources

Under the taxonomy of this review, a CoT data synthesis pipeline consists of four stages: question sources $`\mathcal{Q}`$, trace generation $`\mathcal{G}`$, retention criteria $`\mathcal{V}`$ and selection strategy $`\mathcal{S}`$ (our assessment). This section discusses the first stage: where the training questions come from, whether they can be judged correct automatically, relative to whom their difficulty is defined and how it is controlled, and how overlap with evaluation sets is ruled out. The mainstream practice in 2023–2024 was to rewrite or expand benchmark training questions and then have a strong teacher write the solutions, and most of the new questions had no checkable answers (our assessment). From 2025 onward, question sets have increasingly been constructed so that "the answer is verifiable and the difficulty falls at the edge of the learner's ability", and a line in which the model poses its own questions has emerged (our assessment). Trace generation and retention criteria are discussed in later sections.

### 5.1 Reusing and Rewriting Existing Question Banks

**Lineage and mechanism.** The earliest math reasoning datasets directly rewrote the training questions of GSM8K and MATH (our assessment). MetaMath bootstraps math questions by rewriting them from multiple perspectives, yielding MetaMathQA[^75]. The forward direction includes the original questions and LLM-rephrased questions; the backward direction masks one token in a question as "x", gives the answer, and has the model solve for the masked quantity[^75]. On the answer side it uses rejection sampling, keeping only reasoning paths with correct answers, 395K in total[^75]. Evol-Instruct, proposed by WizardLM, rewrites instructions step by step to make them more complex[^79]. WizardMath applied it to math, adding downward evolution, which simplifies questions, to the upward evolution that makes them harder[^114]. Using the GSM8K and MATH training sets as seeds, it evolved each question for 5 rounds with GPT-4 (2 downward, 3 upward), obtaining 448k samples after deduplication[^114]. Decontamination filtered out another 30k, and the answers were generated step by step by GPT-4-0613[^114].

MathScale argues that new questions rewritten one by one are highly similar to the original training questions and can hardly support large-scale data[^115]. It used GPT-3.5-Turbo-0613 to extract 2,018 topics and 8,892 knowledge points from about 20K MwpBench training questions, built a concept graph, and then generated 2M question-answer pairs by combining them[^115]. OpenMathInstruct-1 does not augment the questions; it only synthesizes code-interpreter solutions for the training questions of GSM8K and MATH[^132]. Its motivation is licensing: MetaMathQA and MAmmoTH come from closed-source models with restrictive commercial terms, so the authors switched to the permissively licensed Mixtral[^132]. MAmmoTH's training set is compiled from 13 math datasets with intermediate reasoning[^66]. OpenMathInstruct-2 points out that the questions of OpenMathInstruct-1 are all taken from the MATH and GSM8K training sets, which limits their diversity[^88]. It uses these two training sets as seeds for 5-shot prompting and, without explicitly asking for harder questions, relies only on the randomness of nucleus sampling to generate new questions[^88]. The final data contain 14M question-answer pairs and about 600K unique questions, synthesized by Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct[^88].

**Evidence.** This branch has several controlled comparisons with the same student and the same data volume (our assessment). With supervised fine-tuning of Mistral-7B, two rounds of downward evolution raised GSM8K from 59.7 to 74.5 and MATH from 15.1 to 34.7, compared with the original 7.5k human-annotated samples[^114]. Likewise with Mistral-7B as the base model and at the same data scale, the evolved data score 3%–6% higher than MetaMath on GSM8K and 15%–20% higher on MATH[^114]. MathScale trained LLaMA-2 7B on 25K samples, and restricting the seed questions to the GSM8K and MATH training sets lowered performance by 3.5%[^115]. Keeping only half of the knowledge points lowered it by 8.6%, while keeping only half of the topics lowered it by just 2.3%[^115]. OpenMathInstruct-2, with Llama3.1-8B-Base as the student and 256K question-answer pairs held fixed, found that increasing the number of unique questions from 1K to 6.5K improved the MATH validation set score by 10.5%[^88]. OpenMathInstruct-1 added more solutions for a fixed set of questions: Mistral-7B's MATH score on a 1K validation subset rose from 37.0 with 128K samples to 41.6 with 512K samples[^132]. The authors report that simply training for more steps brings no gain[^132].

**Limitations and controversies.** The common cost of rewriting methods is that the new questions have no checkable answers (our assessment). WizardMath acknowledges that the evolved instructions lack correct answers[^114]. In a controlled experiment with 5K samples and LLaMA-2 7B, MathScale used GPT-4 to identify and replace 26% of the GPT-3.5 solutions[^115]. Yet the macro average did not improve: 11.5 with all GPT-3.5 solutions, 11.1 after replacement, and 10.9 with all GPT-4 solutions[^115]. On this basis the authors removed the verification step from the final pipeline[^115]. Their explanation is that wrong solutions still help open-source models imitate the distribution of GPT-3.5[^115]. This conclusion comes from only 5K samples, and there is as yet no evidence on whether it extrapolates to larger scales (our assessment). Including a reference solution in the prompt also induces answer copying: OpenMathInstruct-1 observed shortcut solutions of the print(ANSWER) kind[^132]. After switching to masked reference solutions in the prompt, coverage of the MATH training set rose from 80.1% to 85.9%[^132]. Old question sets may also be too easy for stronger students (our assessment, based on [^17]). Agentic Proposing reports that, under the same GRPO recipe, training on MetaMath degraded the performance of stronger reasoning models such as Qwen3-4B[^17]. Its diagnosis is that the base models' pass rates on these questions are as high as 85%–89%, so the within-group relative advantage tends to zero[^17].

### 5.2 Mining Questions from the Web and Documents

**Lineage and mechanism.** MAmmoTH2 argues that instructions synthesized by GPT-4 are biased, low in diversity and prone to hallucination, and instead harvests naturally occurring question-answer pairs from web corpora[^116]. It trains fastText on seeds from quiz websites to recall documents, then has GPT-4 screen the recalled domains a second time, obtaining 18M documents[^116]. Only 30% of them contain question-answer pairs, about 5M pairs[^116]. Mixtral-22B×8 and Qwen-72B then rewrite the format and fill in the intermediate steps when an answer lacks an explanation, yielding WebInstruct with 10M pairs[^116]. General-Reasoner traces WebInstruct back to its original web pages, re-crawls them, and removes questions without human-written answers[^120]. It uses Gemini-1.5-Pro to extract about 1M verifiable questions with short answers[^120]. It then has Gemini-2.0-Flash generate 8 candidate solutions per question and removes the questions on which the candidates are all wrong or all correct, leaving about 230k[^120].

NaturalReasoning does not extract questions already present in documents; instead it has an LLM write a new, self-consistent question based on DCLM-baseline and FineMath documents[^122]. When a reference answer can be derived from the document, it is kept as well, and 81.68% of the 2.8M questions come with reference answers[^122]. DeepMath-103K argues that existing question sets mostly re-collect existing questions and overlap heavily with one another[^119]. It draws mainly on a Math StackExchange subset, supplemented by NuminaMath-CoT, obtaining 2,869K raw questions[^119]. After decontamination, difficulty filtering and verifiability filtering, 95K remain, and the 8K questions of SimpleRL are then merged in[^119]. The winning AIMO-2 solution extracts questions from 620K AoPS forum discussions, removes multiple-choice, true/false and invalid questions, and rewrites proof questions into questions with answers, obtaining 540K questions[^133]. For web text without reference answers, Golden Goose has an LLM mask a key span of reasoning as the answer and generate distractors, turning fill-in-the-blank into multiple choice, so that RL only needs to compare options[^15]. Synth uses 58,698 Wikipedia articles as seeds and amplifies them in a structured way into a synthetic corpus of nearly 80B tokens[^50]. The authors found that when a single LLM is prompted with rotating instructions, its generations collapse into a narrow distribution of wording and format[^50].

**Evidence.** Without using any in-domain training data, MAmmoTH2-7B (Mistral) rose from 11% to 36.7% on MATH and from 36% to 68.4% on GSM8K[^116]. With zero RL from Qwen2.5-7B-Base, General-Reasoner scores 58.9% on MMLU-Pro, versus 47.7% for the base model and 57.0% for the Instruct version[^120]. In its 7B ablation, training only on math questions gives 49.1% versus 48.5% on math benchmarks, but MMLU-Pro, GPQA and SuperGPQA each drop by about 2 percentage points[^120]. With supervised fine-tuning of Llama3.1-8B-Base, measured by the average score on MATH, GPQA and MMLU-Pro, 1.5M NaturalReasoning samples already surpass Llama3.1-8B-Instruct[^122]. OpenMathInstruct-2 and WebInstruct still do not surpass it at 2.8M samples, but the former always has the highest MATH score, 59.25 at 2.8M samples[^122]. Randomly sampling 1K NaturalReasoning questions, with DeepSeek-R1 writing one response for each, to fine-tune Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct gives a three-benchmark average of 75.9, versus 75.6 for s1K-1.1 and 73.3 for LIMO[^122]. With zero RL from Qwen-2.5-7B, DeepMath-Zero-7B scores 85.5, 20.4 and 17.5 on MATH500, AIME24 and AIME25, versus 54.8, 7.7 and 5.4 for the base model[^119]. Golden Goose synthesized about 180K cybersecurity tasks from raw web pages, and after 100 steps of RL, Qwen-4B-Instruct gained 4.44% in absolute terms on 3 benchmarks[^15].

**Limitations and controversies.** The main problem with web questions is that their answers and reasoning are mostly filled in by LLMs (our assessment). In a manual spot check of 50 WebInstruct samples, 78% were improved by the rewriting and 10% had hallucinations introduced[^116]. In the publicly released 1.1M subset of NaturalReasoning, 18.29% of the questions have no reference answer, and only 9.71% have single-word answers[^61]. The average math scores of the question sources listed in Table 5-1 differ by at most 10.4 points (our count). DeepMath-103K and Big-Math, which were curated for RL, do not come out ahead in this distillation setting; the value of a question set depends on its downstream use (our inference, based on [^4][^118][^119]). This comparison covers only supervised distillation, and question sources with too few samples were padded out by repeating questions (our assessment, based on [^4]). The multiple-choice format also has a shortcut: with only 3 options, most questions are too easy for ProRL-1.5B-v2, which answers by elimination[^15]. With 9 options, more than 70% of the questions fall into the medium-difficulty range in which the model sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails[^15]. In the open-ended version, more than 83% of the questions always score zero for this model[^15].

### 5.3 Persona-, Skill-, and Code-Driven Synthesis

**Lineage and mechanism.** This branch supplies diversity through external structure rather than prompt wording (our assessment). Persona Hub argues that adding a persona to a synthesis prompt is enough to steer an LLM toward the corresponding perspective[^117]. The authors extracted and expanded 1,015,863,523 personas from RedPajama v2[^117]. Of these, 1.09M were used to have GPT-4 pose math questions zero-shot, with solutions generated directly by gpt-4o[^117]. OSS-Instruct argues that Self-Instruct- and Evol-Instruct-style methods inherit the biases of the LLM and of the predefined tasks, and uses real open-source code as seeds instead[^123]. It extracts 1–15 lines from each of 80K code documents and has gpt-3.5-turbo-1106 write both the question and the solution, for about 75K examples in total[^123]. KodCode synthesizes questions from 12 sources with 5 methods and has GPT-4o-0513 write both solutions and unit tests, keeping only triples whose tests pass and whose branch coverage reaches 100%[^121]. To avoid losing hard questions, each question is attempted up to 10 times[^121]. rStar-Coder points out that Magicoder, KodCode, and similar work mainly target function-level code that can be judged correct with almost no testing[^134]. It writes 37,754 seed questions together with their reference solutions into structured prompts and synthesizes 1,565K new questions[^134]. Output labels are determined by mutual verification, and questions on which multiple QwQ-32B solutions have an output agreement rate below 60% are discarded[^134]. CodeI/O has DeepSeek-V2.5 rewrite about 810.5K code files into executable functions and asks the model to predict their inputs or outputs in natural language, yielding 3.5M instances in total after filtering[^124]. Combinatorial Synthesis argues that heuristic expansion only adds linguistic diversity and leads to premature reward saturation in RLVR[^32]. It decomposes seed questions into atomic elements, recombines them in a controlled way around core elements, and then filters by execution[^32]. SynLogic equips 35 types of logic tasks with generation code and rule-based verifiers, so difficulty can be tuned through the generation parameters[^125]. Agentic Proposing has an agent select and combine skills from a skill library to pose questions[^17]. Kimi K3 has an agent recursively expand a knowledge graph through web search and then synthesize training tasks from the retrieved material[^37].

**Evidence.** After Qwen2-7B is fine-tuned on 1.07M persona questions, it reaches 64.9% on MATH, which the authors treat as an out-of-distribution test[^117]. With CodeLlama-Python-7B as the base model and 75K examples each, fine-tuning directly on comment-function pairs from open-source code leaves HumanEval+ at 34.1 and drops MultiPL-E to 24.1[^123]. Under the same conditions, OSS-Instruct scores 55.5 and 37.8[^123]. With Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct as the student, KodCode's 10K hard-question subset scores 31.8% on BigCodeBench-I Hard, versus 27.7% for a random 10K subset[^121]. On 64 seed questions and 3,150 test inputs, rStar-Coder's mutual verification labels with 96.8% accuracy, whereas having GPT-4o generate the inputs and outputs directly reaches only 12.7%[^134]. With Qwen2.5-Coder-7B as the base model, CodeI/O raises the average over 14 benchmarks from 54.8 to 57.2, versus 56.0 with the full OpenMathInstruct-2[^124]. In RL on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct with 5,000 examples each, the average LCB-v5/v6 pass@1 is 18.19 for the base model, 23.23 for real TACO questions, 23.16 for KodCode, and 25.76 for ADR[^32]. In zero RL on Qwen2.5-32B-Base with the same number of steps, adding 17k SynLogic questions on top of 35k math and 9k code questions raises BBEH from 18.5 to 28.6[^125].

**Limitations and disputes.** External structure solves wording diversity, but correctness still has to be ensured by execution or verification (our assessment). Two mathematics experts spot-checked 200 hard persona questions and found 7 invalid[^117]. KodCode's self-verification has high precision but a low retention rate: on MBPP, 97.5% of the solutions that pass self-verification also pass the original unit tests, but on LiveCodeBench-V5 only 49.9% of solutions pass self-verification[^121]. Going from 1 to 5 attempts raises the pass rate by more than 20%, which suggests that discarding a question after a single generation systematically removes hard questions (our inference, based on [^121]). By ADR's count, 47.5% of the samples unique to KodCode are simple function-level completions[^32]. On the other hand, in CodeI/O's experiments with Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, removing wrong responses according to execution results gives an average of 56.5, lower than the 56.7 of a subset about half the size[^124]. The authors attribute this to reduced data diversity[^124]. This runs opposite to the strict filtering of KodCode and rStar-Coder, which may be because CodeI/O is used in an earlier training stage, where coverage matters more than answer accuracy (our inference, based on [^124][^121][^134]).

### 5.4 Difficulty Control and Hard-Question Generation

**Lineage and mechanism.** RL turns difficulty into a question of whether a training signal exists (our assessment). SwS points out that if a group of responses is all correct or all wrong, the within-group advantage collapses to zero and the gradient vanishes[^128]. Big-Math likewise points out that questions a model never answers correctly provide no signal for most RL methods[^118]. The resulting common practice is to filter questions by a pass-rate interval under a reference model (our assessment):

```math
\hat p_M(q)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{1}\big[\mathrm{ans}(y_j)=a(q)\big],\quad y_j\sim M(\cdot\mid q),\qquad \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{keep}}=\{q:\ l\le \hat p_M(q)\le u\}
```

Here $`M`$ is the reference model, $`k`$ is the number of samples, and $`[l,u]`$ is the retention interval (our assessment). Table 5-2 lists the choices made by each work. Big-Math filters 251,122 questions from open-source question sets, checking solvability with 64 samples per question from Llama-3.1-8B and 5–8 samples per question from Llama-3.1-405B, and keeps a question if either model produces the given answer[^118]. The authors argue that multiple-choice questions can be guessed and are unsuitable for RL, so they rewrite multiple-choice questions as open-ended ones[^118]. Another family of approaches estimates difficulty from proxy signals such as GPT-4o ratings and teacher response length (our assessment, based on [^119][^11]). A NeMo Curator tutorial filters a subset from about 30M synthetic samples and then sorts it by response length in ascending order to serve as an easy-to-hard curriculum[^62].

**Hard-question generation.** Another line of work drives a question generator directly to create hard questions (our assessment). MathSmith argues that rewriting human-written questions is bound by the original distribution and makes precise difficulty control hard, so it instead randomly samples concepts from 11,000 concepts in PlanetMath and poses questions from scratch[^127]. The question generator, Qwen3-8B, is first fine-tuned on about 8k GPT-4o cold-start samples and then trained with GRPO[^127]. The reward measures complexity by the length of Qwen3-30B-A3B's solution traces and favors questions for which the teacher can give a definite answer[^127]. CoDiQ evolves seed questions step by step into harder variants over at most 8 rounds, with Doubao-Seed-1.8 ranking relative difficulty[^16]. Solvability is judged by Qwen3-32B, and the process stops when the difficulty falls below that of the previous version or the question is judged unsolvable[^16]. SwS treats as weaknesses the questions that never reach 50% accuracy and show a downward trend during first-stage RL, and extracts their concepts to recombine into new questions[^128]. Reference answers for the new questions come from self-consistency voting by reasoning models such as QwQ-32B, and questions with less than 50% agreement are discarded[^128].

**Evidence.** All MathSmith baselines use 50K questions each, with solutions rewritten by the same teacher, Qwen3-30B-A3B[^127]. In the Qwen3-8B long-CoT setting, the average on hard-question benchmarks is 71.8, versus 68.2 for NuminaMath-CoT and 65.4 with no added data[^127]. Adding the consistency reward raises the share of usable questions from 84.92% to 95.38%[^127]. An evaluation of 200 CoDiQ samples by three PhDs shows a precision of 82% for accepted questions[^16]. Compared with continuing training on the original question set for the same number of steps, SwS raises the average score of 7B and 32B models over 8 benchmarks by 10.0 and 7.7[^128]. With Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base serving as both question generator and solver, Learning to Pose Problems scores 2.69 (one round) and 3.39 (three rounds) percentage points above using only the seed questions on 10 benchmarks[^9]. However, the directional success rate of its difficulty adjustment averages only 0.61[^9].

**Limitations and disputes.** Difficulty control has two known ceilings (our assessment). The first is the verifier: CoDiQ points out that valid hard questions beyond the verifier's capability are misjudged as unsolvable and discarded[^16]. The second is student capacity: MathSmith-HC is slightly worse on the small 1.7B and 4B models, which the authors attribute to small models having difficulty learning complex questions[^127]. Filtering by correctness also removes hard questions along the way (our assessment, based on [^89][^121]). OpenCodeReasoning fine-tunes Qwen-2.5-14B-Instruct, and the full set of 445,618 examples gives 54.1 on LiveCodeBench[^89]. Using only the 151,251 examples that pass the tests gives 47.0, while using the same number of failing solutions gives 52.3[^89]. The authors find that the failing solutions are concentrated on harder questions[^89].

On how hard the questions should be, two studies reach opposite conclusions (our assessment). Using the 8-sample accuracy of the Qwen2.5-7B model after initial training, SwS divides synthetic questions into three bands, easy (5–7 correct out of 8), medium (3–5), and hard (1–4), adds 40k questions from each band to the original training set, and trains for another 200 steps[^128]. The easy band converges fastest but then saturates, while the hardest band yields the most lasting gains[^128]. Measured by the pass@1 of Qwen2.5-Math-7B on AIME 2025 and 2026, QbQ ends at 16.46% when seeds come from questions answered correctly 8 to 15 times out of 16[^39]. With seeds from questions answered correctly 1 to 7 times, it reaches 14.17% at one point midway but ends at only 11.35%, less than 0.4 percentage points away from GRPO on the original questions alone[^39]. The authors argue that variants of hard seeds often exceed the range the model can solve reliably, so the responses within a group show no difference between correct and wrong[^39]. The hard bands of the two are similar: 1–4 correct out of 8 for SwS and 1–7 correct out of 16 for QbQ (our count, based on [^128][^39]). The disagreement more likely comes from how the hard band is used: SwS trains directly on hard-band questions, while QbQ uses its hard band only as seeds and trains on their variants (our inference, based on [^128][^39]). The evaluations also differ: SwS uses 8 math benchmarks ranging from GSM8K to AIME, while QbQ looks only at new AIME questions (our assessment, based on [^128][^39]).

### 5.5 Questions Self-Posed by the Model and Their Collapse Risk

**Lineage and mechanism.** The self-posed-question route replaces the question poser with the learner itself or a model of the same origin, and the key is where the labels for the new questions come from (our assessment). CoT-Self-Instruct uses 893 verifiable questions from s1k as seeds and has Qwen3-4B first analyze the seeds and then write both new questions and their answers[^126]. A question is kept only if the answer given at generation time agrees with the model's majority-vote answer[^126]. Absolute Zero starts from just a single identity-function triple, and the same model proposes three types of code-reasoning tasks (deduction, abduction, and induction) and solves them itself[^130]. A code executor validates both the tasks and the answers, and only deterministic programs are allowed[^130]. The proposer reward is learnability: when the average solve rate is 0 the reward is 0, and otherwise it is $`1-\bar r_{\text{solve}}`$ [^130]. R-Zero does not rely on an executor and initializes a Challenger and a Solver from the same base model[^131]. The Challenger's reward is highest when the Solver's accuracy is close to 50%[^131]. The pseudo-label is the majority answer over 10 Solver samples, and only questions where 3 to 7 answers agree with it are kept[^131]. SvS has the policy rewrite its own correct solutions to underperforming questions into variant questions that reuse the original answer, so no new labeling is needed[^129]. It shares its first three authors with SwS[^128][^129]. In QbQ, the teacher GPT-5-mini rewrites questions that the current checkpoint mostly answers correctly, using five hand-designed structural transformation operators[^39]. A variant's answer must differ from that of the original question, and training uses only the question statement and the final answer[^39].

**Evidence.** In GRPO on Qwen3-4B-Base (averaged over 16 samples), CoT-Self-Instruct scores 53.0% without filtering, versus 42.7% for Self-Instruct[^126]. The 2,926 synthetic questions filtered by answer consistency reach 57.2, higher than the 44.6 of the original s1k questions and the 47.5 of 10k OpenMathReasoning questions[^126]. AZR-Coder-7B, which uses no human-curated data at all, scores 1.8 points higher in overall average than the previous best 7B model in the zero setting[^130]. On 3B, 7B, and 14B code models, AZR's overall gains are +5.7, +10.2, and +13.2[^130]. R-Zero raises Qwen3-4B-Base's math average from 42.57 to 49.93, versus 46.42 for Absolute Zero[^131]. When Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct is trained on DAPO-17k, SvS's AIME24 and AIME25 Pass@32 are 18.3% and 22.8% higher than those of standard RLVR[^129]. Compared with extended RLVR at the same exposure, SvS's average Pass@32 is 53.1, versus 46.3 for the control[^129]. Static augmentation and QbQ data without rounds stall at a pass@1 of 12.5% and 14.5% respectively, even when the data volume is greatly increased[^39]. Under the same data budget, curriculum-style QbQ improves Qwen2.5-Math-7B from 5.62% to 16.46%[^39].

**Limitations and collapse risk.** The reported risks fall into three classes: label degradation, reward hacking, and pseudo-diversity (our assessment). On label degradation, with GPT-4o as the reference, R-Zero's pseudo-label accuracy drops from 79.0% to 63.0% by the third round[^131]. After multiple rounds, the performance of models of every scale keeps declining, and the larger the model, the later the decline sets in[^131]. There is no single label-accuracy threshold for degradation: the 0.6B model already starts to degrade at 70.6%, while the 4B model tolerates 48.8%[^131]. The authors speculate that the more fundamental cause is model collapse caused by training only on self-synthesized data[^131]. On reward hacking, if only solvable variants are rewarded, SvS's policy embeds hints in the question statement and even writes the answer into it directly[^129]. The authors therefore let only variants whose policy accuracy falls within [1/8, 5/8] receive a positive reward[^129]. Absolute Zero finds that global variables in self-posed programs may leak the answer, but performance drops after they are removed, so they are ultimately kept[^130]. On pseudo-diversity, in a human audit of 100 candidate questions from each method, the near-duplicate rates with respect to the seeds are 18% and 21% for R-Zero and CoT-Self-Instruct, versus 4% for Learning to Pose Problems[^9].

The weaker the external signal, the smaller the gain (our inference). AZR's gain on Llama3.1-8B is +3.2, lower than SimpleRL's +4.5[^130]. The fully self-evolving SwS variant, with Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct as the policy, improves the average by only 1.2%[^128]. The 7B version, which relies on external models for question posing and labeling, improves by 10.0, but the base models and initial question sets differ, so the two cannot be subtracted directly (our assessment, based on [^128]). The most likely reconciling variable is the strength of the verification signal: an executor can judge correctness exactly, majority-vote labels become distorted as difficulty rises, and teacher answers bring in external knowledge (our inference, based on [^130][^131][^39]). QbQ's questions and answers come from a stronger teacher, so strictly speaking it is not zero-data self-play (our assessment, based on [^39]).

### 5.6 Decontamination Practice and Open Recipes

**Decontamination practice.** Decontamination methods vary widely across works (our assessment). The simplest is exact string matching, which Big-Math uses to screen against the 500 test questions each of MATH and Omni-MATH[^118]. SynLogic only verified that no sample is identical to one in the test set[^125]. Matching on n-grams is more common, but window lengths vary (our count). WebInstruct removes web pages that have a 10-gram match with evaluation questions or answers[^116]. NaturalReasoning uses 13-grams and removes 0.026% of entries[^122]. rStar-Coder uses 16-grams[^134], and Nemotron-Cascade uses 9-grams[^11]. Olmo 3 and ZGCM-1 use 8-grams with a 50% overlap threshold[^10][^45]. KodCode uses an embedding cosine-similarity threshold of 0.95 and flags only 94 of 447K questions[^121]. Another family of approaches combines embedding recall with LLM-as-judge review (our assessment). WizardMath retrieves the 5 most similar training samples for each test question and has GPT-4 judge them[^114]. DeepMath-103K has Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct judge whether a candidate is the same question or a rewrite of it[^119]. OpenMathInstruct-2 uses LLM decontamination to remove about 50K of 569K newly synthesized questions, and gives examples that n-gram matching cannot detect[^88]. OpenThoughts combines Indel similarity (threshold 75%) with 13-grams and, on a manually contaminated set, misses only 12 of 3092 examples while wrongly removing 1.4% of clean samples[^4].

Question pools and corpora collected at scale naturally contain many test questions (our assessment, based on [^119][^124]). Before decontamination, DeepMath-103K's raw question pool contained 90% of the test questions of AIME24 and AMC23 and 76.6% of those of MATH500[^119]. After CodeI/O removes the original questions, 21.5% of LeetCode-O questions are still flagged as potential leaks because they share descriptions with sibling questions[^124]. The authors report similar gains on the leaked and non-leaked subsets[^124]. In the full texts we read, no description of a decontamination step was found for SwS, SvS, or R-Zero (our count, based on [^128][^129][^131]). None was found for General-Reasoner or CoT-Self-Instruct either (our count, based on [^120][^126]).

**Open recipes.** OpenThoughts runs ablations over 27 code, 21 math, and 14 science question sources, generating 31,600 examples for each strategy[^4]. Taking only 1 to 2 high-quality question sources beats mixing 8 or 16 question sources[^4]. Filtering questions by LLM-labeled difficulty or by response length beats embedding-based or fastText filtering[^4]. Fewer questions with more answers each performs about as well as, or even better than, more questions with fewer answers each, from which the authors conclude that question diversity matters little when answer diversity is high[^4]. The math domain is an exception, where 1 annotation per question works best, yet the authors still choose the latter because 16× sampling makes scaling up easier[^4]. The dataset card shows that OpenThoughts3-1.2M is built on only about 75k questions, each annotated 16 times by QwQ-32B[^58]. OpenThinker3-7B, trained on it with SFT only, reaches 53%, 51%, and 54% on AIME 2025, LiveCodeBench 06/24-01/25, and GPQA Diamond[^57]. NaturalThoughts uses NaturalReasoning as its question pool and DeepSeek-R1 as the teacher; on a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct student, its 500K examples beat the 1.2M examples of OpenThoughts3 on 3 of 4 benchmarks[^5]. It also argues that the diversity of reasoning traces matters more than the diversity of the questions themselves[^5]. rStar-Coder and OpenMathInstruct-2 reach the opposite conclusion (our assessment, based on [^134][^88]). With a 7B student and a fixed set of 37.7K questions, rStar-Coder scores 40.8, 51.1, and 54.7 on LiveCodeBench with 1, 8, and 16 solutions per question[^134]. A 580K-example dataset with more diverse questions scores 57.3 on LiveCodeBench and 16.15 on USACO 2025, whereas the 603K examples with 16 solutions per question score only 10.41 on USACO 2025[^134]. OpenThoughts' own math ablation points in the same direction as the latter two, and the disagreement is concentrated in the code and science domains (our assessment, based on [^4]). The reconciling variables are likely data scale and the evaluation distribution (our inference). Each OpenThoughts experiment has only 31,600 examples, whereas rStar-Coder's comparison is made at a scale of 580K and 603K examples (our assessment, based on [^4][^134]). OpenMathInstruct-2's gain appears in the low range where unique questions increase from 1K to 6.5K, while rStar-Coder's gap is largest on USACO (our inference, based on [^88][^134]).

Licensing likewise constrains the reuse of question sources (our assessment). OpenMathInstruct-1 switched to Mixtral for licensing reasons[^132]. Olmo 3 removes samples that are non-commercial or of unclear license, and separately synthesizes replacement data for high-performing datasets whose licenses are not permissive[^10]. The AM series data is permitted for research use only[^1][^60]. NaturalReasoning uses cc-by-nc-4.0[^61], and OpenThoughts3-1.2M uses apache-2.0[^58]. ### 5.7 Summary (our assessment)

- The main thread of question sourcing has shifted from "posing more questions" to "posing verifiable questions". Early rewriting and synthesis mostly had a strong teacher pose the questions and then write the solutions, so the new questions lacked answers that could be checked (based on [^114][^115][^123]). Question sets from 2025 onward generally use execution tests, closed-form answers, or rule-based verifiers as admission criteria (based on [^121][^119][^125]).
- Difficulty is a pass-rate interval relative to some reference model, not an intrinsic property of a question (based on [^118][^128][^129]). Whether difficulty labels transfer across model families has not yet been verified (based on [^118]). SwS and QbQ reach opposite conclusions on "how hard the questions should be", and the disagreement more likely comes from whether hard questions are used directly for training or only as seeds for rewriting (based on [^128][^39]).
- Personas, code snippets, and concept graphs, along with skill libraries and knowledge graphs in 2026 work, are the main external structures for expanding question diversity (based on [^117][^123][^17]). Whether question diversity or the number of samples per question matters more depends on data scale and the evaluation distribution (based on [^4][^134][^88]).
- Self-posed questions are most robust when an exact executor is available; when they rely on majority voting, they degrade over iterations, accompanied by reward hacking and near-duplicates (based on [^130][^131][^9]). Gains shrink markedly once external signals are removed, and the existing evidence does not support long-term self-evolution without external signals (based on [^128][^129]).
- Web-mined question pools are heavily contaminated before decontamination, while decontamination methods vary widely, from exact matching to LLM-as-judge review (based on [^119][^4][^88]). License terms determine whether a question source can be reused and have become a constraint on recipe design (based on [^10][^132][^61]).
