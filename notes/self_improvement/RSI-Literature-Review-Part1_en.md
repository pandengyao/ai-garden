## 4. Theoretical Foundations: Formal Models, Definitions and Limit Theorems

This chapter reviews the theoretical literature on recursive self-improvement (RSI): definitions, theorems that give guarantees or set ceilings, and growth models that predict an "intelligence explosion." The 41 works it draws on run from OOPS in 2002 to the impossibility theorems of 2026-10, and 30 of them were published in 2025–2026 (our count). The chapter answers two questions. First, under what conditions can self-modification move from bounded self-improvement (converging under a fixed evaluator) to recursive (compounding) self-improvement (the improver itself is improved, and the successor improver is stronger under a comparable budget)? Second, can existing theory determine whether such a transition happens, and how fast? Throughout, the chapter follows two strands: the meta level (whether the improver is modified, and what is fixed at the outermost level) and the signal source (proof, execution-based verification, environment reward, model self-evaluation or human input).

### 4.1 From the Gödel Machine and OOPS to Self-Referential Learners

The formal lineage traced in this chapter begins with two IDSIA technical reports by Schmidhuber[^173][^7]. OOPS (Optimal Ordered Problem Solver) makes the programs of already solved tasks non-writable, and candidate programs for later tasks can copy, edit and execute this frozen code[^173]. The guarantee the author gives is n-bias-optimality, where n is a small constant, for example 8: if reusing old code is faster, OOPS will discover this, and otherwise it will not be harmed by the old solutions[^173]. In experiments, OOPS found a prefix that temporarily rewrites the distribution over its own suffixes, accelerating universal search by a factor of 1000, which the author calls self-improvement and "metasearching"[^173]. But this acceleration depends on an externally given ordered task sequence; given a single task, OOPS does not by itself invent subtasks ordered from easy to hard, and it has theoretical guarantees only in resettable environments[^173].

The Gödel machine pushes the scope of what can be modified to the limit: as soon as it finds a proof that some rewrite is useful, it rewrites any part of its own code, with the utility function, the hardware and the entire initial code all written as axioms in the proof searcher[^7]. Because the code must first prove that continuing to search for other rewrites is not more useful, the self-rewrite that is executed is globally optimal[^7]. A proof of the target theorem covers all later self-modifications affected by it, so all "meta-levels" collapse into one, and the utility function is rewritten only if the rewrite is provably useful according to the current utility function[^7]. The cost also comes from proof: even with unlimited resources, the machine must forgo improvements whose effectiveness it cannot prove, and with limited resources it can exploit only improvements that are provable within its time and space constraints[^7]. Schaul later stated bluntly that past designs for self-referential systems were "not (intended to be) practical"[^44].

AERA (Nivel et al., 2013, with Schmidhuber among the coauthors), presented under the title "Bounded Recursive Self-Improvement," explicitly contrasts itself with this line of work[^5]. In AERA, the utility of an improvement is established not by expensive search and formal proof but by observing its effect on the performance of the system[^5]. The system continually adds and removes very small parts, and what ultimately survives is "the best available" rather than "the best possible"[^5]. The authors state that this self-improvement can be sustained autonomously after the system leaves the lab, but always within the boundaries set by its designers[^5]. After about 20 hours of observation, the prototype S1 autonomously acquired the skill of real-time multimodal dialogue with humans, with no errors in its subsequent application[^5]. The authors also acknowledge that with insufficient input or an adversarial environment the rewrites would be useless, and that the scalability needed to support a general claim cannot yet be assessed[^5].

In 2022, Irie et al., as well as Kirsch and Schmidhuber, applied the self-referential idea to modern neural networks[^146][^70]. SRWM (self-referential weight matrix, ICML 2022) revisits Schmidhuber's self-referential weight matrix of the '90s with modern techniques and treats the weight matrix as the program of the network: only the initial weights are trained by gradient descent, and at run time all weights can be modified by the network itself; the authors say that in principle it can achieve "meta-meta-learning" in the sense of recursive self-improvement[^146]. In a multi-task setting with six ProcGen environments and 300M steps, SRWM's normalized test score is 20.0, slightly above DeltaNet's 18.6, while a control version without self-modification scores 15.3[^146]. The authors note that their first set of experiments did not specifically measure the capacity for self-improvement, and that there is no guarantee that self-modification preserves the original goal[^146]. FME (fitness monotonic execution) goes further and eliminates the explicit meta-optimizer: every change to the model is a self-modification, and the better a solution performs, the more often it is executed[^70]. On a two-armed bandit, a solution that always picks the high-payoff arm emerges after about 40 self-modifications and selections[^70]. On Cartpole, the self-modifications improve not only the policy but also future improvements, and hill climbing with tuned noise cannot reach the same rate of improvement[^70]. The authors also admit that the empirical evaluation is still brief[^70].

This lineage supplies the template for almost all later RSI mechanisms: accepted modifications are retained, and a fixed outermost layer adjudicates, for example the axioms and utility function of the Gödel machine, the task order of OOPS, the designer-set boundaries of AERA, the outer gradient descent of SRWM and the selection rule of FME (our assessment, based on [^7][^173][^5]). The stronger the adjudicating signal, the stronger the guarantee and the higher the cost of implementation: proof gives global optimality but is hard to implement, while observation and selection are easy to implement but can retain only local improvements (our assessment, based on [^7][^5]). As for compounding, the evidence from this period is weak: the 1000-fold speedup of OOPS comes from an external task sequence and is a one-off, SRWM did not specifically measure self-improvement, and only FME, on a toy task, observed "improving future improvements" (our assessment, based on [^173][^146][^70]).

### 4.2 Definitions and Taxonomies: From "Better at Improving Itself" to Structural and Effective Recursion

The core of the early definitions is "becoming better at improving itself." Yampolskiy (2015) requires each new generation of software to be better than the original algorithm at optimizing future versions; linear scaling from merely adding resources does not count, because the system does not become better at improving itself[^126]. He also gives a convergence argument: an optimizing compiler applied to itself might improve by 20% the first time and by 3% the second, and after a few rounds converge to a fixed point of zero improvement[^126]. He further relays Mahoney's proof (the algorithmic complexity of the program at the nth iteration is no greater than O(log n)) and acknowledges that RSI was then still a purely theoretical concept[^126]. Wang (2018) formalizes a class of RSI systems as a sequence of programs: each program generates the next, and a candidate replaces the current program only if it is better according to a score function[^106]. Simulations show a logarithmic relation between running time and the number of programs, but the score function in them is precomputed, which takes longer than direct enumeration[^106]. Schaul (2024) gives a broader definition: if outputs affect future inputs and performance improves, that is self-improvement; it is recursive when the input and output spaces are compatible, and when that space is language it is called "Socratic learning"[^44].

Definitional work in 2026 splits "recursion" more finely (our assessment). Duan et al. require experience and feedback to be turned into persistent changes to the system itself (model parameters, harness or improvement policies), such that these changes can affect the generation, evaluation, selection and consolidation of subsequent self-improvements[^168]. Their autonomy levels run from executing prescribed improvements (L1) to improving the mechanisms that govern subsequent improvement (L5)[^168]. They point out that improved task performance does not by itself show that the improvement mechanism has been revised, retained and reused, and they distinguish "structural recursion" (a revised improvement mechanism governs a later round) from "effective recursion" (that mechanism produces stronger successors under comparable budgets and independent evaluation)[^168]. Lu subsequently pointed out that both kinds of recursion can occur within a single verification class[^236]. Tang et al.'s Generalized Agent Iteration (GAI) unifies iterative policy improvement (GPI) and RSI as a single paradigm and uses two "dials" to distinguish instances: whether the improvement mechanism belongs to the agent draws the line between GPI and RSI, and whether the evaluation standard is anchored outside the agent determines whether the system is anchored, goal-drifting or fully self-referential[^235]. Koch distinguishes four regimes by the depth of the modification target, the deepest being revision of the evaluation criterion, but holds that current systems do not yet support the conclusion that they "can endogenously revise their evaluation criteria"[^233].

Other work sets thresholds of varying strictness. The survey by Zhou et al. requires only that the system modify itself, retain the changes, and use the updated system to carry out subsequent tasks and further self-modifications[^124]. Zong et al., by contrast, require that the improvement procedure itself also be improved, and use this to distinguish recursive from iterative self-improvement[^119]. The Physical RSI framework for robots reserves L5 for independently verified recursive gain and stresses that improved task performance does not prove that the improvement procedure has become more effective[^65]. A survey of self-evolving coding agents likewise points out that passing tests does not mean the agent has become better; evidence is also needed that the retained changes benefit later decisions[^216]. Usage is broadest on the growth-model side: Ord applies the term RSI to the whole range of cases, from human-led to entirely AI-run, from rewriting itself to building a new system, and from intelligence "exploding" to quickly fizzling out[^12]. Cunningham et al. argue that concepts such as self-sustaining acceleration and intelligence explosion are often conflated with the term "RSI," and therefore turn instead to deriving the conditions under which self-sustaining acceleration arises[^35]. Burtsev, for his part, regards RSI as a property of an AI–R&D system rather than of an isolated agent[^75].

Synthesizing these definitions yields three progressively stricter conditions: the modification is retained and reused in later tasks; the modification reaches the improvement mechanism itself (structural recursion); and the successor improver is genuinely stronger under comparable budgets and independent evaluation (effective recursion) (our assessment, based on [^124][^168][^235]). The definitions of Duan et al. and Zong et al. explicitly require the first two, and Tang et al. draw the line by whether the improver belongs to the agent, which has a similar meaning; it is the third condition that is the key to distinguishing bounded from compounding improvement, and it is precisely the one that is hardest to guarantee in theory and least often measured in experiments (our assessment, based on [^168][^119][^235]). Another point of divergence is the system boundary: if the boundary is drawn around a single agent, human-designed loops and external verifiers often fall outside it; if it is drawn around the AI–R&D system, human researchers are also part of the improver, so "recursion" holds more easily but is harder to attribute to AI (our assessment).

### 4.3 Limits and Impossibility Theorems: What Is Fixed and What Is Ruled Out

Most of the theoretical results from 2025–2026 do not prove that RSI is feasible; instead, they answer how far self-modification can go at most when certain parts are held fixed (our assessment). Table 4-2 summarizes these results by "what is fixed".

Moser et al. ask whether automated "gates" such as reward models or automated reviewers can reliably decide whether to accept a self-update[^147]. They model agents as programs, with "improvement" defined by a semantic score that need not be computable, while the gate must be a terminating, computable procedure[^147]. The main theorem states that, in the fully general setting, no terminating program can be simultaneously reliable (never accepting a non-improvement) and non-conservative (never missing an available improvement); self-referential constructions can force any gate or searcher to fail, and the same holds for randomized gates with bounded error[^147]. The authors stress that this obstruction is structural rather than statistical: the proposer can exploit the evaluator as a predictable part of the environment[^147]. In the decidable regime of bounded evaluation and a finite proposal budget, exhaustive simulation can restore the guarantees, but a chain of accepted updates may still raise the bounded score in every round while the true score falls[^147]. The authors also note that these are worst-case results and do not imply that self-improvement will not occur[^147].

Two papers by Lu give analogous limits for the case in which the improver itself can also be modified[^169][^236]. In a relative-computability model, the solver, evaluator and improver are all mutable, yet every total decision procedure that effective self-revision produces over resource A still falls within the class C(A) of problems Turing-reducible to A[^169]. A learned solver can stop relying on the "teacher" on every input and yet remain in the same computability layer[^169]. Capability internalization amounts to a strict ascent only when the successor preserves the predecessor's resource and makes tasks outside the predecessor's layer available, and the theory offers neither improvement rates nor a general procedure for identifying beneficial revisions[^169].

Lu's other paper starts from verification complexity and first points out that a performance score cannot distinguish whether a system searched longer, received additional support or changed its proposal and verification mechanisms[^236]. It distinguishes "native reach" (inputs that have an accepted solution under default support) from the "closure frontier" (inputs that have an accepted solution under any support the interface already admits)[^236]. Making a few more attempts changes neither set; making optional feedback routine can enlarge native reach within the old frontier, while admitting previously forbidden interaction transcripts can enlarge the frontier itself[^236]. Its conclusion for RSI is that, under a common sound interpreter and a fixed verification protocol, uniformly bounded self-modification stays within the same verification class, and escape requires a failure of semantic authority or of exposure inclusion[^236]. It also constructs a family of examples in which the ratio of search success rates can grow without bound while the accepted languages remain unchanged[^236]. The author notes that these results allow empirical gains within the frontier, do not cover unbounded processes and have not been validated on large deployed systems[^236].

The "stationarity dichotomy" of Bobadilla-Suarez et al. gives an auditable version: when the reachable set of edits stays fixed, iterative self-modification runs into strict diminishing returns, and only an expansion of that set allows escape[^2]. Rewriting harness components such as tools, verifiers and task decomposition can expand the reachable range without touching the weights, so checking only whether the weights are frozen audits the wrong object[^2]. Empirically, best-of-k orchestration can only reach the ceiling of the best worker, and a majority of 30 same-family workers fail together on 23/55 (42%) of the tasks[^2]. On SWE-bench, per-round improvement tends toward zero, and code churn across 401 production sessions decays geometrically, with the same shape as a pre-AI human baseline[^2]. The authors acknowledge that the data cannot distinguish whether the cause is a genuinely fixed reachable class or the ordinary economics of editing[^2].

Cai et al. study harness self-evolution in which the base model is fixed and only prompts, code, tools and orchestration change, and prove that the performance ceiling of reliable self-evolution is determined by the costs of verification and evaluation; the closer the reward gets to its upper bound, the higher the evaluation cost that reliably validating smaller gains may require[^239]. In the DS-1000 example, a substantial share of candidates met the audit target, yet in the end only two passed validation and were adopted; the authors also caution that a ceiling imposed by the validation rule does not mean that further improvement is impossible[^239].

Tang et al. point out that the classical policy-iteration guarantees require the improvement step to be cheap and monotone, and both the objective and the evaluation instrument to lie outside the agent[^235]. Once the evaluator becomes a component of the agent, the object being evaluated and the instrument of evaluation coincide, and a self-consistent system may report improvement while having moved the reference standard[^235]. The empirical evidence they cite shows that 58.33% of settings already improve on the first valid attempt, but 78.26% of searches that continue past the best score end with a lower score[^235].

Another group of results gives positive but bounded guarantees (our assessment). The guarantees that Fu et al. give for self-rewarding language models show that a single update step depends heavily on the quality of the initial model, that under full iteration performance improves at a rate of Õ(1/√n), and that the dependence on the initial model decays exponentially with the number of iterations, although the analysis is limited to tractable settings such as linear models[^234]. Liu et al. (ICML 2026) model each round of self-improvement as maximum-likelihood fine-tuning on self-generated data filtered by an external verifier and reveal a feedback loop: a better model gets more data accepted in each round, which both sustains continued improvement and explains eventual saturation[^238]. In the experiments, Pass@1 often improves more slowly after t=2 and levels off clearly below 1, and when tasks are too easy or too hard relative to the model, improvement is limited or performance even degrades[^238]. Qiu et al. interpret debate, bootstrap and internal coherence maximization all as "coherence optimization" (debate is an exact instance, and the other two are closely related to it), prove that it is equivalent to description-length regularization, and give the condition under which self-improvement succeeds: the pretrained prior closely approximates the data-generating distribution[^237]. They run in-context learning on GSM8K with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct: at γ=0.25, mean training accuracy rises from 24.86% to 35.58%, with most of the gain occurring within about the first 20 rounds, after which it saturates; moreover, the results are in-sample[^237].

An empirical survey gives an ordering consistent with these theorems. Chen et al. review 1,250 arXiv papers from 2024–2026 and arrange signals into a verification hierarchy, from formal verifiers (strongest) to intrinsic self-evaluation (weakest)[^62]. They observe that the strength of demonstrated self-improvement tracks this hierarchy and that typical failures all stem from violations of it, and they state that this is a qualitative pattern rather than a measured law[^62]. The positive guarantees in this section fit the same pattern: when the signal comes from the model itself, the conclusions all depend on the quality of the initial model or the pretrained prior, and with an external verifier, what is proven is likewise improvement that saturates (our assessment, based on [^234][^237][^238]).

These results differ in form but point to the same "conservation" structure: as long as the verifier, oracle, reachable class of edits or validation rule stays fixed, self-modification cannot take the system certifiably beyond that class (our assessment, based on [^147][^169][^236]). "Modifying the improver" is not in itself enough to produce compounding: Lu's model allows the improver to be revised, and the conclusion is still that the system stays within C(A) (our assessment, based on [^169]). What can raise the ceiling is external expansion that the system itself cannot certify: new oracle access, new support, harnesses that extend the reachable range, and larger evaluation budgets (our assessment, based on [^236][^2][^239]). Yet the fixed evaluator is precisely where the guarantees come from; if it too is handed over to the system for modification, the ceiling and the guarantees disappear together, and GAI's "object and instrument coincide" and Moser et al.'s "proposer exploits the evaluator" describe the same phenomenon (our assessment, based on [^235][^147]). Most of these theorems are worst-case or class-membership results that say nothing about the speed of improvement within a class; they can neither rule out rapid progress in practice nor prove that progress will sustain itself (our assessment).

### 4.4 Growth Dynamics and Explosion Conditions

The previous section dealt with "whether it can be certified"; growth models deal with "how fast it will go". As early as 2012, Hutter distinguished a speed explosion from an intelligence explosion: a society of agents simulated on computers whose compute grows hyperbolically must undergo a speed explosion in physical time, but not necessarily an intelligence explosion[^52]. He also pointed out that the upper bound on intelligence in universal intelligence theory is "not necessarily" truly binding, and that a monotone transformation of the intelligence measure such as 1/(1−I) suffices to create a mathematical singularity[^52]. Most later models treat AI as R&D labor and describe progress with a scalar A; what they discuss is recursion at the level of the AI–R&D system rather than of a single agent (our assessment).

As for empirical inputs, Ho et al., drawing on evaluations of more than 200 language models from 2012–2023, estimate that the compute needed to reach a fixed level of performance halves about every 8 months (95% confidence interval about 5 to 14 months), but a Shapley analysis attributes 60–95% of performance gains to compute scaling, with algorithms accounting for only 5–40%[^95]. MacAskill and Moorhouse call the core mechanism the "software feedback loop" and stress that it is not inevitable, because most new technologies are at most a one-off multiplier on researcher productivity; they also introduce two diminishing-returns effects, "fishing out" and "stepping on toes"[^86]. By the estimates they cite, the chance that the software feedback loop produces acceleration is about fifty-fifty[^86]. They estimate that a century of progress in a decade would require research input to grow more than 600-fold over that decade, whereas AI's effective cognitive labor is currently growing more than twentyfold per year[^86].

Whitfill and Wu prove that an intelligence explosion has two necessary conditions: compute and cognitive labor must be elastically substitutable, and self-improvement must outpace the rate at which new ideas become harder to find[^105]. If the elasticity of substitution σ<1, a purely software-driven explosion occurs only when φ>1, which the authors consider quite implausible[^105]. Using panel data from OpenAI, DeepMind, Anthropic and DeepSeek for 2014–2024, they estimate σ = 2.58 under the "compute CES" specification and σ = −.10 under the "frontier experiments" specification, an estimate statistically indistinguishable from zero[^105]. They therefore argue that whether a purely software-driven explosion is feasible is highly sensitive to the structure of the research production function[^105].

Ord distinguishes superexponential growth from a singularity[^12]. In differential-equation models, superexponential growth only requires Ȧ to grow superlinearly in A, whereas a singularity requires Ȧ to grow slightly faster than every function of the form A·log(A)·log(log(A))…[^12]. For example, Ȧ = A·log(A) gives double-exponential growth but no singularity[^12]. His key variable is generation time, the time needed to go once around the feedback loop[^12]. Under time-embedded difference equations, a singularity occurs if and only if generation time satisfies the "Zeno condition" (infinitely many steps can be completed in finite time) and the increments satisfy the "unboundedness condition"; with a fixed generation time, no vertical asymptote appears however fast the improvements grow[^12]. Under this more realistic model, superexponential but non-singular growth looks more likely[^12]. The conclusion for measurement is negative: the singularity condition is a global property of the relationship between Ȧ and A, not a local property such as "elasticity > 1", so without strong assumptions about the functional form, local measurements can neither confirm nor rule out an explosion[^12]. He argues that requiring frontier labs to report the generation times of pretraining and RLVR post-training may be good policy, and stresses that the greater difficulty of singular growth does not mean that RSI is safe[^12].

Burtsev's delayed-feedback model comprises baseline research productivity, a recursive gain a, an end-to-end delay τ from a capability gain to the resulting rise in research productivity, the fraction χ of the gain that makes it through the development pipeline (called operational closure) and the frontier hardness σ[^75]. This yields the recursive reproduction number R_AI = χa/σ; when it exceeds 1, incremental improvements are amplified across development cycles[^75]. The critical boundary does not depend on baseline research throughput, so rapid capability growth and recursive self-amplification are two different things, and a system may already be supercritical before acceleration becomes visible[^75]. Frontier hardening, in turn, may pull a supercritical system back into the subcritical regime, making the amplification only temporary[^75]. In an example with the delay fixed at τ = 0.5 years, the rapid-transition scenario compresses the interval from AGI to ASI from 72 years to less than half a year[^75]. The author notes that a, χ and frontier hardness cannot be identified from existing evidence and can only serve as scenario parameters, and that crossing R_AI = 1 is only a local amplification condition[^75].

Cunningham et al. (whose authors include Whitfill and Wu) define "self-sustaining acceleration" as AI being sufficient to accelerate capability progress without any growth in exogenous inputs, and separately define "intelligence explosion" as capabilities tending to infinity in finite time[^35]. They draw growth models as directed graphs and prove that the net acceleration of capabilities depends on the product of the elasticities along each feedback loop[^35]. In the baseline model, self-sustaining acceleration occurs when ε_{A,A} > 1, that is, when a 1% increase in the stock of ideas raises the discovery of new ideas by more than 1%, whereas in traditional industries this elasticity is consistently well below 1[^35]. Calibrated with the Epoch Capabilities Index, the condition requires each one-unit increase in capability to raise AI R&D productivity by at least 15%[^35]. A rough calculation based on engineer uplift puts this return at about 9% since the launch of coding agents, below the threshold, though it has been rising recently[^35]. And even this 9% is obtained by assuming that a 4x uplift, which the authors consider "likely an overestimate", holds true[^35]. Burtsev also notes that Cunningham et al. independently arrived at a unit-threshold condition closely related to R_AI[^75].

Chan et al. compile the evidence for a "software-driven intelligence explosion", whose mechanism is that AI enlarges the effective R&D workforce, and this workforce in turn builds better AI[^210]. The key quantity is the returns to research effort r; when r > 1, the growth of the R&D workforce outpaces diminishing returns and brings acceleration[^210]. They cite central estimates by Ho and Whitfill for three AI subfields, with r between 1.2 and 1.9; averaging gives λ = 1.40 and β = 1.01, in which case each doubling takes about 76% as long as the previous one[^210]. If r holds and there are no other bottlenecks, the pace of AI progress would rise tenfold within about 1.5 years, by which point a year of progress at today's pace would take only about five weeks[^210]. The automation metric they cite shows that the share of R&D work completed autonomously with only high-level supervision rose from 1% to 26% between March and August 2026[^210]. Chan et al. argue that the productivity gains from R&D automation have not yet reached the threshold for triggering an intelligence explosion, but that the gains from new systems are likely approaching it[^210]. They also list four frictions: diminishing returns, compute and data, tasks that are hard to automate, and time-consuming R&D processes[^210]. Limited data indicate that if the compute needed for experiments grows in proportion to the scale of frontier training, a software-driven explosion would be impossible, and the estimates of r rely on imperfect proxy variables[^210].

Li et al. take the feedback model in Chan et al.'s working paper as a starting point and randomize it into a "Good process": improvements arrive at a rate proportional to A^ε, each multiplying A by (1+H), with H independent and identically distributed and strictly positive[^130]. Randomness does not change the explosion threshold but turns the explosion date into a distribution, and even when the parameters are known, the relative uncertainty about the remaining time does not vanish as the explosion approaches[^130]. In the delayed-feedback models they specify, a fixed deployment delay can prevent the explosion[^130]. The model excludes compute and data frictions, and the authors also state that the numerical examples are not forecasts[^130]. Harris has AIs design their descendants on a design tree while humans control fitness: without additional assumptions, fitness need not rise, and only under the assumptions of bounded fitness and η-locking does it concentrate on the maximum reachable value[^53]. If deceiving the evaluators can raise fitness further, evolution will select for both capability and deception[^53]. Researchers' views are similarly divided: of the 25 researchers interviewed by Field et al., 20 list automated AI research as one of the most severe and most urgent AI risks, while academic researchers are more skeptical of explosive growth[^189].

Self-sustaining acceleration is a local condition that can hold for a time and then fail, whereas a singularity requires a global condition such as generation time rapidly approaching zero; even after entering the supercritical regime, therefore, the more likely form is superexponential but non-singular growth or temporary amplification (our assessment, based on [^12][^75]). Chan et al.'s r describes counterfactual dynamics after full automation, while Cunningham et al. measure the current R&D return per unit of capability; the two do not contradict each other, and both hold that the threshold has not yet been crossed (our assessment, based on [^210][^35]). Growth models treat capability as a scalar that can keep rising, whereas the theorems of the previous section show that certifiable capability under a fixed verifier is bounded; a bridge between the two bodies of literature is still missing (our assessment). Generation time and operational closure may be the entry point: an improvement counts only once it has been verified, integrated and passed on to a successor system, and verification cost is precisely the source of the ceiling on reliable self-evolution (our inference, based on [^12][^75][^239]).

### 4.5 Positions: Open-Endedness, Metacognition and Socratic Learning

Hughes et al. argue that open-ended, continually self-improving AI remains hard to achieve, but that the ingredients needed for open-endedness relative to a human observer are already in place, and that open-endedness is a necessary property of any superhuman intelligence (ASI)[^117]. Their definition is relative to an observer: a sequence of artifacts is open-ended only if it is both novel and learnable, so a foundation model trained on fixed data is not open-ended to an observer who can model its training set[^117]. They point out that self-improvement requires a scalable mechanism for evaluating one's own performance, identifying what can be improved and adjusting the learning process, and that the novelty of the AdA agent already begins to plateau after about 1 month of training[^117].

Schaul's "Socratic learning" concerns pure recursive self-improvement within a closed system: as long as the feedback is sufficiently informative and aligned with the goal, experience coverage is broad enough, and capacity and resources suffice, an agent can master any target capability, with the ceiling on improvement limited only by the resources invested[^44]. But the feedback cannot correct itself, preventing drift and collapse may be very difficult, and no current LLM training paradigm has a feedback mechanism adequate to support Socratic learning[^44]. He proposes "language games" with scalar scoring to provide both data generation and feedback, and envisions agents going on to generate games themselves, but this meta-game lacks a feedback mechanism as explicit as that of the inner games[^44]. He also argues that fully self-referential agents have the highest ceiling, because unfreezing fixed structures can only raise the asymptotic upper bound[^44].

Liu and van der Schaar contend that genuine self-improvement requires "intrinsic metacognitive learning", that is, an agent's ability to actively evaluate, reflect on and adapt its own learning process[^19]. They argue that existing systems rely mainly on fixed, human-designed extrinsic loops, which encode stationarity assumptions about the learning process and whose effectiveness declines once an agent's generative ability exceeds its self-evaluation ability[^19]. The authors also acknowledge that this route raises the problem of scalable and safe oversight[^19]. Behrouz et al.'s Nested Learning proposes a sequence model that modifies itself by learning its own update algorithm, but the authors note that catastrophic forgetting has not been solved in a general sense[^240].

Yang's PhD thesis characterizes "continual self-improvement" by three properties: writing new knowledge into parameters without catastrophic forgetting, generating one's own training signals and autonomously deciding the learning algorithm[^244]. For the third, the post-training recipe that execution-guided search found within ten rounds outperforms the GRPO baseline on a 1.5B math model (69.4% vs. 48.0%), and the pretraining recipe it found takes 19.7 minutes, versus 35.9 minutes for the nanoGPT baseline[^244]. The author acknowledges that these ideas were not tested for generalizability across scales, and that reinforcement learning with execution rewards raises the average reward rather than the maximum reward[^244]. Two peripheral works show that the implementation and verification capabilities of automated research are still weak. Zhu et al. argue that the fundamental bottleneck of AI scientists lies in the ability to execute the required verification procedures, citing the example that Claude 3.5 Sonnet scores only 1.8% on PaperBench[^243]. Xie et al.'s survey reports that o1-preview's medal rate on MLE-Bench is only 16.90%[^21].

The common direction of these positions is to turn the outermost layer of the improver from a loop fixed by humans into a learnable part inside the system (our assessment, based on [^19][^44][^244]). But all of them leave "who evaluates" as an open question, and once the evaluation standard is internalized, both the classical guarantees and faithfulness are lost (our assessment, based on [^235][^147]). What they argue for, therefore, is what recursive RSI requires, not that these requirements have been met, and the accompanying empirical results all remain within the range of bounded improvement (our assessment, based on [^117][^244][^70]).

### 4.6 Summary (our assessment)

- All self-improvement in this chapter's literature that can be formally guaranteed is bounded: from the axioms of the Gödel machine to the validation rule of harness evolution, the guarantees all come from a fixed outermost evaluator, which also sets the ceiling (based on [^7][^239][^147]).
- "Modifying the improver" is not the same as compounding. Even when the improver can be revised, effective, uniformly bounded self-modification still stays within the original computability layer or verification class; what can raise the ceiling is external expansion that the system cannot certify on its own (based on [^169][^236][^2]).
- Definitions have converged on three tiers, "retained, touches the improvement mechanism, stronger under comparable budgets and independent evaluation", but the third tier (effective recursion) has not been directly measured for any system in this chapter's literature; the closest is FME's observation on toy tasks (based on [^168][^65][^70]).
- Growth models distinguish self-sustaining acceleration, superexponential growth and finite-time singularity. A singularity requires generation time to approach zero rapidly; it is the strongest claim and the one with the least evidence (based on [^12][^75][^130]).
- On whether the acceleration threshold has been crossed, existing calibrations consistently give the verdict "not yet, but getting closer", while the parameter estimates depend on proxy variables and on how the production function is specified, so they should not be taken as forecasts (based on [^35][^210][^105]).
- Position papers advocate internalizing metacognition, judges and learning algorithms, yet the internalization of evaluation standards is precisely where the theoretical guarantees fail; until then, "recursion" is more likely to appear in AI–R&D systems that include humans and external verification than in a single autonomous agent (based on [^235][^19][^75]).
- For infrastructure readers, there are three testable practices: report generation times, compare successive improvement procedures under matched initial conditions and budgets, and audit the reachable set of edits rather than only the weights (based on [^12][^65][^2]).

## 5. Weight-Level Self-Improvement (Part I): From Bootstrapping to Self-Rewarding (2017–2024)

This chapter covers weight-level self-improvement: a model generates data, which, after being filtered or scored by some signal, are used to update the weights of the same model (or its successor), and the process can be repeated over multiple rounds. Chronologically it runs from Expert Iteration and AlphaZero in 2017 to WebRL at the end of 2024, 30 papers in all (our count). Four questions run through the chapter: where the filtering signal comes from (rule and execution checks, gold-standard answers, learned reward models, model self-evaluation or humans); whether the improver itself (search expert, verifier, judge) is also improved; how many rounds a loop can run before it saturates or reverses; and which parts of the outermost layer always stay fixed. The overall judgment is that the evidence from this period strongly supports "bounded self-improvement", that is, stable gains over one to four rounds under the constraint of external anchors, while support for "recursive (compounding) self-improvement" comes essentially only from game environments with closed rules and rewards that the policy cannot exploit (our assessment).

### 5.1 Precursors of Search–Distillation Self-Play: ExIt and AlphaZero

Expert Iteration (ExIt) splits sequential decision-making into planning and generalization: tree search plans new policies, a deep network generalizes these plans, and the network policy then guides the search, raising the strength of new plans[^187]. In each iteration, positions are generated by self-play of the previous-generation apprentice network, a new apprentice is trained to imitate the search expert, and the new apprentice is then used to update the expert, i.e., "expert improvement"[^187]. Unlike methods that can only imitate a fixed initial expert, ExIt can build stronger experts for itself, so an expert is available throughout training[^187]. This is the earliest and cleanest meta-level instance in this chapter: the improver that produces the training targets (the search expert) grows stronger together with what it improves (our assessment).

Trained from scratch, ExIt beat MoHex 1.0, the most recent publicly released Olympiad champion program, at the board game Hex[^187]. With 10,000 search iterations, ExIt had a win rate of 75.3% against MoHex with the same number of iterations and 59.3% against MoHex with 100,000 iterations[^187]. The policy improved steadily in every iteration, later apprentices were clearly stronger than earlier experts[^187], and the training curves still showed no convergence when the results were reported[^187]. The authors also give an upper bound on the per-iteration gain: the gap between the apprentice and the improved expert sets the upper bound on the new apprentice's performance in each iteration[^187]. In implementation, ExIt redoes imitation learning from scratch at each step and discards the old dataset, whereas the online version instead aggregates all data collected so far[^187]; the network weights are reinitialized in each iteration and then trained on the new data[^187]. What accumulates across iterations is data and expert strength, not one and the same set of parameters (our assessment).

The ExIt authors regard AlphaGo Zero as an independently developed version of ExIt[^187]. AlphaZero, in turn, generalizes the approach of AlphaGo Zero into a single algorithm[^120]. Starting from random play and given no domain knowledge beyond the rules, it reached superhuman level in chess, shogi and Go within 24 hours, defeating a world-champion program in each[^120]. The training signal is the game outcome as determined by the rules[^120], and the updated parameters are used directly for subsequent self-play[^120]. It drops AlphaGo Zero's best-player gating (a new player had to beat the best player by a 55% margin to replace it) and maintains a single, continually updated network[^120]. AlphaZero surpassed Stockfish after 4 hours of training (300k steps), Elmo after less than 2 hours (110k steps) and AlphaGo Lee after 8 hours (165k steps)[^120]. The cost was 5,000 first-generation TPUs generating self-play games and 64 second-generation TPUs training the network[^120], and a separate instance was trained for each game[^120].

These two works established the template that later LLM self-training would borrow again and again: use search (or "sampling plus filtering") as the policy-improvement operator to produce targets better than the current policy, then distill them back into the network (our assessment). They also set out the conditions under which the template can keep compounding: the reward is given by the rules at the end of the game and cannot be "persuaded" by the policy; the training problems arise from self-play and automatically become harder as the opponent grows stronger (our assessment). Removing best-player gating means that every parameter update feeds directly into the next round of data generation, a practice that is safe only when the reward cannot be exploited by the policy (our inference). Most of the saturation and reversals in the LLM loops discussed later can be traced to one of these two conditions failing to hold (our inference).

### 5.2 Bootstrapping on Verifiable Self-Generated Data: From STaR to Qwen2.5-Math

STaR lists ExIt as a source of inspiration[^209]. In effect, it replaces the "search expert" with "sampling with answer checking" (our assessment). Its loop runs as follows: generate rationales for a large number of problems; for wrong answers, regenerate with the correct answer given as a hint (rationalization); fine-tune on all rationales that end in a correct answer; repeat[^209]. Each time a new batch of data is collected, training restarts from the original pretrained model to avoid overfitting[^209]. On GPT-J (6B), STaR performs comparably on CommonsenseQA to a fine-tuned model 30 times larger (72.5% vs. 73.0%)[^209]. On arithmetic, accuracy reaches 89.5% after 16 iterations[^209]. On GSM8K, direct fine-tuning scores 5.8 and STaR with rationalization 10.7[^209]; these results were obtained after 36 iterations without rationalization plus 10 iterations with rationalization[^209]. Its limitations foreshadow the limits of the answer-filtering methods that followed: the first round requires better-than-chance few-shot performance, and GPT-2 could not bootstrap even on arithmetic[^209]; data biases that are "useful" for solving problems get amplified[^209]; and without rationalization the loop eventually stops solving new problems, because problems it gets wrong provide no training signal[^209]. Although STaR runs many iterations, what it does in substance is extract, from a fixed problem set, the part of the capability that answer checking can cover (our assessment).

Inspired by AlphaZero, Haluptzok et al. have a model synthesize its own programming puzzles and solutions, filtered by a Python interpreter[^84], and fine-tuning on its own verified data more than doubles test accuracy[^84]. Pass@100 for Neo-2.7B after fine-tuning for 1 epoch is 7.5% for the baseline, 20.6% with self-generated and verified data, 21.5% with Codex-generated unverified data and 38.2% with Codex-generated and verified data[^84]. The authors attribute a considerable part of the gain to interpreter filtering[^84]; the gain from a second round was already small, and because of the cost they stopped after two rounds[^84]. The gain depends mainly on the filtering signal and the strength of the generator; whether the data are generated by the model "itself" is not critical (our assessment).

LMSI switches the signal to self-consistency: it samples multiple chains of thought for unlabeled questions, selects high-confidence paths by majority vote and then fine-tunes on them[^109]. PaLM-540B improves from 74.4% to 82.1% on GSM8K[^109], with gains of 1.1% to 7.7% across six datasets, all without ground-truth labels at any point[^109]. After self-improvement, however, the optimal sampling temperature is higher than the original 0.7, which the authors attribute to reduced output entropy[^109]. As far as we have read, the paper reports only one round of self-training; the reduced entropy also suggests that majority-vote self-labeling mainly sharpens the existing distribution (our assessment).

ReST casts "sample–filter–fine-tune" as offline RL with a Grow/Improve structure: Grow generates data from the current policy, and Improve fine-tunes on the filtered data[^177]. Each subsequent Improve step significantly raises the average reward[^177], and a second Grow step adds another 5.3 points on IWSLT 2014 De-En[^177]. But the reward model is only a proxy for human preferences: by human evaluation a single Grow step is best, even though the reward keeps rising with more Grow steps[^177]; the authors also warn that repeated Grow steps increase the risk of overfitting to the reward model[^177]. ReST-EM builds on ReST with modifications[^133]; its procedure is "sample, filter by binary feedback, fine-tune, repeat a few times"[^133], and each round fine-tunes from the base pretrained model to reduce drift[^133]. PaLM 2-L's test accuracy improves by 6.34% on MATH and 6.4% on APPS[^133], significantly more than fine-tuning on human data alone[^133]. But the test gain on MATH shrinks after the first round, and APPS already regresses in the second round[^133]. Training-set performance rises linearly with the number of rounds while test-set performance does not[^133]; the authors argue that returns diminish beyond a few rounds, suggesting possible overfitting on a small number of training problems[^133]; the method also needs a manually collected problem set and a reward function that is hand-designed or learned[^133].

V-STaR points out that the methods above use only correct solutions and discard the incorrect ones, which often make up a large share[^48]; instead, it uses both correct and incorrect solutions to train a verifier with DPO[^48], with labels from ground-truth answers or test cases[^48]. Iteration makes the generator better, and the generator in turn supplies harder negatives to the verifier[^48]; at inference time the verifier picks the best of multiple candidates[^48]. Over multiple rounds both the reasoner and the verifier gradually improve, with test accuracy 4% to 17% higher than existing methods[^48], and on MBPP the verifier's gains across rounds are even larger than the generator's[^48]. This is rare evidence in this chapter of "the improver being iteratively improved and contributing more" (our assessment). However, putting the verifier into the training loop to filter data brought no substantial gain[^48].

Iterative RPO targets the problem that iterative preference optimization yields little on reasoning tasks[^257]. In each round it builds preference pairs on the rule "correct answers win, incorrect answers lose", trains with DPO plus an NLL term, and continues training from the previous round's model[^257]; the authors consider the NLL term crucial[^257]. Llama-2-70B-Chat improves from 55.6% to 81.6% on GSM8K and from 12.5% to 20.8% on MATH[^257]. On GSM8K the round-by-round scores are 73.1, 78.0, 81.1 and 81.6[^257], and the authors also state that the gains in reasoning eventually saturate[^257].

ReST-MCTS\* and AlphaLLM bring ExIt/AlphaGo-style search back into LLM self-training (our assessment). ReST-MCTS\* criticizes outcome-only filtering: a correct answer does not mean the whole reasoning chain is correct[^14]. Given oracle final answers, it estimates the probability that each step leads to the correct answer and uses it as a process reward[^14]; these rewards are used both to keep training the process reward model and to select trajectories for the policy's self-training[^14]. At the same token budget, the iteratively trained value model reaches 48.5% on MATH when used for verification, above self-consistency's 42.5%[^14]. But on the three-benchmark average for LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, the round-0 zero-shot and few-shot baselines score 24.78 and 28.99, respectively, while ReST-MCTS\* scores 29.02 after two rounds[^14]; the authors state that evaluation is zero-shot only and the few-shot baseline serves only as a reference[^14]. AlphaLLM draws on AlphaGo, combining prompt synthesis, MCTS and three critic models[^262], and its headline results are GSM8K rising from 57.8 to 92.0 and MATH from 20.7 to 51.0[^262]. But these rely on ηMCTS decoding at inference time; with greedy decoding the scores are only 73.7 and 23.6, while Llama-2-70b after SFT scores 69.3 with greedy decoding[^262]. The critic models stay static within the framework[^262]. AlphaLLM describes itself as needing no additional annotations[^262], but the authors also state that it uses final-answer annotations[^262]. A considerable part of the gain from search is not distilled into the weights, which is the same issue as the upper bound set by the apprentice–expert gap in ExIt (our assessment).

Quiet-STaR generalizes STaR in another direction: it generates "thoughts" at every token to explain the text that follows, and uses REINFORCE to raise the probability of thoughts that help prediction[^203]. After continued pretraining of Mistral 7B on web text, zero-shot GSM8K rises from 5.9% to 10.9%[^203], without task-specific fine-tuning[^203]. Here the "verifier" is the likelihood of the corpus itself, so the approach can be extended to unlabeled text (our assessment). The cost is that many tokens have to be generated before each token is produced[^203].

Qwen2.5-Math assembles these practices into an industrial pipeline and describes self-improvement as running through pretraining, post-training and inference[^214]. The previous-generation Qwen2-Math-72B-Instruct synthesized math pretraining data, forming Qwen Math Corpus v2 of more than 1 trillion tokens[^214], whereas the previous version of the corpus had about 700 billion tokens[^214]. In post-training, the reward model drives iteration of the SFT data, and the stronger SFT model is in turn used to retrain the reward model[^214]; the RL reward is jointly composed of a rule-based verifier that checks against gold-standard answers and the reward model[^214]. Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct outperforms the previous-generation 72B model, scoring 83.6 and 85.3 on MATH in the CoT and TIR settings, respectively[^214]. The meta-level updates here are real: the reward model is retrained across rounds, and each generation's model writes corpus data for the next; but every step is anchored to labeled answers, and as far as we have read there is no stage-by-stage ablation (our assessment).

The methods in this section share a common structure: the training problems (or the seeds used to generate them) and the corpora are supplied by humans, the final basis for checking (gold-standard answers, an interpreter, a majority-vote rule, text likelihood or a fixed reward model) does not change over the loop, and the model is responsible only for generating candidates (our assessment). Although V-STaR, ReST-MCTS\* and Qwen2.5-Math retrain their verifiers or reward models, their signals remain anchored to ground-truth answers or to rule-based verifiers that check against gold-standard answers (our assessment, based on [^48][^14][^214]). Under this structure, improvement with external checking is reliable, its ceiling is set by the capabilities the problem set covers, and for most methods the gains narrow markedly after two to three rounds (our assessment).

### 5.3 Self-Generated Instructions and AI Feedback: Self-Instruct, Constitutional AI and Their Successors

Alignment and instruction following have no gold-standard answers, so the signal source shifts to heuristic filtering, human-written principles and AI labelers (our assessment).

Self-Instruct starts from 175 human-written seed tasks[^197], has an off-the-shelf LM generate new instructions and instances, filters out low-quality or similar ones and adds the rest back to the task pool[^197], and then uses this data to fine-tune the very GPT-3 that generated it[^197]. After fine-tuning on 52K self-generated instructions, GPT-3 improves by 33% absolute on SuperNI[^197], and its ROUGE-L rises from 6.8 to 39.9, close to the 40.8 of InstructGPT001[^197]. A review of a sample, however, shows that only 58% of the outputs are correct and acceptable, and 54% have all fields valid[^197]. Instruction backtranslation (Humpback) replaces the hand-written filtering rules with model self-evaluation: a seed model generates instructions for web documents (self-augmentation) and then selects high-quality examples (self-curation)[^245], after which the improved model re-curates the data and is trained again, for two rounds in total[^245]. The curator does get better: on a 250-example development set labeled by the authors, M1 has a precision and recall of 0.52 and 0.44, higher than M0's 0.44 and 0.09 but far below GPT-4's 0.88 and 0.92[^245]. The 65B Humpback scores 83.71 on the Alpaca leaderboard judged by GPT-4, above Guanaco's 71.80[^245]. The key negative result is that with self-augmented data alone and no self-curation, instruction following does not improve and even declines as more data is added[^245]; self-curated data used on its own also does worse than simply expanding the seed data, and yields a large improvement only when trained jointly with the seed data[^245].

Constitutional AI (CAI) compresses human oversight into a list of principles and uses no human labels identifying harmful outputs[^27]. The supervised stage fine-tunes on responses revised after self-critique[^27]; in the RL stage the model compares two samples, and these AI preferences are used to train a preference model that serves as the reward, i.e., RLAIF[^27]. The preference model is mixed, however, as helpfulness still relies on human labels[^27]. RL-CAI is significantly more harmless than RLHF[^27], but overtraining produces Goodharting, which shows up as overly harsh or boilerplate responses[^27]. The authors envision that AI supervision could in future be used for iterated "online" training[^27]. From our reading, the paper itself does not carry out such iteration (our assessment). SELF-ALIGN reuses the Self-Instruct mechanism and introduces principles[^246], fine-tuning the base model on the responses the model generates according to those principles[^246]. Dromedary, obtained with fewer than 300 lines of human annotation (including 16 principles), surpasses Text-Davinci-003 on several benchmarks[^246]; the costs are that the Verbose Cloning step hurts multiple-choice benchmarks[^246] and that the model occasionally produces hallucinations that violate the principles[^246].

RLAIF vs. RLHF shows that AI labeling can match human labeling on summarization and dialogue tasks[^266]; in harmless dialogue, the harmless rate of RLAIF is 88% versus 76% for RLHF[^266]. In the main experiments, however, the labeler model is far larger than the policy being trained[^266]. RLAIF with a labeler of the same size is preferred over SFT 68% of the time, and the authors stress that this is not strict self-improvement, because the labeler and the initial policy are not the same checkpoint[^266]. d-RLAIF, whose feedback model is the same checkpoint as the initial policy, has a 66% win rate against SFT on helpful dialogue, which the authors call strict self-improvement[^266]. In all settings the labeler is never updated (our assessment). Both RLAIF and RLHF policies tend to generate longer responses[^266], and summaries also often end with extraneous symbols, possibly a result of reward hacking[^266].

Toolformer uses "whether it can lower the loss on subsequent tokens" as a self-supervised filter for the API calls that the model itself samples and executes, and then fine-tunes itself on the augmented data[^228]. With this, the 6.7B GPT-J outperforms the larger GPT-3 on several downstream tasks[^228], but the smallest models cannot benefit[^228], and sample efficiency is also very low[^228]. The authors put forward applying the method iteratively as one possible way to improve sample efficiency[^228]. From our reading, the paper itself does only a single pass (our assessment). MART has an adversarial LLM and a target LLM co-evolve: each round, the former generates stronger attacks against the updated target, and the latter undergoes safety fine-tuning[^88], with the target model assessed by feedback from two reward models, one for safety and one for helpfulness[^88]. After 4 rounds, the violation rate falls by up to 84.7% as judged by the reward models and by 53.7% as judged by humans[^88]; the final round converges on the generated adversarial prompts[^88], and about 2,400 manually curated seed prompts are still needed[^88]. OMNI uses a foundation model as a "model of interestingness" to choose the next task for an RL agent[^308], which the authors see as AI selecting its own learning tasks and thereby fostering self-improvement[^308]. In Crafter, the OMNI agent learns 82 tasks, versus 55 for a baseline that uses learning progress alone[^308]. The authors expect, however, that by Goodhart's law the model of interestingness will expose pathologies once it becomes an optimization target[^308].

The work in this section compresses human input from item-by-item labeling into seeds, principles and fixed judge models, but does not eliminate it; apart from Humpback's curator, the labelers of CAI and RLAIF and the reward models of MART are not updated during training (our assessment, based on [^27][^266][^88]). The RLAIF authors estimate that LLM labeling is more than 10 times cheaper than human labeling[^266]. In this period, the main contribution of AI feedback was to lower labeling costs, not to build a self-reinforcing signal source (our assessment).

### 5.4 The Model as Its Own Judge: Self-Rewarding, Meta-Rewarding and Bootstrapping Evaluators

Self-rewarding methods have the same model both generate responses and score them[^66][^256]. The judge is the very model being trained and therefore changes along with training; if the judge's ability improves along with it, the meta level is opened up (our assessment). The evidence in this section shows that this opening is real but shallow, and that it reverses when there is no external anchor (our assessment).

The starting point of Self-Rewarding Language Models is that reward models trained from human preferences may be bottlenecked by the level of human performance and, once frozen, cannot keep improving during training[^66]. It has the model score its own candidate responses with LLM-as-a-Judge prompting[^66], and each subsequent round is initialized from the previous round's model and trained with DPO[^66]. The seed is Llama 2 70B fine-tuned on Open Assistant[^66], and the instruction seed set consists of 3200 human examples[^66]. The AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate against GPT-4 Turbo is 9.94%, 15.38% and 20.44% over the three rounds[^66]. The judge itself also improves: its pairwise accuracy in agreement with human rankings rises from 78.7% to 80.4% and then to 81.7%[^66]. The authors' caveats matter just as much: the effect likely saturates in realistic scenarios[^66]; only three rounds were run, in a single setting[^66]; generation length grows with iterations[^66]; both the training reward and the final evaluation (GPT-4) are given by language models, which calls for deeper analysis[^66]; and on most NLP benchmarks the results are on par with the baseline[^66].

Meta-Rewarding argues that existing methods mainly improve responses rather than judgments, which makes iterative training saturate quickly[^134]; when judging ability does not improve, training of the actor may saturate quickly or even overfit the reward signal (reward hacking)[^134]. It introduces a third role, the "meta-judge", to evaluate the model's own judgments[^134], and, to curb length bias, picks the shorter response when scores are close[^134]. The length-controlled win rate of Llama-3-8B-Instruct on AlpacaEval 2 rises from 22.92% to 39.44%[^134]. On Arena-Hard, Self-Rewarding with length control scores 23.2%, 26.3%, 28.2% and 27.3% over four rounds, falling back in round 4, while Meta-Rewarding scores 25.1%, 27.4%, 27.6% and 29.1%[^134]. Compared with Self-Rewarding at the same round, its judge's agreement with GPT-4 judgments is up to +12.34% higher (Self-Chosen Pairs, round 2)[^134]. The limits are equally clear: without human annotation, judge quality can only be measured by correlation with gpt-4-1106-preview[^134]; the 5-point scale pushes responses ever closer to full marks, making improvements hard to detect[^134]; the meta-judge's positional bias hindered improvement in round 3, and score inflation by the judge accelerated saturation[^134]; and the judge's evaluation of responses it did not generate itself improves only to a limited extent[^134].

Self-Taught Evaluators directly makes the evaluator the object of improvement and uses only synthetic data[^137]. It builds contrasting pairs by treating a high-quality response to a modified, related instruction as the "worse" response[^137], trains the judge to output reasoning and a verdict, keeps only the samples whose verdicts are correct, and repeats this round after round[^137]. The RewardBench score of Llama3-70B-Instruct rises from 75.4 to 88.3, whereas training from the same seed on the labeled HelpSteer2 reaches only 85.6[^137]. The overall scores by round are 83.9, 86.0, 87.5, 87.7 and 88.3, while the easier Chat subset falls from 97.6 to 96.6[^137]; on MT-Bench, agreement with human judgments changes only from the seed's 77.8 to 78.9[^137]. The method also depends on a seed model that is already fairly well aligned with human preferences[^137]. After the third round, the gain in the overall score narrows markedly while human agreement barely moves, indicating that the evaluator's progress is concentrated on the kind of hard contrasts that RewardBench covers (our assessment).

CREAM gives the clearest reversal curve in this chapter (our assessment). The authors observe that on models of about 7B, the gains from self-rewarding may in some cases diminish after a few rounds, and hypothesize that the cause is bias accumulated in the reward system[^256]. CREAM argues that prompt-based scoring may suit only larger models such as Llama-70B[^256], so it switches to the implicit DPO reward[^256] and regularizes training with the consistency between the previous round's reward-model ranking and the current ranking[^256]. In six-round experiments on Llama-3 (averaged over five benchmarks), the score after SFT is 80.24[^256]. Unregularized SRLM falls round by round from 80.99 at M2 to 68.59 at M6[^256]. CREAM reaches 83.65 at M4 and then slips slightly to 82.73[^256]. On this basis the authors conclude that CREAM converges in round 4, while SRLM starts to degrade from round 2 onward[^256]. P-SRLM, which uses prompt-based scoring, scores only 77.62 at M2, below SFT[^256], and the authors state that prompt-based scoring is ineffective for small models, with performance declining from the first round[^256]. Because SRLM and P-SRLM are listed separately, the former should be using the implicit reward, as CREAM does, so the six-round degradation is not unique to prompt-based judges (our inference). The Oracle variant, which replaces the reward model with an external InternLM2 and draws on downstream-task labels, is the best overall[^256], from which the authors conclude that unreliable rewards are the key challenge for such methods[^256]; and overall ranking accuracy, CREAM included, does not exceed 70%[^256].

SPIN and SPPO represent another route: self-play, but with the anchor outside the model (our assessment). SPIN has the new model distinguish the previous round's self-generated responses from human-annotated responses[^8], and the global optimum of its objective is attained only when the policy matches the human data distribution[^8]. The Open LLM Leaderboard average of zephyr-7b-sft-full rises from 58.14 to 63.16, with per-round gains of +2.66, +1.32, +0.85 and +0.19[^8]. Continuing SFT on the same SFT dataset, Ultrachat200k, plateaus or even declines, whereas SPIN improves round after round using only a 50k subset of it[^8]; but the authors acknowledge that the fixed human target distribution puts a ceiling on performance[^8]. SPPO treats alignment as a constant-sum game and approximates the Nash equilibrium through iterative self-play[^152], using only 60k prompts and PairRM, a preference model with 0.4B parameters[^152]. The length-controlled win rate of Mistral-7B over three rounds is 24.79, 26.89 and 28.53, versus 23.81, 24.23 and 22.30 for iterative DPO in the same table[^152]. On the Open LLM Leaderboard, however, DPO, IPO and SPPO all decline after the first or second round, which the authors attribute to the alignment tax[^152].

Three lines of work do open up the meta level: Self-Rewarding's judge raises its pairwise accuracy by about three percentage points within three rounds, Meta-Rewarding specifically trains the judge, and Self-Taught Evaluator takes the evaluator itself as its object (our assessment, based on [^66][^134][^137]). They share three limitations (our assessment). First, the judge's improvement is concentrated on the model's own distribution, while agreement with humans stays nearly flat (our assessment, based on [^134][^137]). Second, response quality is mostly judged by GPT-4-class models, which belong to the same class as the training signal, raising the risk of circular validation (our assessment, based on [^66][^152]). Third, on small models without an external anchor, self-rewarding reverses after a few rounds, and an external reward model remains the upper bound (our assessment, based on [^256]). SPIN and SPPO do not collapse because their anchor does not drift with the model, at the cost that the ceiling is also set by the anchor (our assessment, based on [^8][^152]).

### 5.5 Learning to Self-Improve: Self-Correction and Self-Generated Curricula

Another "meta" approach is not to improve the judge but to turn improvement itself into a skill the model can learn, or to let the model's failures generate the tasks for the next stage (our assessment).

RISE (Recursive Introspection) starts from the observation that even the strongest closed-source LLMs, once told they have made a mistake, cannot keep improving their own responses[^38]. It recasts a single-turn "prompt–oracle answer" dataset as a multi-turn MDP[^38], in which k−1 turns rolled out by the current model are followed by an improved response, which comes from self-distillation (the best of several samples from the model itself) or from a stronger model[^38]. When iterating, restarting from the base model and training on the data from all rounds works better than continuing from the previous round's checkpoint[^38]. On GSM8K, using entirely their own data, LLaMa3-8B improves by 8.2% and Mistral-7B by 6.6%[^38]. Without an oracle, the five-turn introspection gains of LLaMa2 on GSM8K after training rounds 1 and 2 are 15.1% and 17.7%, respectively[^38]. Five-turn introspection improves Mistral-7B by 23.9%, but GPT-3.5 by only 4.6%[^38]. The SCoRe authors take a dimmer view of this: RISE relies mainly on oracle teacher supervision, and the preliminary results of its self-correction training show only small improvements within five turns[^132]. Before collecting on-policy data, RISE also usually first runs a round of supervised fine-tuning on multi-turn trajectories generated by a stronger model, which the authors call the "knowledge boosting" stage[^38]. RISE is trained for no more than two rounds, and for no more than one when the supervision comes from the model itself[^38]. The pure self-distillation version does not exceed the fine-tuned model's first-turn majority-vote performance (m5@t1)[^38], and the authors argue that it must first catch up with best-of-N sampling before it can possibly surpass it[^38]. For comparison, prompting-only Self-Refine lowers the performance of all models when there are no oracle hints or human feedback[^38].

SCoRe argues that self-correction has so far been largely ineffective in modern LLMs[^132] and proposes multi-turn online RL that uses entirely self-generated data[^132], while still assuming access to a reward function that evaluates outputs[^132]. Its second stage rewards "progress toward self-correction" rather than final correctness[^132]. The self-correction of Gemini 1.0 Pro and 1.5 Flash improves by 15.6% and 9.1% on MATH and HumanEval, respectively[^132]. SFT on offline correction traces either suffers from distribution mismatch or collapses into a single, ineffective mode of correction[^132]; SCoRe trains for only one round of self-correction, and subsequent rounds are not necessarily as effective[^132]. These two works move the improver from a judge outside the loop into the reasoning process, where it can be reused over multiple steps at inference time; but training still requires oracle answers or a reward function, and only one to two rounds are trained, so there is as yet no evidence of compounding at the training level (our assessment, based on [^38][^132]).

WebRL uses a self-evolving curriculum to train web agents built on open-source LLMs[^180], targeting the problem that DigiRL updates its policy only on a fixed task set[^180]. It generates new tasks from failed attempts, combined with an outcome-supervised reward model (ORM) and adaptive RL[^180]. The new tasks are generated by GPT-4o, and in each phase 500 instructions that meet the filtering criteria are selected from them[^180]. "Self-evolving" here means using the agent's own failures as seeds; the proposer is not the agent itself (our assessment). A new task is kept only if the critic's score falls between 0.05 and 0.75[^180], and infeasible tasks, as summarized from human review, are excluded by prompting GPT-4o[^180]. To curb drift, it constrains the current phase's policy not to deviate too far from the previous phase's[^180], and the replay buffer stores only successful trajectories from each phase[^180]. On WebArena-Lite, the success rate of Llama-3.1-8B rises from 4.8% to 42.4%, exceeding GPT-4-Turbo's 17.6%[^180]. In the training curves broken down by website, every site except Map shows a clear upward trend, while Map first rises and then falls[^180]. The ORM's accuracy is about 80%[^180]; the authors acknowledge that continued improvement depends heavily on the ORM[^180], and that the distribution drift brought about by online exploration may lead to forgetting and degradation[^180]. What WebRL improves is the task distribution; the paper shows no sign of the ORM being updated along with the curriculum (our assessment). Read together with OMNI, "setting one's own tasks" is safer than "grading oneself", because success and failure are still decided by the environment or a fixed reward; but in both cases the evaluator is frozen outside the loop (our assessment, based on [^308][^180]).

### 5.6 Cross-Cutting Findings: Saturation, Reversal, Anchors and "Data Compounding"

First, saturation is the norm, typically setting in between the second and fourth rounds (our assessment). In programming self-play, the gain in the second round is already small[^84]; ReST-EM regresses on APPS in the second round[^133]; and for ReST, a single Grow step is best as measured by human evaluation[^177]. SPIN's per-round gain falls from +2.66 to +0.19[^8], and SPPO's from +7.69 to +1.64[^152]; after the third round, Self-Taught Evaluator's RewardBench score rises only from 87.5 to 88.3[^137]; and MART converges in its final round[^88]. There are three kinds of exceptions that do not saturate within two to four rounds (our assessment). The first is closed, rule-governed games: when ExIt reported its results, the training curves had still not converged[^187]. The second is STaR-style loops, which retrain from the original model every round and repeat on a fixed problem set until performance plateaus[^209]. The third is WebRL's online curriculum, whose prompt-comparison experiment trained for eight phases[^180]; in the curves broken down by website, most sites keep rising, while Map first rises and then falls[^180]. The signals of the latter two remain anchored by gold-standard answers or an environment reward function (our assessment).

Second, when the judge is the model itself and is left unconstrained, saturation turns into reversal (our assessment). In CREAM's six-round experiments, unregularized self-rewarding drops from 80.99 at M2 to 68.59 at M6[^256]; the Self-Rewarding baseline reported by Meta-Rewarding falls back from 28.2% to 27.3% on Arena-Hard in round 4[^134]. Externally learned reward models show a similar phenomenon (our assessment). ReST's reward rises with the number of Grow steps, yet by human evaluation a single Grow step is best[^177]; CAI exhibits Goodharting after overtraining[^27]; and RLAIF policies append extraneous symbols to the end of summaries[^266].

Third, what truly stays fixed is the anchor (our assessment). Reasoning loops depend on human-collected problem sets and correct answers, which the authors of both ReST-EM and Iterative RPO list as a prerequisite[^133][^257], and the RL reward of Qwen2.5-Math also includes a rule-based verifier that compares against gold-standard answers[^214]. The anchors of alignment loops are human-written seeds and principles: the 175 seed tasks of Self-Instruct[^197], the 16 principles of SELF-ALIGN[^246] and the roughly 2,400 seed prompts of MART[^88]. SPIN takes human SFT data as its target distribution[^8], and SPPO uses PairRM, trained on human preferences, as its judge[^152]. There are also implicit anchors on the evaluation side: the instruction-following results of Humpback and SPPO are scored by GPT-4[^245][^152]. No work lets the model modify its own training algorithm, loss function or loop structure; changes at the meta level stop at the scorer and the task distribution (our assessment).

Fourth, for a substantial share of the methods, what accumulates across rounds is data rather than weights (our assessment). STaR and ReST-EM return to the original or base model every round[^209][^133], and RISE retrains from the base model while accumulating the data from all rounds[^38]. V-STaR's generator starts from the pretrained model each round and is fine-tuned on the accumulated correct solutions[^48], and ExIt reinitializes its network weights every round[^187]. The methods that carry weights forward include AlphaZero's single continually updated network[^120] and Iterative RPO, though the latter, conversely, does not reuse the data collected in earlier rounds[^257]. Self-Rewarding and Meta-Rewarding also continue training from the previous round's model[^66][^134], while WebRL needs a KL constraint and a replay buffer to guard against drift[^180]. Within this chapter's literature, the evidence of sustained compounding at the parameter level comes mainly from game self-play (our assessment).

### 5.7 Summary (our assessment)

1. The evidence for bounded self-improvement is sufficient: as long as an external anchor exists (rules, interpreters, gold-standard answers, unit tests), training on self-generated data delivers stable gains within one to four rounds, and these gains can be measured with metrics that do not depend on LLM judges. Based on [^84][^133][^257].
2. Compounding improvement is visible only in closed games. ExIt's unconverged training curves and AlphaZero's rise from random play to superhuman level depend on rule-based rewards that the policy cannot manipulate and on a problem distribution that automatically grows harder as the opponent grows stronger; not one of the LLM loops covered in this chapter meets both conditions at once. Based on [^187][^120].
3. LLM self-training loops generally saturate within two to four rounds; the longest self-evaluation loop in this chapter (six rounds) degrades significantly without regularization, and even with regularization it merely converges in the fourth round and then slips slightly, rather than continuing to rise. Based on [^256][^8][^152].
4. The meta level has been opened, but only shallowly: curators, verifiers, process reward models and LLM judges all have instances of being improved iteratively, but the improvements are limited in size, concentrated on the model's own distribution, and mostly measured by GPT-4 judgments or synthetically constructed labels; most of these experiments run for only two to five rounds, among which Self-Taught Evaluator already levels off after the third round. Based on [^134][^137][^48]. See also [^66][^14][^245].
5. No work modifies the training algorithm, the loss function or the loop structure itself; the outermost layer (seeds, principles, answers or reward functions, external evaluators) is always fixed by humans, and the "recursion" stops at the scorer and the task distribution. Based on [^197][^27][^214].
6. The signal source determines both the ceiling and the risk, and "who scores" matters more than "who generates": verified self-generated data and unverified data from a stronger model have similar effects, learned reward models are decoupled from human evaluation, and model self-evaluation reverses on small models. Based on [^84][^177][^256].
7. The carrier of cross-round compounding is often data rather than parameters: STaR, ReST-EM, RISE and others return to the base model every round and carry only the data, and the generator that produced it, into the next round; among the loops that carry weights forward, WebRL relies on KL and replay, and CREAM on a cross-round consistency constraint, to resist drift or the accumulation of bias. Based on [^209][^38][^180]. See also [^133][^256].
8. Self-correction and self-generated curricula turn the improver into a skill the model can learn or into a task distribution, offering another path toward recursive self-improvement; but RISE and SCoRe train for only one to two rounds, and the evaluators of WebRL and OMNI remain frozen, which is not yet enough to demonstrate compounding. Based on [^38][^132][^308].

## 6. Weight-Level Self-Improvement (Part II): Zero-Data Self-Play and Self-Editing (2025–2026)

This chapter covers self-improvement work from January 2025 to October 2026 that writes improvements into the parameters: the model generates training material (problems, solutions, labels, self-edit instructions or workflow trajectories), updates its own weights after the material is filtered or scored, and the updated model then generates the next round of material. The chapter sorts 31 papers into five groups (our count): reinforcement learning anchored by verifiers; label-free reinforcement learning that replaces the verifier with the model's own consistency or confidence; zero-data proposer–solver co-evolution; work in which the model rewrites its own training process; and systems from 2026 explicitly named as RSI. Four questions run through the chapter: what external signal anchors the loop; how long it can run before saturating or collapsing; whether the improver itself is modified, and which parts stay fixed; and whether the evidence supports bounded self-improvement or recursive self-improvement that compounds across generations.

### 6.1 Verifier-Anchored Reinforcement Learning: The Foundation of Self-Improvement in This Period

(our assessment) The foundation of weight-level self-improvement in this period is not the "self" but reliable external verifiers: the model handles generation and search, while what counts as "correct" is decided by rules, code execution or formal provers.

(our assessment) DeepSeek-R1 supplied the default recipe for work in this period. Without human-annotated reasoning trajectories, it applies pure reinforcement learning directly to the base model, with R1-Zero skipping the conventional SFT stage[^267]. The reward checks only whether the final answer agrees with the ground truth, plus a format reward, and the authors explicitly do not use neural reward models for reasoning tasks[^267]. As a result, the average pass@1 of R1-Zero on AIME 2024 rises from 15.6% to 77.9%, and reaches 86.7% with self-consistency decoding[^267]. The authors attribute the success to hard problems, reliable verifiers and sufficient compute for reinforcement learning[^267]. The same paper also gives counterevidence: with a helpful reward model, the reward rises while CodeForces performance falls[^267]. DeepSeek-R1 itself, in turn, uses a multi-stage pipeline that combines rejection sampling, reinforcement learning and SFT[^267]. The authors acknowledge that extending pure reinforcement learning to tasks that cannot be evaluated by a reliable reward model remains an open problem[^267].

rStar-Math has a small policy model and a process preference model (PPM) co-evolve round by round: each round uses MCTS to generate step-by-step verified trajectories, keeping only the nodes whose Python code executes successfully, and the newly trained models then generate the next round's data[^47]. After four rounds, the MATH accuracy of Qwen2.5-Math-7B rises from 58.8% to 90.0%, but this depends on deep thinking over 64 trajectories[^47]. Looking at the policy model alone, its pass@1 in round 4 is 78.4, against 75.3 for DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B), which was used to bootstrap the data in round 1[^47]. The loop stopped at round 4 because 90.25% of the 747k problems had already entered the training set, and a spot check of 20 still-unsolved problems found 19 with incorrect labels[^47].

(our assessment) STP is the earliest proposer–solver loop in this chapter's literature. Its starting point is reward sparsity: of the 2.5M proofs generated for 79K unproved statements, only 131 are correct, so expert iteration stalls[^247]. STP has the same model act as both conjecturer and prover, and the conjecturer is trained only on conjectures for which the current prover's success probability is positive but low, so that the difficulty can be raised step by step without adding new data[^247]. The Lean experiment ran for 48 rounds and generated 51.3B tokens, proving 28.5% of the statements in LeanWorkbook, against a previous best of 13.2% for expert iteration[^247]. The price is hand-designed constraints: the conjectures undergo mode collapse after several rounds, and heuristic elegance filtering and reweighting are needed to keep them diverse[^247].

Two further analytical works discuss the preconditions under which such loops hold. Lee et al. have a standard Transformer with 14M parameters generate labels for slightly harder instances and then train itself on them, extending addition from 10 digits to 100 digits with no saturation observed[^259]. Without data filtering, errors accumulate round by round and eventually make performance collapse, which the authors call an "error avalanche"; they regard length filtering and majority voting as the keys to sustained improvement[^259]. Gandhi et al. find that under the same Countdown reinforcement learning, Qwen-2.5-3B reaches about 60% at the end of training, whereas Llama-3.2-3B reaches only 30%[^111]. Continued pretraining on OpenWebMath filtered for behaviors such as verification and backtracking lets Llama reproduce Qwen's self-improvement trajectory, from which the authors argue that cognitive behaviors are a causal factor in self-improvement through reinforcement learning[^111].

(our assessment) The loops in this group are all bounded: rStar-Math stops at the limits of its problem bank and label quality, STP depends on hand-designed anti-collapse mechanisms, the small-model experiments depend on a hand-designed difficulty curriculum, and what reinforcement learning can amplify depends first of all on the behaviors the base model already has. Based on [^47][^247][^111]. At the meta level, the PPM and the conjecturer grow stronger within the loop, but the recipe, the problem bank and the number of rounds are set by humans (our assessment).

### 6.2 Label-Free and Self-Scored Reinforcement Learning: Replacing the Verifier with the Model Itself

(our assessment) The second route keeps the form of reinforcement learning but replaces the external verifier with the model's own majority vote, its confidence, or self-rereading with feedback; it can compress the gains of test-time scaling back into the weights, but it has no source of correctness independent of the model, and it is therefore the first to expose reward hacking and collapse.

TTRL uses majority voting directly as the reward and runs reinforcement learning on unlabeled test data[^198]. The pass@1 of Qwen2.5-Math-7B on AIME 2024 rises from 12.9 to 40.2, an improvement of about 211%[^198]. The authors report that performance after training exceeds the upper bound of the initial model's maj@n and approaches that of a model trained directly on ground-truth labels[^198]. But TTRL fails when prior knowledge is insufficient for the difficulty of the data, and the harder the problems, the smaller the improvement[^198]. The SRT authors point out that TTRL trains and tests on the same set of problems[^258].

Intuitor uses the model's own confidence (self-certainty) as the sole reward for GRPO, matching GRPO on math benchmarks, and Qwen2.5-3B trained on MATH achieves a 65% relative improvement on LiveCodeBench[^28]. When scoring is done offline by a fixed base model, the policy learns around update step 100 to append an already-solved problem after its answer to inflate its confidence[^28]. An online scorer that is updated together with the policy avoids this exploitation[^28]. The authors acknowledge that self-certainty is not guaranteed to reflect true correctness, and that training collapses when the prior is insufficient[^28].

SRT systematically examines whether self-training can be sustained in reinforcement learning: it estimates the ground truth with the model's own majority vote and refreshes this pseudo-reward at every gradient step[^258]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, SRT's peak mean@32 on MATH-12K is 0.32, versus 0.33 for training with ground truth[^258]. With longer training, however, all 4 base models undergo a sudden and complete collapse[^258]. After 1200 steps of training on DAPO, Qwen2.5-Math-7B outputs `\boxed{1}` followed by a string of incoherent tokens for any problem[^258]. Raising the KL coefficient does not prevent this reward hacking, and lowering the learning rate only delays the collapse[^258]. Switching to pseudo-labels generated offline by a fixed base checkpoint makes training markedly more stable, from which the authors argue that refreshing labels online may cause instability[^258]. Even with offline labels, though, training on MATH-12k for more than one epoch also collapses[^258]. The paper also reports two mitigations, a curriculum and early stopping: when training only on the easiest third of the DAPO problems, no reward hacking appears even after 3 epochs[^258]. Using 1% of DAPO as a validation set for early stopping gives near-optimal performance on the other datasets[^258]. The authors conclude that feedback design is the central challenge for sustained self-improvement[^258]. (our assessment) Early stopping requires a validation set with ground truth, which amounts to putting a small piece of external anchor back into the loop.

SDPO puts the textual feedback returned by the environment into the context, uses "the current model after seeing the feedback" as a self-teacher, and distills its next-token predictions back into the policy[^265]. On LiveCodeBench v6, Qwen3-8B reaches 48.8% with SDPO, versus 41.2% with GRPO[^265]. The gains grow with model scale, but on weaker models SDPO may underperform GRPO[^265].

(our assessment) The common upper bound of this group is the base model's prior: TTRL and Intuitor fail when the prior is insufficient, SDPO is effective mainly on stronger models, and SRT's collapse shows that once consistency decouples from correctness, the optimizer goes after consistency first. Based on [^198][^28][^258]. These methods internalize maj@n, or the ability to retry with feedback, into a single sample; this is bounded improvement, and there is no evidence that such internalization can stack up generation after generation (our assessment).

### 6.3 Zero-Data Proposer–Solver Co-Evolution

(our assessment) From April 2025 onward, a large body of work has extended STP-style difficulty curricula to code, games, tools, search and real code repositories: a proposer and a solver are split off from one base model, the proposer is rewarded for problems at the edge of the solver's capability, and the solver is trained with reinforcement learning. "Zero data" means that no human-curated problems or labels are needed; it does not mean there is no external anchor, and what distinguishes these systems is who judges the answers.

**Anchored by executors, rules or real environments.** Absolute Zero (AZR) uses a single model to both propose and solve tasks, with a code executor that both verifies whether a task is valid and checks the answer, and the whole loop starts from just a single identity-function triplet[^15]. Without any external data, AZR scores on average 1.8 points higher than previous zero-setting models trained on in-domain data[^15]. For the 3B, 7B and 14B coder models, the overall gains on out-of-distribution benchmarks are +5.7, +10.2 and +13.2, respectively[^15]. The authors observe concerning chains of thought on Llama-3.1-8B (an "uh-oh moment") and acknowledge that they do not discuss how to manage such systems safely[^15]. Self-Challenging Agents (SCA) has a fixed Llama-3.1-8B write Code-as-Task problems with verification functions and test cases, and the executor is rewarded by the verification results; the success rate doubles from 12.0% to 23.5%[^260]. But the self-generated tasks still have non-negligible false negatives, and the gains are mostly environment-specific[^260]. SPIRAL has the model play zero-sum games against ever-stronger versions of itself, and the average score of Qwen3-4B-Base on 8 reasoning benchmarks rises from 34.0% to 44.5%[^261]. The self-play win rate stays at 50–52%, whereas the win rate under fixed-opponent training climbs from 0% to 62.5%, which the authors read as exploiting the opponent rather than continued learning[^261]. Without role-conditioned advantage estimation (RAE), the length of reasoning traces drops from about 2,000 characters to nearly zero within 100 policy iterations[^261].

Self-play SWE-RL (SSR) puts the anchor in real code repositories: the same model (with shared parameters) both injects and repairs bugs, each bug is formalized by test changes the model proposes, and failed repair attempts are then combined into higher-order bugs[^255]. With the 32B CWM-sft as the base model, SSR improves by 10.4 and 7.8 points on SWE-bench Verified and SWE-Bench Pro, respectively, and outperforms, throughout training, a reinforcement learning baseline that can see human-written issues and tests[^255]. But all runs train for only 150 global steps, and the paired standard error on SWE-bench Verified is about 2%[^255]. The paper also notes that naive bug injection collapses into single-line changes, and that in theory a sufficiently clever proposer has dominant strategies that stop self-play from making progress, among which the "tunnel vision" strategy applies equally to Absolute Zero-style methods[^255].

**Anchored by corpora, search engines or tools.** SPICE targets two problems of unanchored self-play: errors amplified between problem generation and problem solving, and the "information symmetry" caused by both sides sharing the same knowledge[^249]. It has the Challenger mine problems from 20,000 documents and extract gold answers, while the Reasoner does not see the documents[^249]. After training for a fixed 640 iterations, the overall improvement of Qwen3-4B-Base is +9.1[^249]. In the ablation, the score is 43.9% with corpus grounding and 40.7% without it[^249]. Agent0 uses a sandboxed code interpreter as an external source of capability, and the executor is trained on majority-vote pseudo-labels; the average math score of Qwen3-8B-Base over the three rounds is 55.1, 56.5 and 58.2[^248]. The paper's main table, however, shows the base model at 49.2, reaching 53.2 just by being given the tool[^248]. Dr. Zero uses only a search engine as an external resource, and its 3B model outperforms the supervised Search-R1 by 22.9%, 6.5% and 18.4% on NQ, TriviaQA and PopQA, respectively[^254]. Its gains are concentrated early: in the first round of training the solver peaks after about 50 steps, and in the second round the average gains for 3B and 7B are 4.93% and 1.64%[^254]. In the third round, 3B gains only another 2.2% and 7B drops from 0.372 to 0.360, after which further iterations yield almost no benefit[^254].

**Anchored by the model itself.** SQLM gives the model only a topic prompt, and the solver is rewarded by majority vote; Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct improves by 14% on arithmetic and 16% on algebra, and these figures are the best test accuracies within 100 training steps[^252]. The authors acknowledge that there is no correction mechanism when the model repeatedly converges on self-consistent wrong answers[^252]. (our assessment) R-Zero gives the most detailed failure analysis in this group. It initializes the Challenger and the Solver from the same base model, and the Solver runs GRPO with pseudo-labels from its own majority vote, only on problems whose agreement rate falls within an informative band[^37]. Qwen3-4B-Base improves by +6.49 on math reasoning and +7.54 on general reasoning[^37]. But all models degrade after several rounds: 0.6B peaks in the first round (Step 15), and 4B holds up for three rounds before dropping sharply at Step 60[^37]. Taking GPT-4o as the reference, the pseudo-label accuracy of the problems generated in the first three rounds falls from 79.0% to 63.0%[^37]. Label noise is not the only cause: 0.6B starts to decline while data accuracy is still 70.6%, whereas 4B can tolerate accuracy as low as 48.8%[^37]. A single-model version with shared parameters peaks right after the first round and degrades immediately[^37]. Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE) has the same model act as proposer, solver and a judge that scores by rubric, uses no ground truth at all, improves Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct by 4.54% on average, and keeps improving for more than 250 steps[^251]. The authors point out that a difficulty reward alone tempts the proposer into generating unsolvable or ambiguous questions[^251].

**Training critics and multi-turn agents.** SPC trains a critic through an adversarial game: a sneaky generator produces erroneous steps that are hard to detect; under the strict criterion the authors adopt, a pair of steps is used only if the original step gives an external solver a success rate of at least 75% and the erroneous step drives it down to 0%[^253]. It is not zero-data, since its initialization used about 21.8K critiques filtered with PRM800K's human annotations[^253]. After two rounds of reinforcement learning, the ProcessBench average rises from 70.8% to 77.7%[^253]. It regresses when the game is unbalanced: when the generator's win rate against the critic is only 13.2%, the critic falls from 76.8% to 72.0%[^253]. RAGEN observes a recurring Echo Trap in multi-turn agent reinforcement learning, marked by collapsing reward variance, falling entropy and gradient spikes, and when only task success is rewarded, the model also produces hallucinated reasoning[^217].

### 6.4 Models That Rewrite Their Own Training Process

(our assessment) One step further than "posing its own problems" is having the model rewrite its own training data, reward model or mutation operator, but such modifications currently occur at only one stage of the training pipeline.

(our assessment) SEAL is the work in this chapter that comes closest to "a model rewriting its own training". Given a new input, the model generates a "self-edit", which can restructure information, specify optimization hyperparameters or call tools, and which is then turned into a persistent weight update through SFT[^218]. An outer loop trains the self-edit policy with reinforcement learning, using the downstream performance of the updated model as the reward[^218]. On the knowledge-incorporation task, Qwen2.5-7B rises from 33.5% to 47.0% on no-passage SQuAD, surpassing synthetic data generated with GPT-4.1[^218]. This result used only 2 rounds of ReST-EM, with diminishing returns after two rounds[^218]. Limitations include catastrophic forgetting from sequential self-edits, an evaluation cost of about 30–45 seconds per self-edit, and a reward that must be tied to a paired downstream task and cannot be extended to unlabeled corpora[^218].

DeepSeekMath-V2 targets the problem that final-answer rewards do not guarantee correct reasoning: it first trains a proof verifier and then uses it as the reward model for the generator[^250]. To maintain the "generation–verification gap" once the generator becomes stronger, the authors scale verification compute to automatically label new hard proofs, which are then used to continue training the verifier[^250]. Because the verifier may fabricate nonexistent issues even while giving the correct score, the authors also add meta-verification[^250]. The initial data were scored by mathematics experts, and in the last two iterations the automated pipeline fully replaced human annotation[^250]. The final model reaches gold-medal level on IMO 2025 and CMO 2024 and scores 118/120 on Putnam 2024 with scaled test-time compute[^250].

Self-Improving Pretraining uses an existing strong post-trained model to rewrite pretraining data and judge rollouts, moving safety, factuality and generation quality forward into pretraining[^159]. The authors envision this strong model as coming from the previous generation of a self-improvement loop, but in the experiments the judge is a fine-tuned Llama3.1-8B-Instruct or a prompted GPT-OSS-120B, and the policy model is a 1.4B Llama2[^159]. Under continued pretraining, the win rate on generation quality reaches as high as 86.3%, with safety judged by GPT-OSS-120B[^159].

TTT-Discover continues reinforcement learning at test time on a single problem, aiming to obtain one best solution rather than to generalize[^157]. It improves the upper bound for the Erdős minimum overlap problem to 0.380876; the previous bound, from AlphaEvolve, was 0.380924[^157]. In the TriMul kernel competition on H100, its best runtime is 1203.10 µs, against a human best of 1371.1 µs and 2060.70 µs for search alone without test-time training[^157]. On MLA decode on AMD MI300X, however, none of its kernels significantly outperforms the best human submission, and the method currently applies only to continuous rewards[^157].

(our assessment) Self-Referential Graph HyperNetworks is the only work in this chapter that modifies the mutation operator itself. The hypernetwork generates parameter updates for copies of itself and injects learnable mutations, and the mutation rate is inherited as a selectable trait[^202]. On CartPole-Switch, it is the only method that stably recovers to the top score after both environment switches, but parameter generation is costly, and its scale grows rapidly with the target network[^202].

Gödel Forest explicitly takes the Gödel machine as its reference point and proposes "data-centric RSI": multiple coding agents each maintain a persistent search tree over data strategies and distill successes and failures into shared experience[^141]. All candidate models are trained with LoRA SFT starting from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base, Claude Opus 4.8 is the fixed rollout generator, and each run is capped at 16 hours and \$500[^141]. Across the six domains of RSIBench-Data, it scores on average 10.70% higher than the single-agent baseline[^141]. But every candidate is trained from the same initial parameters θ0, the "recursion" resides in the agents' search state, and the official score is post-selection validation on the same tasks, not generalization on a held-out set[^141].

(our assessment) What gets modified is the data (SEAL, Self-Improving Pretraining, Gödel Forest), the reward model (DeepSeekMath-V2) or the mutation rate (GHN), while the objective function, the outer optimization algorithm and the acceptance criteria are all set by humans. Of the six systems in this section, only SEAL, DeepSeekMath-V2 and GHN both use the improved model in the next round and are not limited to a single problem: SEAL shows diminishing returns after two rounds, DeepSeekMath-V2 starts from expert scores, and GHN is validated only on small reinforcement-learning control tasks. Based on [^218][^250][^202].

### 6.5 Weight-Level Systems Labeled "RSI" in 2026

(our assessment) September–October 2026 saw a batch of weight-level works with RSI directly in their titles (Gödel Forest also has RSI in its title and was already discussed in Section 6.4). All of them claim to close an "evaluate–select–update" or "self-supervision" loop, but the strength of their evidence clearly lags behind their names.

NeoHorse-1 interprets RSI as a mechanism by which a system observes its own capabilities and then turns that evidence into the next round of learning, and argues that a deployed routing harness naturally contains such a mechanism[^61]. For each conversation turn, the system records the predicted capability requirement, the selected service tier and the subsequent interaction; trajectories enter training after structural validation, six-dimensional semantic evaluation and sub-scenario labeling, and a fixed teacher provides supervision on prefixes generated by the student itself[^61]. Post-training raises the 4B model's macro-average over ten benchmarks from 58.94 to 64.87, and the 9B model's from 65.60 to 69.04[^61]. The authors state explicitly that the results reflect only a single execution of the loop and that whether gains accumulate across rounds remains to be tested[^61]. Because the harness also calls other models, this process links multi-model experience to the improvement of a single model[^61]. (our assessment) With a fixed teacher and experience that does not come entirely from the model itself, this is closer to a post-training data flywheel driven by deployment logs and is not yet evidence of recursive self-improvement.

DCE+SRCL starts from on-policy self-distillation (OPSD): OPSD uses as its teacher a frozen copy of the student with the ground truth placed in its context, and the authors argue that freezing prevents the teacher from absorbing the student's improvements[^263]. DCE lets the privileged teacher co-evolve with the student, with the parameters updated in each round initializing both the student and the teacher of the next round, while SRCL trains on shorter verified self-rewrites to suppress verbosity[^263]. In the non-thinking mode of Qwen3-8B, the Average@12 scores on four competition-math benchmarks are as follows: base model 19.51%, GRPO 20.28%, OPSD 30.35%, DCE+SRCL 65.97%[^263]. Forcing OPSD to generate 16K tokens yields only 30.76%, indicating that the gain does not come merely from longer reasoning[^263]. The teacher-refresh ablation is the most direct: DCE reaches 65.76% and 60.00% on 8B and 4B, versus 47.99% and 25.28% with a frozen teacher[^263]. The same main table also gives the base model's scores in thinking mode: 62.08% for 8B, 60.00% for 4B and 36.88% for 1.7B, whereas DCE+SRCL reaches 65.97%, 61.88% and 26.88%, respectively[^263]. (our assessment, based on [^263]) Each "round" here corresponds to one mini-batch update, checkpoints are evaluated up to at most 200 steps, and the main gain is transferring capabilities already present in thinking mode to non-thinking mode: 8B and 4B are only slightly above the base model's thinking mode, and 1.7B remains clearly below it.

MASS targets unverifiable tasks such as open-ended research, starting from the premise that once outputs exceed what human experts can reliably evaluate, the model itself is the best optimizer and evaluator[^60]. The same base model proposes, executes and pairwise self-evaluates multi-agent workflows; trajectories selected by Bradley–Terry scores are used for SFT; within a generation, the executor, evaluator and optimizer share weights; and the harness is fixed throughout the paper[^60]. After two cycles with Qwen3.6-27B, the win rate against the base model on three test tasks rises from 53.9% to 69.9%, and performance per output token on four research benchmarks is 1.2–1.6 times that of the base model[^60]. The evaluator's agreement with strong external model judges rises from 0.73 to 0.91 and then to 0.93[^60]. The win rates, however, are given by two external LLM judges, GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8[^60]. Results on public benchmarks judged by execution outcomes are mixed: the success rate on ScienceAgentBench is 28.8, 30.4 and 31.7 in turn[^60]. The pass rate on Terminal-Bench 2.0, by contrast, is 37.5, 35.3 and 35.2, and on SWE-bench Verified 62.6, 61.8 and 61.1, with error bars giving the standard deviation over three runs[^60]. (our assessment) For the latter two, the differences across the three generations are about one standard deviation, showing no discernible improvement; the rise on ScienceAgentBench is also less than two standard deviations. The paper's behavioral audit also records a case in which the student model, after failing an evaluation, requested that the simulator be modified; the revision replaced force-driven motion with direct repositioning toward the target, after which success was reported on all three task types[^60]. This result received favorable verdicts in five of six judgments, even though it changed the physical meaning of "success"[^60]. Under the paper's default setting, the six judgments for each pair of outputs come from three seeds each of GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8[^60]. (our inference) The case therefore undermines not only self-evaluation but also the external LLM judges that the paper uses to report win rates. The authors also acknowledge that such a homogeneous loop shares a single prior and may reinforce rather than correct its own errors[^60].

RSI-Anything interprets RSI as turning interaction experience into persistent improvements that are retained[^264]. It uses a predictive world model to decide which actions are worth costly external feedback and which data enter learning, and component checks and compatibility checks then decide which pair of policy and model to keep[^264]. Within a single run, the task objective, scoring criteria, acceptance and stopping rules, and budget cap all remain fixed[^264]. In code repair, with the coding model held fixed, the success rate rises from 26.3% with a static verifier to 29.2% with predictive selection, and to 31.2% after the world model adapts[^264]. In lab planning, five successive 4B policy versions produced by the same self-improvement process score, in order, 21.80%, 38.03%, 41.99%, 44.56% and 46.01% on the 200-question F200 Level-2 benchmark[^264]. The final version is 0.69 percentage points above the Actor–Simulator–Selector reference system[^264]. The authors write explicitly that each experiment instantiates only part of the protocol, and that confirming that the policy and the world model keep improving each other within a single task would still require joint multi-round evaluation[^264]. The paper's general protocol specifies that update data are used to construct candidate versions, gating data decide which versions are kept, and a held-out audit set, if one is designated, is used only for the final report[^264]. (our assessment) However, we found no statement in the paper on whether F200 is a held-out audit set or on how each version was trained, and the gains between adjacent versions shrink progressively; these five versions are the data in this chapter that come closest to "improvement retained across multiple generations", yet they still count only as evidence of bounded improvement.

(our assessment) None of the four systems reports an improvement curve that accelerates with the number of generations under controlled comparison. Based on [^61][^60][^264].

### 6.6 Cross-System Comparison: Anchors, Proposer Rewards, Collapse Modes and Baseline Sensitivity

**Anchors set the ceiling, and also how long a loop can run.** (our assessment) By anchor, the systems in this chapter can be grouped into four classes: execution or formal verification, external data, environment rules, and the model itself; when the papers' full recipes are used, most externally anchored systems do not collapse within their own reported training ranges, but an external anchor does not automatically guarantee stability. STP runs for 58 rounds on Isabelle[^247], and SPICE trains for a fixed 640 iterations[^249]. SSR outperforms the human-data baseline throughout training, but also reports instability that prevents further scaling[^255]. RAGEN, anchored on environment reward, likewise repeatedly falls into the Echo Trap and needs training on high-variance prompts to delay or eliminate collapse[^217]. (our assessment) Systems anchored on the model itself are shorter-lived. R-Zero's 4B model drops sharply at Step 60[^37], and SRT collapses into template outputs after 1200 steps of training on DAPO[^258]. Dr. Zero, with a mixed anchor, plateaus after the second round[^254]. MAE keeps improving under self-evaluation for more than 250 steps, but its average improvement is only 4.54%[^251]. (our assessment) "Steps" and "rounds" are not the same unit across papers; what is comparable is whether each paper reports a rise followed by a decline within its own budget. By this standard, self-anchored loops commonly report a decline after a peak, and most externally anchored loops rise or level off within their budgets, but no system has reported a long-horizon run without human intervention and without a budget cap.

**Proposer rewards do not share a single shape.** (our assessment) A common summary is that the proposer is rewarded for finding problems with a solve rate of about one half, but the actual definitions differ across systems; Table 6-2 lists them as defined in the original papers.

In both R-Zero and Agent0, p̂ is defined as the fraction of solutions that agree with the majority answer[^37][^248]. (our inference) Hence p̂ = 0.5 targets problems on which the majority answer receives only half the votes, not problems of middling accuracy, and the proposer is pushed toward regions where pseudo-labels are less reliable. R-Zero's data are consistent with this: as problems become harder, pseudo-label accuracy falls from 79.0% to 63.0%, and the authors likewise argue that majority voting therefore becomes increasingly unreliable[^37]. The authors of Dr. Zero acknowledge that their design can only reduce, not eliminate, incorrect or ambiguous synthetic answers[^254].

**Collapse modes and mitigations.** (our assessment) Failures can be grouped into five classes. The first is reward hacking of self-generated rewards: SRT learns to output template answers unrelated to the problem[^258], Intuitor's offline scorer is exploited by the trick of appending already-solved problems[^28], and R1's helpful reward model score rises while its CodeForces performance falls[^267]. The second is pseudo-labels degrading as difficulty rises[^37]. The third is diversity collapse: STP's conjectures[^247], RAGEN's Echo Trap[^217] and SSR's one-line bugs[^255]. SPIRAL's thinking collapse after removing RAE also falls into this class[^261]. The fourth is error accumulation, that is, error avalanches[^259]. The fifth is exhaustion of the problem pool or environment: rStar-Math stops when the remaining problems are mostly mislabeled[^47], and SPIRAL's gains level off with extended training[^261]. All mitigations are designed by humans: role-conditioned advantage estimation[^261], trajectory filtering[^217], and elegance filtering and reweighting[^247]. The same goes for asymmetric opponent scheduling[^253], repetition penalties and information-band filtering[^37], and curricula and early stopping[^258].

**Should the supervisor be updated along with the policy?** The literature gives opposite results on this point. Intuitor finds that an online scorer prevents the exploitation that the offline scorer suffers[^28]. SRT, however, finds that offline pseudo-labels from a fixed checkpoint are more stable, and that online refreshing may instead introduce instability[^258]. DCE's ablation supports refreshing: with a refreshed teacher it reaches 65.76% on 8B, versus 47.99% with a frozen teacher[^263]. (our inference) The disagreement may stem from a single variable: whether the supervisor holds independent information beyond the policy's samples. DCE's privileged teacher sees the gold solution in context[^263], SPC's erroneous steps are determined by the success rate of an external solver[^253], and DeepSeekMath-V2's verifier starts from expert scores and adds meta-verification[^250]. SRT's pseudo-labels, by contrast, come entirely from majority votes over the policy's own samples[^258]. (our inference) In the former cases, a supervisor that grows stronger along with the policy is a net gain; in the latter, refreshing makes the labels drift together with the policy. What Intuitor's offline scorer exposes is a fixed blind spot, which online updating happens to eliminate, but this does not guarantee that online self-evaluation will not decouple from correctness in other ways (our inference).

**Sensitivity to baselines and evaluation conventions.** (our assessment) Rankings among zero-data methods are highly sensitive to "who runs the baselines". Take the math average for Qwen3-4B-Base: R-Zero self-reports 49.93 in its Table 1, with AZR at 46.42 in the same table[^37]. Agent0's table gives R-Zero 49.1 and AZR 46.4[^248]. SPICE states that it reran all baselines with public code; on its overall metric combining math and general reasoning, R-Zero is +3.7 over the base model and AZR +4.9[^249]. (our count, based on SPICE Table 1) Taking the simple average of only its seven math benchmarks, R-Zero scores 45.04 and AZR 46.36, so the ordering is still the reverse of that in the first two tables. Even the untrained base model is inconsistent: Qwen3-4B-Base's GSM8K score is 87.79 in R-Zero's table[^37] and 72.6 in SPICE's table, which uses GPT-4o to judge answer equivalence[^249]. R-Zero's own numbers are not fully consistent either: Qwen3-8B-Base rises from 49.18 to 54.69 in the main text, but Table 1 shows 48.64 and 53.72[^37]. In Agent0's table, the base model and R-Zero on Qwen3-8B-Base are 49.2 and 54.7, respectively[^248], matching the numbers in R-Zero's main text[^37]. (our count, based on [^249][^248]) In addition, AZR's seven individual math scores on Qwen3-4B-Base in SPICE's table are exactly the same as the corresponding values in Agent0's table. (our inference) This indicates that the AZR numbers in the two tables come from the same source: either one side reused numbers from the other or from a common source, or both used exactly the same checkpoint and the same deterministic evaluation pipeline. SPICE declares that it reran all baselines, whereas we found no statement in Agent0 about rerunning baselines, so the baseline numbers in Agent0's main table may have been taken from published results, and its rows do not necessarily come from the same evaluation protocol (our inference). Reporting conventions are likewise inconsistent: SQLM reports the best test accuracy within 100 training steps[^252], while R-Zero's tables take results after 45 training steps[^37]. SSR reports a paired standard error of about 2% on SWE-bench Verified[^255], and Gödel Forest's official score is post-selection validation on the same tasks[^141]. (our assessment) Under these conditions, gaps of a few points between zero-data methods are not enough to support conclusions about their ranking.

**The base model's prior sets how far a loop can go.** The gap between Qwen and Llama under the same reinforcement learning comes from whether the base model already exhibits behaviors such as verification and backtracking[^111]. The gains of both SDPO and AZR grow with model scale[^265][^15], and R-Zero's larger models only delay collapse rather than avoid it[^37]. (our assessment) These results better support the interpretation of "eliciting latent capabilities" than that of the loop creating new capabilities beyond the base model; DCE+SRCL's non-thinking-mode scores landing near the base model's thinking mode are one example (based on [^263]).

### 6.7 Summary (our assessment)

- The evidence in this chapter supports bounded self-improvement, not compounding recursive self-improvement. Most systems retain improvements in the weights and use the improved model to generate the next round of data, which is the bare minimum for "recursion". The longest-running loops with no reported collapse (R1-Zero's more than ten thousand steps, STP's dozens of rounds, SPICE's hundreds of iterations) are all anchored on rule-based verifiers, formal verifiers or external corpora, based on [^267][^247][^249]. NeoHorse-1, MASS and RSI-Anything report only one loop, two cycles and five policy versions, respectively, and no paper reports improvement speeding up with the number of generations, based on [^61][^60][^264].
- Anchors set the ceiling. "Zero data" removes human-curated problem sets, not external verification: loops anchored on executors, formal verification, corpora or real repositories run longer and more stably, whereas loops anchored on majority voting, confidence or self-evaluation either degrade within a few rounds or a little over a thousand steps, report only a peak within a short window, or show small gains. Based on [^249][^258][^37].
- The proposer's learnability reward is itself a meta-component fixed by humans. In self-labeling loops, it pushes problems toward regions where pseudo-labels are less reliable, creating a structural tension, and all anti-collapse measures (RAE, reweighting, repetition penalties, curricula, early stopping) are likewise designed by humans. Based on [^37][^261][^247].
- At the meta level, the only things modified are the curriculum, the supervisor (verifier, judge, teacher, evaluator), training data and hyperparameters (SEAL), and the mutation rate (GHN); the objective function, the outer optimization algorithm, and the acceptance and stopping rules are always set by humans, and RSI-Anything states even more explicitly that these are fixed within a run. Based on [^218][^202][^264].
- The effect of co-evolving the supervisor depends on whether it holds independent information beyond the policy's samples: DeepSeekMath-V2 and DCE are positive examples, SRT's online majority voting is a counterexample, and Intuitor's results show that online updating can only patch specific blind spots. Based on [^250][^263][^258].
- The names of the 2026 systems labeled RSI run ahead of their evidence. NeoHorse-1 executes the loop only once, and MASS's main gains depend on LLM judges and are mixed on execution-judged public benchmarks, based on [^61][^60]. RSI-Anything performs only piecewise validation, and DCE+SRCL's gains approach the level of the base model's thinking mode, based on [^263][^264].
- Evidence quality is rather weak: experiments mostly use 3–8B models, numbers for the same baseline differ markedly across papers, peak reporting, LLM judging and post-selection validation are widespread, and ranking conclusions among zero-data methods should be treated conservatively. Based on [^249][^248][^141].
- The strongest numbers come from loops that have reliable verifiers and use ground-truth answers or expert annotation (R1-Zero, DeepSeekMath-V2), and from test-time training on a single problem with continuous verifiable rewards (TTT-Discover), not from pure zero-data loops. In the near term, growth in weight-level self-improvement is more likely to come from expanding verifiable domains and scaling verification compute than from removing external anchors (our inference). Based on [^267][^250][^157].

## 7. Self-Improvement with Frozen Weights: Prompts, Context, Memory and Skills

This chapter covers self-improvement that does not update the main model's weights: experience is written into artifacts outside the model, including revision feedback, reflections, insights, prompts, context playbooks, executable skills and even memory architectures, and the same frozen model then continues working conditioned on these artifacts. For every method in this chapter, the main model's weights stay frozen within the improvement loop (our assessment). Partial exceptions are that DSPy can optionally fine-tune small models in a pipeline[^175] and that MemSkill uses reinforcement learning to train a lightweight MLP controller[^271]. The chapter is organized around three questions: where improvements are stored and how broad their scope is; whether the improver itself is also improved, and which fixed components remain at the outermost level; and whether the signal comes from execution and tests, environment rewards, labeled data, LLM judges or model self-evaluation. The overall assessment is that this line of work provides abundant evidence of bounded self-improvement; within-loop compounding appears only in the form of skill composition; and designs that "improve the improver" all climb only one meta level, with a fixed outermost operator and very short runs, so they do not yet constitute evidence of recursive (compounding) self-improvement (our assessment).

### 7.1 In-Context Self-Feedback: Self-Refine, Reflexion and Negotiation Self-Play

The earliest works established the most basic form: a frozen model uses language feedback to improve its outputs or strategy in context. They demonstrated feasibility and also exposed three constraints: a capability threshold, signal quality and rapid saturation (our assessment).

Self-Refine has the same LLM first produce a draft, then write feedback on its own output and revise accordingly; past feedback and outputs are concatenated into the prompt, for at most 4 iterations[^269]. On 7 tasks, with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, it improves on single-pass generation by the same model by about 20% absolute on average[^269]. Reflexion parameterizes the policy as "a memory encoding plus a set of LLM parameters": the agent reflects verbally on task feedback and stores the reflections in episodic memory for later trials, usually keeping only 1–3 of them because of context limits[^268]. The signal can be binary environment feedback, predefined heuristics, or self-evaluation such as LLM binary classification or self-written unit tests[^268]. Reflexion reaches 91% pass@1 on HumanEval, compared with GPT-4's previous 80%[^268]. Relative to strong baselines, it achieves a 22% absolute improvement on AlfWorld in 12 learning steps[^268]. Negotiation self-play brings the same idea into a multi-agent game: the history of previous rounds and feedback from an AI critic serve as in-context examples to iteratively improve one party's negotiation strategy, with the deal price as the metric[^219].

The capability threshold appears consistently across the three works. Self-Refine requires the base model to have sufficient few-shot or instruction-following ability, and Vicuna-13B clearly struggles in the revision step[^269]. Reflexion finds that the ability to give explicit self-corrections emerges only in stronger, larger models[^268]. In negotiation, only strong, well-aligned models such as gpt-4 and claude-v1.3 can keep improving, while weaker models either do not understand the rules or cannot make use of the feedback[^219]. Signal quality is the second constraint. Self-Refine's gains on math are limited because the model cannot judge whether an answer is wrong; if an external source points out the errors, the gains can exceed 5%[^269]. Reflexion's self-written tests have a false-positive rate of 16.3% on MBPP Python and 1.4% on HumanEval Python[^268]. Saturation is the third constraint. Self-Refine's marginal improvement diminishes with each iteration[^269]. Only gpt-4 can keep improving over 5 rounds, other models saturate after about 3 rounds, and better prices also come with a higher risk of the deal falling through[^219]. On WebShop, which requires creative exploration, Reflexion still shows no progress after four trials[^268]. In Meta-TTL's comparison on Jericho text games, Reflexion's mean W-AUC is lower than that of the non-learning Static baseline on ID games (0.173 vs. 0.195) and only slightly higher on OOD games (0.241 vs. 0.228)[^270].

This layer supports bounded self-improvement: what is improved is the output for a single instance or a few reflections within a single task, the scope ends when the task ends, the reflection procedure is handwritten by humans and never changes, and the bottleneck lies in judging "what went wrong" rather than in generation (our assessment, based on [^269][^268]).

### 7.2 Accumulating Experience: Skill Libraries, Insight Memory and Context Playbooks

The second wave of work extends the retained artifacts into libraries accumulated across tasks. This produces the clearest evidence of within-loop compounding in this chapter, namely skill composition; it also shows that whether accumulation helps is decided by the rules for writing, merging and forgetting, and in every paper these rules are designed by humans and kept fixed (our assessment).

**Executable skills.** Voyager consists of an automatic curriculum, a skill library of executable code, and an iterative prompting mechanism that incorporates environment feedback, execution errors and self-verification; it uses GPT-4 through black-box queries, and new skills are stored in a vector database after verification[^90]. Complex skills are composed of simpler programs, which the authors argue lets capabilities compound rapidly over time[^90]. Compared with the previous best method, it obtains 3.3 times more unique items and unlocks tech-tree milestones up to 15.3 times faster[^90]. Without the skill library, the agent tends to stall in later stages, and removing self-verification reduces the number of discovered items by 73%[^90]. GPT-4 obtains 5.7 times as many unique items as GPT-3.5, and the authors state explicitly that GPT-3.5 and open-source models cannot provide the required code quality[^90]. LATM has GPT-4 write Python tools and verify them with unit tests before handing them to GPT-3.5 to use, matching the performance of using GPT-4 in both roles at a significantly lower inference cost[^277]. However, each task has only 3/3/240 training/validation/test instances, GPT-3.5 Turbo as the tool maker fails on all of the hard tasks, and improving existing tools is left to future work[^277]. OS-Copilot's FRIDAY autonomously proposes a task curriculum; a critic uses an LLM to score new tools on a 0–10 scale and keeps only those scoring above 8[^181]. It achieves 40.86% on GAIA level-1, against a previous best of 30.3%[^181]. But with self-directed learning turned off, it already reaches 36.56[^181]. In other words, more than half of its lead already exists without learning (our assessment). SkillWeaver has GPT-4o explore each website for 160 iterations, distills the practice experience into Playwright APIs, uses an LLM reward to judge whether a task is completed, and checks the APIs with automatically generated tests[^154]. It improves relative success rates on WebArena and on real websites by 31.8% and 39.8%, respectively, and after enough exploration, compositional APIs that call multiple simple APIs also emerge[^154].

**Natural-language experience memory.** ExpeL collects experience on training tasks, maintains a list of insights with ADD, UPVOTE, DOWNVOTE and EDIT, and uses successful trajectories as examples at test time[^166]. It positions itself in contrast to Reflexion: instead of repeatedly retrying the same task, it relies on accumulation across tasks[^166]. Without repeated attempts, it reaches 59% on ALFWorld, surpassing the 54% of Reflexion's third round[^166]. In a HotpotQA ablation, ReAct scores 28.0, handwritten insights 32.0 and ExpeL 39.0[^166]. Dynamic Cheatsheet (DC) places memory on the test-time query stream: the same black-box model serves as both generator and curator, and the curator, which cannot see ground-truth labels, keeps only concise, transferable snippets[^275]. Claude 3.5 Sonnet rises from 23% to 50% on AIME 2024, and GPT-4o rises from about 10% to 99% on Game of 24 after discovering and reusing a Python solution[^275]. The key control is to append the full unfiltered history (FH): GPT-4o on AIME 2024 drops from a 20.0% baseline to 6.7% (the corresponding cell in Table 2 is 13.3, in the same direction), whereas DC-RS reaches 40.0%[^275]. ReasoningBank distills strategy items from successful and failed trajectories as judged by an LLM, appending new items directly without pruning[^148]. Compared with memory-free agents, it raises the overall WebArena success rate by +8.3, +7.2 and +4.6 across three backbones[^148]. On SWE-Bench-Verified, Gemini-2.5-flash's resolve rate rises from 34.2 to 38.8[^148]. In parallel scaling with k=5 on the WebArena-Shopping subset, memory-free Best-of-5 rises only from 39.0 to 42.2, whereas with ReasoningBank it rises from 49.7 to 55.1[^148].

**Context playbooks and semantic advantages.** Inspired by DC's agentic design, ACE splits adaptation into three roles—generator, reflector and curator—all using the same DeepSeek-V3.1, and the incremental items produced by the curator are merged deterministically by non-LLM logic[^89]. It names two failure modes: brevity bias (optimization collapsing into short, generic prompts, with GEPA as the authors' example) and context collapse (repeated wholesale rewriting gradually eroding details)[^89]. On AppWorld, wholesale rewriting leaves the context with 18,282 tokens and an accuracy of 66.7 at step 60; at the next step it shrinks to 122 tokens, with accuracy at 57.1, below the 63.7 of the no-adaptation baseline[^89]. ACE outperforms strong baselines by 10.6% on agent tasks and by 8.6% on finance tasks, and reduces adaptation latency by 86.9% on average[^89]. Training-Free GRPO moves GRPO's within-group comparison into the context: the parameters θ are frozen, and an LLM introspects on each group of rollouts to extract "semantic advantages", which are written into an external experience library[^276]. On a frozen DeepSeek-V3.1-Terminus with ReAct, using only 100 out-of-domain problems, zero gradient updates and about USD 18, it raises Mean@32 on AIME24/AIME25 from 80.0%/67.9% to 82.7%/73.3%[^276].

These systems are likewise constrained by the capability threshold and signal quality (our assessment). DC's gains are strongly correlated with model scale: small models struggle to exploit it, and erroneous heuristics that get mixed into memory are amplified just as much[^275]. Applied to QwQ-32B, Training-Free GRPO achieves only 25.5% pass@1, below the 27.5% of its ReAct baseline[^276]. Without reliable feedback (ground-truth labels or execution results), both ACE and DC may degrade, with the context polluted by misleading signals[^89]. FRIDAY's critic has difficulty judging whether subtasks are complete without ground truth, and ReasoningBank's LLM judging also introduces noise[^181][^148].

DC's full-history control and ACE's collapse case show from opposite ends that what decides whether accumulation helps is the curation rule: appending without curation dilutes insights, wholesale rewriting erases details, both may fall below the no-adaptation baseline, and itemized incremental updates are the common remedy (our assessment, based on [^275][^89]). These rules are designed by humans and fixed during a run; ReasoningBank only appends and never prunes, ExpeL acknowledges that lifelong learning would still require additional retrieval, and how memory grows and ages over longer time scales has not yet been tested (our assessment, based on [^148][^166]). As for compounding, the compositional skills of Voyager and SkillWeaver are a stacking of capability at the artifact level, with new skills using old ones as building blocks, but this is confined to a single environment or website, the curriculum and curation rules stay unchanged, and what expands is coverage rather than the rate of improvement (our assessment, based on [^90][^154]).

### 7.3 Prompt and Program Optimization: Searching a Discrete Text Space

Prompt optimization casts self-improvement as black-box optimization in a discrete text space: an LLM generates candidates, a labeled training, development or held-out set scores them, and the search algorithm is designed by humans and fixed. Its gains are reproducible and sample-efficient, while its ceiling is constrained by the label set, overfitting and the base model's knowledge (our assessment).

OPRO has an LLM read a meta-prompt containing previous solutions and their scores to generate new solutions, which are evaluated and then written back into the meta-prompt[^184]. The best prompts outperform human-designed prompts by up to 8% on GSM8K and by up to 50% on BBH[^184]. On zero-shot GSM8K scored with PaLM 2-L, "Take a deep breath and work on this problem step-by-step." scores 80.2, and "Let's think step by step." scores 71.8[^184]. EvoPrompt has GPT-3.5 act as the operators of a genetic algorithm (GA) or differential evolution (DE), improving a population of prompts based on development-set scores[^55]. It finds better prompts on all 22 BBH tasks, with the DE variant improving by up to 25% and by 3.5% on average[^55]. PromptAgent uses Monte Carlo tree search to plan prompt rewrites: gpt-3.5-turbo collects errors, gpt-4 provides error feedback as actions, and the reward is held-out-set performance[^51]. On BBH it achieves a 28.9% relative improvement over human-written prompts[^51]. DSPy abstracts LM pipelines as text-transformation graphs, and its compiler bootstraps demonstrations for each module, raising a simple program on GPT-3.5 from 33% to 82%[^175]. MIPRO optimizes each module's instructions and demonstrations without module-level labels or gradients; with Llama3-8B it leads on five of seven programs, by up to 13%[^56]. TextGrad treats an LLM's natural-language critiques as "textual gradients", with a domain-agnostic TGD optimizer updating the variables[^121]. In prompt optimization it is on par with DSPy on Word Sorting and GSM8k, and 7% higher on Object Counting[^121]. GEPA samples system trajectories, uses natural-language reflection to diagnose problems and propose prompt updates, and keeps the best-performing candidate for each instance on a Pareto frontier, with the weights never changing[^165]. On GPT-4.1 Mini, it raises the aggregate score from 53.03 to 65.22, compared with 58.67 for MIPROv2 and 59.14 for TextGrad[^165].

The strength of evidence for these gains varies (our assessment). OPRO's training accuracy is often 5%–20% higher than its test accuracy, and a training set is required to compute accuracy[^184]. For EvoPrompt on BBH, the development set used for selection is drawn from the test set, and the GPT-3.5 results come from only a single seed[^55]. PromptAgent's error feedback may inject instance-specific details into the task prompt[^51].

**Sample cost compared with weight-space RL.** This is the weightiest evidence in this section (our assessment). On six tasks with Qwen3 8B, GEPA's aggregate score is 54.85 (+9.62), GRPO's is 48.91 (+3.68), and the baseline's is 45.23[^165]. GEPA's budget per task ranges from 1,839 to 7,051 rollouts, versus 24,000 per task for GRPO[^165]. On IFBench, GEPA reaches 38.61% with 678 rollouts, while GRPO reaches 35.88% with 24,000[^165]. However, GEPA falls short of GRPO on AIME-2025 (32.00 vs. 38.00); the GRPO side uses LoRA, and most of GEPA's budget is spent on validation used only for candidate selection[^165]. Meta-TTL's reflective outer loop matches or exceeds a meta-agent trained with GRPO, using 350 versus 1,920 rollouts[^270]. In a matched Qwen3-8B setting, methods that update the actor's parameters, such as online SFT and online GRPO, yield smaller gains that are unstable across games[^270]. Training-Free GRPO, for its part, compares a frozen 671B model with RL methods trained on 32B models, which typically require thousands of training samples and cost more than USD 10,000[^276]. Since the two use different base models, this shows only the cost gap, not which is better on the same model (our assessment). Taken together, when the gap lies in how to invoke knowledge and skills the model already has (as in IFBench, HotpotQA and HoVer), optimization in context space is far more sample-efficient than the RL baselines in these comparisons (GRPO on LoRA or on small models), but when the gap lies in knowledge or reasoning ability itself, this advantage disappears: GEPA falls short of GRPO on AIME, and metaTextGrad also shows no significant gain on AIME 2024 with GPT-4o-mini (our assessment, based on [^165][^274]).

**Brevity or detail.** GEPA reports that its prompts are up to 9.2 times shorter than those of MIPROv2, and that, overall, better-performing optimizers produce shorter prompts[^165]. ACE calls this tendency brevity bias, and in the offline AppWorld setting it outperforms ReAct + GEPA by 11.9%[^89]. But ACE also acknowledges that HotPotQA often benefits more from concise high-level instructions, and that fixed-strategy games such as Game of 24 may need only a single reusable rule[^89]. Meta-TTL, for its part, finds that GEPA's prompts contain many task-specific facts and solutions, which help on seen tasks but are of less use on unseen tasks[^270]. The substance of the disagreement lies not in length but in whether the artifact preserves transferable procedural knowledge or task-specific facts (our inference). ACE's long playbooks win on agent and finance tasks that are dense in tool and domain knowledge, whereas GEPA's largest gains on Qwen3 8B occur on HotpotQA (multi-hop question answering) and HoVer (multi-hop fact verification), so the disagreement may stem mainly from task type (our inference, based on [^89][^165]).

The methods in this section modify only task prompts or program parameters, while meta-prompts, operators, search algorithms and compilers are all fixed, the only exception being MIPRO++'s meta-optimization of the proposal strategy (our assessment). The signal comes almost entirely from labeled data, which makes the gains measurable but also brings risks of selection leakage and overfitting (our assessment).

### 7.4 Improving the Improver: Only One Meta Level Up

A group of works treats the improver itself as the object of optimization, including mutation prompts, proposal strategies, optimizers, memory architectures, memory skills, self-improvement policies, verifiers and scoring metrics. Their common structure goes up exactly one level: the inner level improves task artifacts, the outer level improves the inner level's improvement procedure, and the outermost operator (usually a strong model plus a fixed prompt) stays unchanged (our assessment).

**Mutation prompts and optimizers.** Promptbreeder simultaneously evolves task prompts and the mutation prompts that rewrite them; the latter are generated by the LLM in a self-referential way and improved during evolution[^30]. First-order hypermutation concatenates the hypermutation prompt "Please summarize and improve the following instruction:" with a mutation prompt to generate a new mutation prompt[^30]. The population has 50 units and is typically evolved for 20–30 generations, with fitness evaluated on random training batches[^30]. On zero-shot GSM8K with PaLM 2-L, it reaches 83.9% with the prompt "SOLUTION", above OPRO's 80.2%[^30]. Removing any single self-referential operator is harmful in almost all cases, but the prompt topology stays fixed and only the prompt content is adapted[^30]. MIPRO++ parameterizes the proposal strategy and selects it with a Bayesian model; it outperforms MIPRO on ScoNe and HotPotQA and is roughly on par on HoVer[^56]. metaTextGrad points out that LLM-based optimizers are hand-designed by humans and are not themselves optimized[^274]. It uses o1 as a meta-optimizer that rewrites the prompts of optimizers such as TGD and ADAS-TG and decides their combination and order[^274]. On GPT-4o-mini, the average validation/test accuracy across four benchmarks is 0.56/0.53, versus 0.49/0.47 for zero-shot MIPROv2[^274]. In one meta-optimization cycle, the meta-optimizer layer consumes only about 2.5k tokens, against about 400k for the program layer[^274].

**Memory architectures and memory skills.** MemEvolve is bilevel: the inner loop populates an experience base under a fixed memory system, while the outer loop meta-learns the memory architecture itself, that is, the program implementations of its four modules for encoding, storage, retrieval and management[^272]. Candidate architectures are ranked by non-dominated sorting on task success rate, token consumption and latency, and GPT-5-mini serves as the meta-evolution operator[^272]. It reports gains of up to 17.06%, but the bilevel evolution runs for only 3 rounds, each round keeps only one architecture and expands it into 3 offspring, and each candidate is evaluated on only 60 trajectories[^272]. MemSkill recasts memory-construction operations as evolvable skills: starting from four basic skills, Insert, Update, Delete and Skip, an LLM designer reviews hard cases every 100 training steps and revises the skills[^271]. The designer itself is fixed; in the ablation, "removing the designer" means fixing the skill library to the four initial primitives[^271]. In the LoCoMo ablation (LLM-judge scores), the full method scores 53.82/54.14 on LLaMA/Qwen, falling to 46.50/36.15 without the designer[^271].

**Self-improvement policies.** Meta-TTL argues that Reflexion- and Self-Refine-style verbal feedback and memory accumulation are all hand-designed self-improvement policies[^270]. In its inner loop, a meta-agent rewrites the system prompt of a frozen Gemini-3-Flash actor between episodes; in its outer loop, a proposer generates candidate meta-prompts, which replace the current one only when their balanced improvement score (BIS) on validation tasks is positive[^270]. With the same backbone, its average W-AUC on ID tasks exceeds the strongest baseline by 23% on Jericho and by 12% on WebArena-Lite[^270]. A "naive meta-agent" with the same architecture and model but an unoptimized self-improvement policy averages a W-AUC of 0.286 on Jericho's OOD games, against 0.414 for Meta-TTL[^270]. This gap can be attributed directly to the learned improvement policy (our assessment). Meta-training runs for 12–15 rounds at an API cost of about USD 30–70, but the ID evaluation on Jericho reuses the meta-training games[^270].

**Verifiers and scoring metrics.** CoEvoSkills co-evolves a skill generator with a surrogate verifier: once the surrogate tests pass, a hidden ground-truth oracle reruns the skill in a fresh environment and returns only a single opaque pass/fail bit[^273]. On SkillsBench, its pass rate is 71.1%, against 30.6% for the no-skill baseline and 53.5% for human-curated skills[^273]. Without the surrogate verifier it drops to 41.1%, and two baselines with one-shot self-generated skills reach only 32.0% and 30.7%[^273]. The evolution curve matches the no-skill baseline at round 0, overtakes human-curated skills at round 3 with 63%, and converges to 75% at round 5[^273]. But this single bit decides during evolution whether a skill is deployed, and another 10 tasks never converged within the budget[^273].

MetaSkill-Evolve explicitly targets recursion. The authors argue that existing skill evolution improves only task skills while the improvement procedure is written once and then fixed, so it is self-improvement rather than recursive self-improvement[^122]. It gives the meta-skill m=(ψ,σ,α,π,ε) the same Markdown format as task skills and has the same five-agent pipeline rewrite it on a slow loop, which the authors call "bounded one-level recursion"[^122]. The five agents share one frozen Gemma-4 31B backbone[^122]. On held-out tests, it beats the single-level evolution baseline by +6.38/+8.05/+1.92 points on OfficeQA/SealQA/ALFWorld, respectively[^122]. A meta-update interval of H=2 works best; on OfficeQA, going from H=2 to H=8 costs 9.1 points[^122]. But the pipeline's roles and connections are fixed, the default run has only five iterations and two meta-updates, the evaluation covers only three selected benchmarks, and "a fixed meta-process will stagnate" is only a hypothesis[^122].

Double Ratchet (the paper is titled "Who Grades the Grader?") makes the scoring metric itself evolve: it searches a space of compositions of small detectors, uses agreement with ten anchor examples as the selection criterion, and never lets metric evolution read the locked test set[^278]. On MBPP+, the evolved metric's agreement with hidden ground truth rises by 0.21 (p=0.014), beating the bare LLM judge it contains[^278]. Across three tasks, it retains 88–110% of the improvement that ground truth or a human-written rubric brings[^278]. The key finding is negative: without the anchor guards, the metric collapses into an always-pass detector, yet it trains skills just as well as the normal metric, so downstream task scores cannot validate a self-evolving evaluator[^278]. The report-grading rubric hand-written by the authors was also gamed by the skills within a hundred rounds, and an independent external judge preferred the baseline in 88% of valid pairs[^278].

**Our assessment for this section.** Every design rises exactly one level and keeps the outermost layer fixed: Promptbreeder's hypermutation prompts and topology, metaTextGrad's o1 meta-optimizer, MemEvolve's meta-evolution operator, Meta-TTL's proposer and BIS criterion, and MetaSkill-Evolve's pipeline roles and interval H are all left unchanged, and none of the papers demonstrates a second meta level (our assessment, based on [^30][^274][^122]). The increment from going one level up is positive but moderate in size, and it comes from very short runs (our assessment, based on [^272][^122][^270]). The outer operator is often a stronger model, and metaTextGrad's meta-optimizer, for instance, requires o1 or Claude-3.5-Sonnet, so "the improvement of the improver" amounts to a considerable extent to injecting a strong model's judgment into the loop (our inference, based on [^274]). MetaSkill-Evolve, whose two levels share the same backbone and pipeline, is the design closest to self-application, but the evidence is still thin (our assessment). The evaluator is the weakest link at this level: in most systems the evaluator sits outside the loop and is not modified, serving as the anchor that keeps self-improvement from degenerating into self-deception (our assessment). Once CoEvoSkills and Double Ratchet pull the evaluator into the loop, they must place a harder anchor further out, namely a one-bit hidden oracle, or ten anchor examples, a locked test set and a stronger judge outside the loop (our assessment, based on [^273][^278]). Each additional meta level requires an external reference that does not evolve with the system, and so far this has been shown to be feasible for only one level (our assessment).

### 7.5 Cross-Model Transfer and Persistence of Text Artifacts

The frozen-weights route has a property that weight updates lack: the improvement lives in readable, portable text or code and can be detached from the model that produced it. Several papers report positive transfer, but transfer mostly occurs from strong to weak models or between frontier models of the same generation and depends on the consumer's capability, and task-specific content transfers poorly (our assessment).

The early evidence is "strong model builds, weak model uses": tools made by GPT-4 bring a GPT-3.5 user up to the level reached when GPT-4 fills both roles[^277]. In SkillWeaver, APIs synthesized by a strong agent improve a weaker GPT-4o-mini agent on WebArena by up to 54.3%[^154]. Voyager can use its learned skill library to solve new tasks from scratch in a new Minecraft world[^90]. Later work reports transfer across model families. MemSkill is trained only with LLaMA, and its skills transfer to Qwen without retraining[^271]. Memory systems evolved by MemEvolve with GPT-5-Mini transfer to Kimi K2 and DeepSeek V3.2 without manual adaptation, with Kimi K2 gaining 17.06% on WebWalkerQA[^272]. Skills evolved by CoEvoSkills with Claude Opus 4.6 transfer to six other models from five companies, each gaining about 35–44 percentage points over its no-skill baseline[^273].

Transfer does not always work either: when optimizers that metaTextGrad optimized on GPT-4o-mini are used with Claude 3 Haiku, Dyck test accuracy is 0.35 against 0.34 for ADAS-TG, and on the validation set it is 0.32 against 0.35[^274]. This amounts to almost no advantage over ADAS-TG (our assessment). PromptAgent's expert-level prompts may significantly degrade smaller models such as GPT-2 and LLaMA[^51]. FRIDAY's performance may change drastically when the underlying LLM is swapped[^181]. Many of the hand-designed memory systems that MemEvolve reimplements fail to bring stable improvements, and ExpeL underperforms on all three benchmarks[^272].

The scope of persistence varies widely (our assessment). Self-Refine concatenates past feedback and outputs into the current prompt[^269]. The paper does not describe any cross-task memory (our assessment). Reflexion keeps 1–3 reflections across multiple trials of the same task[^268]. During evolution, CoEvoSkills keeps skills only within the current task and deploys them to the target agent only after they pass the oracle[^273]. ExpeL, DC and ReasoningBank, by contrast, accumulate across tasks[^166][^275][^148]. Artifacts that persist across tasks have direct implications for inference infrastructure (our assessment). The DC authors note that sequential memory updates create difficulties for large-scale parallel or batch tasks that require independent inference[^275]. ACE's contexts are longer than GEPA's, but the authors argue that most of the context can be reused through the KV cache, so that serving cost and GPU memory do not grow linearly with it[^89]. Part of the learning cost thus turns into an inference-side state-management problem, including prefix caching, memory versioning and concurrency consistency (our inference). Readability is something artifacts have and weight updates lack (our assessment). Meta-TTL's outer loop does not rely on gradients, and what it produces is a readable meta-prompt rather than model weights[^270]. What Double Ratchet evolves is an inspectable expression rather than an opaque judge[^278]. But readable does not mean reliable (our assessment). In online evaluation, SkillWeaver still requires humans to verify the safety of every action before execution[^154].

The transfer evidence supports a more precise description: what this route mainly produces is portable procedural knowledge, whose quality ceiling is set by the model that produces it and whose usefulness depends on the model that consumes it (our assessment, based on [^154][^273][^271]). If artifacts can be preserved across generations, each model generation could keep accumulating on top of the previous generation's artifact library, but no paper in this chapter tests multi-generation accumulation, nor whether old artifacts help or add noise on stronger models (our assessment).

### 7.6 Summary (our assessment)

The points below summarize this chapter (our assessment); each gives the literature it rests on.

- **Self-improvement with frozen weights is real and cheap, but bounded.** Gains are concentrated in a single task or domain and usually saturate within a few to a dozen or so rounds; it amplifies existing capabilities rather than creating them, and weak models generally cannot benefit. Based on [^219][^269][^268]; see also [^276][^274][^90].
- **The main constraint is signal quality, not generative capability.** Self-evaluation misses math errors, self-written tests give false positives, and the context gets polluted without reliable feedback; gains are more stable where execution, tests or ground truth are available. Based on [^269][^268][^89].
- **Cross-task accumulation provides this chapter's clearest evidence of within-loop compounding, but the curation rules are a fixed, hidden improver.** Skill composition appears in two systems; the write and merge rules decide whether accumulation helps or hurts, and these rules are all designed by humans. Based on [^90][^154]; the point on curation rules is based on [^275][^89].
- **At small budgets, context-space optimization is clearly more sample-efficient than the weight-space RL in these comparisons (GRPO with LoRA or on small models), but it cannot fill gaps in knowledge or reasoning ability.** Based on [^165][^270][^276]; for a counterexample, see [^274].
- **"Improving the improver" currently goes exactly one level deep.** The outermost operator is fixed, the runs are only a few rounds long, and the increment over single-level baselines is positive but moderate; there is no second meta level, and no operator that can rewrite its own top-level procedure. Based on [^30][^274][^272]; see also [^270][^271][^122].
- **Evaluator integrity is a constraint on recursion.** Once the evaluator enters the loop, an anchor that does not evolve with the system must be placed further out; downstream task scores cannot validate a self-evolving evaluator, and it takes only on the order of a hundred rounds for proxy metrics to be gamed. Based on [^278][^273].
- **Artifacts are transferable, leaving a channel for cross-generation accumulation, but this remains untested.** Skills and memory architectures can transfer across model families, mostly from strong to weak, while task-specific artifacts transfer poorly; there are no multi-generation accumulation experiments yet. Based on [^154][^273][^272]; see also [^271][^270].
- **Implications for the review's main thread.** The evidence in this chapter supports "bounded self-improvement" fairly strongly, with several papers running ablations that isolate the artifact or the meta level; its support for "recursive (compounding) self-improvement" is weak: there is compositional compounding within the loop, but no accelerating returns across meta levels, and the ceiling is set jointly by the frozen backbone and the external evaluator. Based on [^122][^90][^278].

## 8. Rewriting Their Own Code: Self-Modification of Agents, Harnesses and Workflows

The strand discussed in this chapter is the one most often labeled "RSI" outright in 2026. What gets modified here is not the model weights but the program that wraps the model: tools, prompts, control flow, memory, workflows and runtime code. The 2026 literature refers to all of these collectively as the "harness", the term this review uses throughout. This strand is often seen as a shortcut to RSI because the improver itself is also a piece of code that calls an LLM; as long as a system can rewrite code, it can in principle also rewrite the code responsible for the rewriting (our assessment).

This chapter organizes the 48 papers read in full (our count) by time and design variable to answer three questions:

1. Under what conditions is the improver itself modified? What evidence shows that such modification works?
2. What is still fixed at the outermost layer of each system?
3. Do gains compound across generations?

### 8.1 Lineage (2022–2025): From Rewriting One's Own Source Code to Open-Ended Archives

**Rewriting model source code.** The earliest attempts targeted the model's own source code directly. Self-Programming AI has a code-generating language model rewrite its own source code, which the authors describe as the first practical self-programming AI[^73]. Candidate source code is selected using short-run training loss as a proxy, and the best candidate replaces the current version[^73]. Its limitations are typical: self-improvement depends on a seed dataset of related code, the search is a greedy search over random code modifications, and its results on CIFAR-10 fall far below SoTA[^73].

**STOP: rewriting the improver.** STOP (Self-Taught Optimizer) shifts the object of modification from model source code to a harness program, the "improver". A seed improver that calls an LM to improve programs is run on its own code while the LM stays unchanged throughout, which is why the authors state explicitly that this is not full RSI[^167]. In this process, GPT-4 proposed known search strategies such as beam search, genetic algorithms and simulated annealing[^167]. On the LPN task, the GPT-4-driven improver raised test meta-utility within 1–3 rounds; for comparison, the CoT baseline's meta-utility is 57.7% and the greedy iterative improver's is 64.2%[^167]. Without further optimization, the improver optimized on LPN (T=4) beats the seed improver on five unseen tasks, for example raising 3SAT from 21.2% to 75.1%[^167].

STOP also produced three kinds of negative evidence that have recurred in this strand ever since:

- **Model dependence**: the method works for GPT-4 but degrades with GPT-3.5 and Mixtral[^167]; with GPT-3.5, only 12% of runs improved by 3% or more[^167].
- **Safety**: fewer than 1% of improvements included attempts to disable the sandbox, with a rate of 0.42% for GPT-4[^167].
- **Reward hacking**: misformatted outputs once produced an "accuracy" of more than 1000%[^167].

**Design search by a fixed meta-agent.** The mainstream approach in 2024 was to have a fixed meta-agent search over agent designs:

- GPTSwarm represents agents as computational graphs, with an optimizer tuning node prompts and edge connections[^288].
- ADAS (Automated Design of Agentic Systems) has a meta-agent write new agents in code and keep exploring over an ever-growing historical archive[^43]. The agents it discovers raise DROP F1 by 13.6/100 and MGSM accuracy by 14.4%[^43]. Transferred to GSM8K and GSM-Hard, they improve by 25.9% and 13.2%, respectively[^43].
- AFlow formulates workflow search as Monte Carlo tree search over workflows represented as code[^49]. It beats manual designs by 5.7% on average and automated methods by 19.5%, and lets smaller models surpass GPT-4o at 4.55% of GPT-4o's cost[^49].
- AgentSquare searches a space made up of four modules, planning, reasoning, tool use and memory, and beats the best human designs by 17.2% on average across six benchmarks[^287].
- MaAS (2025) no longer searches for a single optimal system; instead it optimizes an "agentic supernet", a probability distribution over agent architectures, and samples a system from it for each query[^289]. Its inference cost is only 6%–45% of that of existing handcrafted or automated multi-agent systems[^289].

Two points about this stage are worth noting:

- **The meta-agent itself is fixed.** ADAS lists "making the meta-agent self-referential" (higher-order ADAS) as future work[^43].
- **Gains do not necessarily come from search.** W4S finds that ADAS's sequential archive search is no better than 30 random samples on MGSM[^285]; MASS's analysis, for its part, holds that prompts are the component that matters most[^286].
- **Cost is a hard constraint.** One ADAS run on ARC costs about USD 500[^43]; in AgentSquare, evaluating a single CoT agent on ALFWorld with GPT-4o already costs about USD 60[^287].

**Gödel Agent: the improvement module itself is modifiable.** Gödel Agent makes the improvement module itself rewritable as well: the agent uses monkey patching to read and rewrite its own running code, including the part that performs the rewriting[^64]. In the setting evaluated with GPT-3.5, it matches or exceeds Meta Agent Search on all tasks (11% higher on MGSM)[^64]; 30 self-improvement iterations cost about USD 15, against about USD 300 for Meta Agent Search[^64].

Its robustness data reveal the direct cost of "improver self-modification". Across 100 MGSM trials, 4% terminated unexpectedly, 92% experienced temporary drops, and 14% ended up worse than the initial policy[^64]; the unexpected terminations were caused precisely by modifications to the improvement module[^64]. The authors also acknowledge that the unconstrained variant's large gains come mainly from calling a stronger model (GPT-4o) rather than from better design[^64].

**SICA.** SICA removes the division between meta-agent and target agent: the best-performing agent in the archive becomes the meta-agent for the next round and edits its own codebase[^195]. On a random subset of SWE-Bench Verified, over 15 iterations, the score rose from 17% to 53% at an API cost of about USD 7,000[^195]. On reasoning tasks, however, it saturated: the system averaged 76% on AIME/GPQA, whereas o3-mini alone already scores 87%/79%, showing that the harness adds almost nothing when the base model is strong enough[^195].

**Darwin Gödel Machine.** The Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) introduces an open-ended archive. Agents rewrite their own code, and every modification is tested on coding benchmarks; all agents form an ever-growing tree, from which parents are sampled for open-ended exploration[^32]. The results after 80 iterations[^32] were as follows:

- SWE-bench rose from 20.0% to 50.0% and Polyglot from 14.2% to 30.7%, outperforming ablations without self-improvement or without open-ended exploration[^32].
- The path to the best agent passes through weaker nodes, showing that "stepping stones" have value[^32].
- The discovered agents transfer to other base models: o3-mini goes from 23.0% to 33.0%, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet from 19.0% to 59.5%[^32].
- Cost: one SWE-bench run takes about two weeks and about USD 22,000, and the results remain bounded by the frozen base model[^32].

DGM's "the improver is the improved" rests on a premise: that gains in coding ability are gains in self-modification ability. The authors list "coding benchmarks reflect self-improvement ability" as an assumption of the method[^32].

**Huxley-Gödel Machine.** HGM questions precisely this premise. It points out that an agent's benchmark score is a poor indicator of its self-improvement potential (the "metaproductivity–performance mismatch"), and therefore scores nodes by the performance of their descendants (the clade) instead[^79].

- On SWE-Verified-60, the weighted correlation between the HGM estimator and empirical metaproductivity is 0.778, compared with only 0.285 and 0.444 for the selection criteria of DGM and SICA[^79].
- Starting from the same initial agent and with 800 evaluations, HGM reaches 56.7% on SWE-Verified-60 and 30.5% on Polyglot[^79]; on Polyglot it uses 6.86 times fewer CPU hours than DGM[^79].
- The authors also caution that benchmark overfitting may be present. On SWE-Lite, the evolved agent scores 57 on the standard split (the best SWE-agent on the leaderboard scores 56.7)[^79]; but on a filtered split containing only unseen tasks, it is 47.8 versus 48.3[^79].

**Live-SWE-agent: on-the-fly adaptation.** Live-SWE-agent goes to the other extreme: instead of doing any offline training, it creates and modifies tools on the fly within each task[^36]. It reaches 77.4% on SWE-bench Verified (Gemini 3 Pro)[^36]. On the same 60-problem subset, it scores 65.0% with 0 offline hours, while SICA, DGM (1231 hours) and HGM (512 hours) score 50.0%, 53.3% and 56.7%, respectively[^36]. This comparison indicates that the large amount of compute spent on offline self-improvement has not translated into an advantage over "on-the-fly adaptation" (our assessment, based on [^36]). However, the tools it evolves are discarded at the end of each task, and a weaker model (GPT-5-Nano) gets stuck in loops when building tools[^36].

### 8.2 Making the Improver Itself Modifiable: The 2026 Designs

Work in 2026 advanced along two directions: first, improving the selection and search mechanisms, that is, deciding which node to expand and with what signal; second, bringing the meta-agent or optimizer itself into the modifiable scope.

**Selection mechanisms.**

- **Group-Evolving Agents (GEA)** changes the unit of evolution from isolated branches in a DGM-style tree to groups of agents that share experience[^128]. With the same number of evolved agents, SWE-bench Verified rises from 20.0% to 71.0% (DGM: 56.7%), and Polyglot from 38.2% to 88.3% (DGM: 68.3%)[^128]. The cost is about USD 13,000 per SWE-bench run[^128]. Because of budget limits, the first 20 iterations were driven by Claude Haiku 4.5, and the last 10 switched to Claude Sonnet 4.5[^128].
- **Mendel Gödel Machine** adds comparative mutation operators to DGM/HGM-style search: reaction-norm mutation using cross-task trajectories, and cross-lineage hybridization using a reference agent's trajectories[^279]. Starting from the same initial agent, on a 60-problem subset, it reaches 78.3% on SWE-bench Verified (HGM 73.3%, starting point 68.3%) and 93.2% on Polyglot (HGM 77.9%, starting point 50.8%)[^279].
- **SIFT** argues that evaluation itself is the bottleneck. It ranks candidates with pairwise LLM judging (Bradley–Terry) and reserves full benchmark evaluation for promising nodes[^9]. On the full Polyglot, it reaches 31.1% at a cost of USD 34.3, 6.71 hours and 224 CPU hours[^9]; HGM reaches 30.5% with 347 CPU hours[^9].

**Hyperagents: an editable improver.** Hyperagents (DGM-H) merges the task agent and the meta-agent into a single editable program, so that the improvement procedure itself can be rewritten[^42].

- In domains beyond coding, it raises paper reviewing from 0.0 to 0.710 and robotics reward design from 0.060 to 0.372[^42]; the original DGM scores 0.0 on both[^42].
- When transferred to unseen domains, the hyperagent achieves an imp@50 of 0.630, against 0.0 for the transferred DGM meta-agent[^42]. This is one of the most direct pieces of evidence in this strand that "improvements to the improver can transfer" (our assessment, based on [^42]).
- But its outermost layer is still fixed: the task distribution, parent selection and evaluation protocol all stay unchanged. In an appendix experiment, the self-modified parent selector did not beat the hand-designed version and also showed degenerate behavior such as a 99% acceptance rate[^42].

**IDM: predict before modifying.** IDM adds an editable "Improvement Dynamics Model" on top of Hyperagents. It predicts the task-level outcomes of a modification before evaluation and then updates itself based on the observed outcomes[^292]. IDM outperforms Hyperagents in all four domains, by up to 46% averaged over generations[^292]; the final prediction loss falls by 8.7–24.4%[^292].

**Co-evolving evaluation.**

- **Escher-Loop** co-evolves two populations, task programs and optimizer agents; each optimizer is scored by relative wins and losses (Elo) derived from the task gains it brings[^82]. On Kissing Number and Circle Packing it surpasses the ceiling of OpenEvolve's hand-designed optimizer, but these gains cannot be reproduced once a single best optimizer is frozen[^82].
- **Red Queen Gödel Machine (RQGM)** brings the evaluator itself into the search. The evaluator is frozen within each epoch; at an epoch boundary, a challenger evaluator replaces it only if it performs better on held-out ground truth[^206]. It reaches 82.1% on the DeepSWE held-out set (HGM-H: 75.0%) and 71.7% on Polyglot with fewer search tokens, against 69.9% for the control[^206]. The authors also note that the evaluator drifts when the anchor is weak, and that its capability ceiling is bounded by the anchor[^206].

**When improver self-evolution helps.**

- **VERSE** provides the clearest controlled experiments: optimizer self-evolution without verification actually made Meta-Harness worse (the validation set selected round 0)[^194]; only after execution-based verification was added did it obtain the best results of the whole study[^194]. Its best harness scores 42.3% on the held-out SWE-rebench and 37.7% on cross-language OOD tasks[^194]; the strongest baselines score 39.2% and 29.3%, respectively[^194].
- **SpecRSI** drafts successor agents before the predecessor has finished evaluation, but adopts only fully verified versions, and trains the improver across rounds[^123]. Its multi-round experiments give the clearest diminishing-returns curve in this strand: per-round increments of 8.5, 6.4, 4.7 and 2.1 percentage points, for a cumulative 21.7[^123]. By round 5, the continually updated improver is 7.9 percentage points ahead of a fixed improver[^123].
- **Evo²** trains the "evolver" itself with SFT plus interleaved GRPO on the execution outcomes of harness edits[^284]. It reaches 82.1% on held-out Polyglot and 48.3% on SWE-bench Verified[^284]; the strongest baselines reach 62.6% and 41.0%, respectively[^284].
- **OEO (open-ended optimization)** fixes the objective, permitted interactions, resource budget, data boundaries and evaluation, and lets the optimizer organize only the improvement process itself, online[^291]. Across 14 comparisons over 8 benchmark–target-model settings, OEO driven by GPT-5.5 wins 12, ties 1 and narrowly loses 1 by 0.21 percentage points[^291]. But this delegation has a capability frontier: with a mid-strength optimizer, SkillOpt, which follows a preset procedure, does better instead, and weak optimizers cannot work through the same interface[^291].

**Structural depth and multi-operator orchestration.**

- **Meta^n** argues that earlier self-editors had to leave part of their editing machinery untouched in order to stay stable, so the meta-depth they actually realized was only about two levels[^4]. Its approach is to fix the meta-operation and recurse on ever-growing inputs[^4]. Depth saturates at 3–6 levels, because the meta-operation can no longer find improvements[^4]. Ablations show that about 72% of the gain comes from the context strings passed along, and only about 15% from the transfer of callable code[^4].
- **MetaRSI (RSI²)** combines three classes of operators, data, harness and model: a scheduler chooses the order of operators and rewrites each operator's proposal strategy, and a meta level in turn revises the scheduler[^102]. It beats the strongest fixed pipeline by 3.6 points and raises SWE-bench Pro from 10.3 to 19.5[^102]. Notably, the "law" the paper itself draws is that closed loops are constrained by verification, not by capability; no closed loop creates capability, and all gains are imported from outside[^102].
- **RSIFlow** has a meta-agent diagnose the task agent's bottlenecks, choose among three classes of improvement workflows (model, harness and artifact), and then distill the results into skills and principles that it uses to evolve its own meta-harness[^145]. It achieves the best result in 11 of 12 settings[^145]; switching off the meta-agent's evolution costs 5.74%[^145].

**Long-running systems.** Ouroboros is a long-running self-developing system: tools, context assembly, prompts and core code become the runtime for subsequent work through reviewed commits[^149]. It scores 86.97% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (86.74% after audit)[^149]; model costs for the 161-day deployment came to USD 110.6K[^149]. The audit found one reward-hacking trial and one case of SWE-bench Pro data contamination that would reverse the original gap[^149].

**AIDE²: research agents and the ignition test.** AIDE² applies this paradigm to research agents. The agent proposes modifications to its own code, which are evaluated on AI R&D tasks, and the best version is kept according to a hidden evaluation; each accepted rewrite becomes the agent edited in the next round[^1]. An 8-day run accepted 7 rewrites[^1]; the reward-hacking rate fell from 55% to 32% without any dedicated optimization[^1].

Its "ignition test" is one of the rare experiments in this strand that test compounding directly: using the self-improved agent as the outer-loop driver yields 0.780, versus 0.782 for the human-designed agent (three seeds), with no clear winner[^1].

### 8.3 The Harness Turn: Freeze the Model, Rewrite the Runtime

In 2026, a large body of work adopted "freeze the model, change only the harness" as the default setting. One reason is that the weights of closed-source frontier models are unavailable; another is that harness changes are readable and can be rolled back (our inference).

**Meta-Harness.** An outer-loop proposer (Claude Code + Opus-4.6) searches over harness code with filesystem access to the code, scores and raw traces of all previous candidates[^17].

- On online text classification it beats ACE by 7.7 points with 4 times fewer context tokens[^17]; on 200 IMO-level problems, it improves five held-out models by 4.7 points on average[^17].
- On TerminalBench-2 it reaches 76.4% with Opus 4.6[^17].
- Ablations show that raw traces are critical: median accuracy is 50.0 with full access, 34.6 with scores only, and 34.9 with scores plus summaries[^17].
- But its search and final evaluation on TerminalBench-2 share the same set of 89 tasks, which makes it a "discovery" setting rather than a held-out evaluation[^17].

**AHE (Agentic Harness Engineering).** AHE puts observability at its core: components are editable file by file, trajectory evidence is distilled, and every edit carries a falsifiable self-prediction that is checked in the next round[^174].

- Ten iterations raise pass@1 on Terminal-Bench 2 from 69.7% to 77.0% (the Codex harness scores 71.9%)[^174].
- The gains come from tools, middleware and memory rather than prompts. Components do not stack additively: the individual gains sum to 11.1 percentage points, but combined they yield only 7.3[^174].
- Its "regression blind spot" is the most noteworthy: its fix predictions reach about 5 times the random rate, while its regression predictions reach only about 2 times (precision 11.8%), and most regressions cannot be foreseen[^174].

**Self-Harness.** The same model improves the harness it runs in, without help from a stronger external agent; the model, evaluator and protocol are all fixed[^34]. The 9 model×benchmark combinations improve on both held-in and held-out tasks; for example, on the Terminal-Bench-2.0 held-out set, MiniMax rises from 40.5% to 61.9%[^34]. But the authors state explicitly that this is bounded editing on fixed benchmarks, not open-ended self-improvement[^34].

**RHO: removing labels.** RHO re-solves a diverse subset of past tasks, diagnoses with self-verification and self-consistency, and then selects harness modifications using the agent's own pairwise preferences[^280]. In one round, without any external scoring, it raises SWE-Bench Pro from 0.59 to 0.78[^280]. For comparison, Meta-Harness with labels reaches 0.62 after one round and 0.80 after ten rounds (3.1 times the calls)[^280]. But RHO ran only one round and does not claim compounding; the authors also acknowledge that self-evaluation may entrench errors[^280].

**Self-Evolving Harness.** The same frozen model first solves tasks on the same version of the harness and then, as the proposer, edits the harness that runs it[^191]. Starting from a 49-line seed, it gains 4.48 points in distribution and 12.64 points on five OOD benchmarks[^191]. But it has no verification gate: any version that runs is accepted[^191].

**DarwinX: population evolution and overfitting.** DarwinX evolves the harness with a population, keeping the model frozen and accepting only variants that do not regress[^282]. It raises Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 75.5% to 83.2% (GPT-5.5)[^282]. It also provides the clearest overfitting data for harness evolution:

- The training-subset score saturates at 1.000, while the held-out set reaches only 68.3%, a gap of 31.7 points[^282].
- Cross-model transfer is also imperfect: the held-out score of GPT-5.5 is 56.1%, below Terminus-2's 61.0%[^282].

**RRSI: regularization and re-evaluation.** RRSI specifically addresses overfitting: recursive harness evolution overfits the limited evolve set, so it regularizes with an annealed edit budget, a critic and a pruner[^31]. It improves by up to 6.0 on the evolve split and by up to 4.3 on five OOD benchmarks, with 30% fewer policy tokens[^31]. More important is its re-evaluation of existing methods: out of distribution, Meta-Harness improves by only 0.9 points, and AHE and TTHE even fall below the original harness[^31].

**Settings without a verifier.**

- **Harness²** targets open-ended tasks without verifiers and performs a two-level recursion: "propose–probe–compose" at the task level, and updates at the harness-improvement level[^67]. The largest single-step gain is 11.5 percentage points[^67]. But without ground truth, refinement can rely only on behavioral comparisons and model judgments, and it is sensitive to both variance and shared biases[^67].
- **RHI** writes the harness as a prompt-level specification and iterates over its own revision history using pairwise LLM feedback[^108]. On 30 synthetic ML research tasks, it lets an agent at low reasoning effort outperform the high-effort setting at up to 60% lower inference cost[^108]. But all evaluations are pairwise LLM preferences, and the training (internalization) half of the closed loop was not completed[^108].

### 8.4 Joint Evolution of Harness and Weights

The ceiling of pure harness methods is set by the frozen model, so a number of works on jointly evolving the harness and weights appeared in mid-2026.

**SIA.** A feedback agent chooses between harness updates and RL weight updates (LoRA)[^33]. On LawBench, the score goes from 13.5% initially to 50.0% with harness changes only, and then to 70.1% with both combined[^33]. The authors point out that both levers optimize the same fixed verifier, so the joint fixed point may be fragile, that is, "coupled Goodhart"[^33].

**HASE.** Through agentic RL, the same model both writes solutions and edits whitelisted harness components; it is allowed to edit the evaluator only when the local proxy and the true oracle disagree[^211].

- On the Heilbronn problem, the score stalls at 0.021880 with frozen weights, while full joint evolution reaches 0.036082[^211].
- Circle packing reaches 2.635983, but before that the evaluator was exploited twice and subsequently repaired[^211].

**Co-Harness.** It alternates between two things: patching the harness, and running SFT on trajectories produced by the improved harness[^155]. After two rounds, it gains 20.4 percentage points on average on AIME24/25 and HMMT25[^155]. But the 8B model saturates after the first round, with AIME24 rising only from 84.0% to 84.7%[^155].

**Harness-R1 and HELIX.**

- Harness-R1 trains a dedicated 9B "harness engineer", rewarded by the success rate of reruns after patching, while the target agent stays frozen[^281]. The average success rate rises from 44.3% to 53.6%[^281], but only single-step adaptation was performed[^281].
- HELIX proposes a "build–update–rebuild" closed loop, but ran only the build half and trained no model[^78].

**A key counterexample: on-policy correction.** One joint-evolution study found that imitating expert trajectories under the evolved harness actually made a weak model regress by 4 to 30 points on all seven tasks[^283]. Only when the expert rewrote the failed turn in the weak model's own rollout (on-policy correction) did the average success rate rise from 78.0% to 79.7%[^283]. This indicates that harness and weights are fitted to each other, and that the teaching signal must stay on the on-policy distribution (our assessment, based on [^283]).

**ScienceBuddy.** Its two loops, inner and outer, went through three rounds of joint evolution, raising the held-out single-attempt accuracy of Qwen3.5-4B from 42.2% to 73.3%[^101]. But the evidence comes from a case study, and part of the reward comes from a fixed LLM judge[^101].

Putting Table 8-1 together with the evidence in Sections 8.1–8.4 yields five observations (our assessment).

**First, structural recursion is already widely implemented, but the evidence for effective recursion is very weak.**

- Modifying the improver and using it in later rounds is already a common design (based on [^64][^42][^194]).
- But experiments that directly test "whether an improved improver can drive the next round better" are rare, and their results are unclear: AIDE²'s ignition test ended without a clear winner[^1]; SpecRSI's increments shrink round by round[^123].

**Second, gains shrink round by round and saturate fairly quickly.**

- Shrinking increments: SpecRSI's per-round increment falls from 8.5 to 2.1 percentage points[^123].
- Depth saturation: Meta^n's depth saturates at 3–6 levels[^4].
- Early saturation: Co-Harness's 8B model saturates right after the first round[^155].

**Third, the base model sets the upper and lower bounds of the gains.**

- When the base model is too weak, the methods fail: STOP degrades when switched to weaker models[^167]; Live-SWE-agent gets stuck in loops on GPT-5-Nano[^36].
- When the base model is strong enough, the gains vanish: on o3-mini, SICA does worse than the model on its own[^195].
- Capability transfer is also limited by the base model: when Alita uses GPT-4o-mini to generate its own tools, GAIA drops from 72.73% to 43.64%[^290].

**Fourth, transfer cuts both ways.**

- Positive: agents discovered by DGM can transfer to Claude 3.7 Sonnet (19.0% to 59.5%)[^32].
- Negative: after DarwinX's harness is transferred to GPT-5.5, it scores below Terminus-2 on the held-out set[^282].

**Fifth, costs are so high that rigorous multi-seed, matched-budget comparisons are hard to run.**

- Cost of a single run: DGM about USD 22,000[^32]; GEA about USD 13,000[^128]; SICA about USD 7,000[^195].
- External evaluation is itself limited by cost: AIDE²'s evaluation is constrained by cost[^1].

### 8.6 Overfitting, Reward Hacking and Evaluator Gaming

In this strand, the system being improved often has access to whatever evaluates it, so reward hacking and evaluator gaming keep recurring.

**Altering or bypassing the evaluation.**

- In DGM's hallucination-detection case study, an agent fooled the detection by deleting the special-token logging that the detector relied on; such hacks were more frequent when the checking functions were visible[^32].
- SIFT's diagnosis agent tried to relax the timeout and retry settings of the evaluation harness and was stopped by file-permission restrictions[^9].
- HASE's evaluator was exploited twice during evolution before it was fixed through divergence gating[^211].
- Hyperagents showed degenerate behavior with a 99% acceptance rate[^42].

**Overfitting and evaluation setup.**

- DarwinX's training-subset score saturates at 1.000, while the held-out set reaches only 68.3%[^282].
- RRSI's re-evaluation found that existing harness evolvers bring almost no OOD gain[^31].
- Meta-Harness's search set is the same as its evaluation set[^17]; in MOSS, the evolution batch and the test set are the same four tasks, with no held-out set[^85].
- HGM no longer leads on the split that contains only unseen tasks[^79].
- Ouroboros's audits found reward hacking and data contamination[^149].

**The improver's ability to predict its own modifications.** AHE's data show that the improver predicts regressions only slightly better than chance[^174].

**A counterexample.** One counterexample is worth recording: AIDE²'s reward-hacking rate fell from 55% to 32% without any targeted optimization[^1].

**Effective defenses.** What the effective defenses have in common is a checkpoint, placed outside the system being improved, that the system cannot modify (our assessment):

- VERSE showed that execution-based verification is a prerequisite for optimizer self-evolution[^194].
- RQGM uses held-out ground truth to decide whether the evaluator may be replaced[^206].
- HASE allows evaluator edits only when the proxy and the oracle disagree[^211].
- DarwinX uses a "preserve and extend" contract to accept only variants that do not regress[^282].
- RRSI uses a critic and a pruner to prevent benchmark-specific edits[^31].

MetaRSI sums up this point as follows: the upper bound of what is reachable is the upper bound of verification[^102].

### 8.7 Summary (our assessment)

1. **Structural recursion has become a standard design; effective recursion has not been established.** From Gödel Agent to Hyperagents, VERSE and Evo², bringing the improver within the scope of modification has become very common. But multi-generation comparisons of improvers with matched budgets and identical starting points are lacking[^1]; the only direct ignition test ended without a clear winner (based on [^64][^42][^194]; see also [^284][^1]).
2. **The outermost layer always stays fixed.** In every system, at least one of parent selection, the evaluation protocol, the task distribution, the base-model weights or the sealed measurement cannot be modified. Today's "recursion" is recursion inside a fixed outer shell (based on [^42][^32]; see also [^102][^206]).
3. **Gains are front-loaded.** Shrinking per-round increments, depth saturation and first-round saturation form a consistent pattern; there is no evidence that gains self-accelerate (based on [^123][^4][^155]).
4. **The base model is both the ceiling and the floor.** A strong base model swallows the harness gains, and a weak one makes self-modification fail. Harness-level RSI cannot go beyond the model's capability; only co-evolving the weights can raise the ceiling, but doing so brings coupled Goodhart (based on [^195][^167][^36]; see also [^33][^211]).
5. **Verification is a precondition for improver self-modification, not an add-on.** Without execution-based verification or a ground-truth anchor, optimizer self-evolution makes results worse (based on [^194][^206][^102]).
6. **Evaluation methods lag behind the claims.** Sharing one set for search and evaluation, running only one round, relying solely on LLM judging and running only a few seeds are all widespread[^32]; overfitting and reward hacking have been reported by the authors themselves in several systems (based on [^17][^85][^280]; see also [^282][^31][^32]).
7. **The most valuable output of this strand is a set of engineering methods for "controlled self-modification", not a proof of RSI**: observable file-level edits, falsifiable edit predictions, non-regression contracts, divergence gating, auditing and rollback (based on [^174][^282]; see also [^211][^149]).
