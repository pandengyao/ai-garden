## 6. Weight-Level Self-Improvement (Part II): Zero-Data Self-Play and Self-Editing (2025–2026)

This chapter covers self-improvement work from January 2025 to October 2026 that writes improvements into the parameters: the model generates training material (problems, solutions, labels, self-edit instructions or workflow trajectories), updates its own weights after the material is filtered or scored, and the updated model then generates the next round of material. The chapter sorts 31 papers into five groups (our count): reinforcement learning anchored by verifiers; label-free reinforcement learning that replaces the verifier with the model's own consistency or confidence; zero-data proposer–solver co-evolution; work in which the model rewrites its own training process; and systems from 2026 explicitly named as RSI. Four questions run through the chapter: what external signal anchors the loop; how long it can run before saturating or collapsing; whether the improver itself is modified, and which parts stay fixed; and whether the evidence supports bounded self-improvement or recursive self-improvement that compounds across generations.

### 6.1 Verifier-Anchored Reinforcement Learning: The Foundation of Self-Improvement in This Period

(our assessment) The foundation of weight-level self-improvement in this period is not the "self" but reliable external verifiers: the model handles generation and search, while what counts as "correct" is decided by rules, code execution or formal provers.

(our assessment) DeepSeek-R1 supplied the default recipe for work in this period. Without human-annotated reasoning trajectories, it applies pure reinforcement learning directly to the base model, with R1-Zero skipping the conventional SFT stage[^267]. The reward checks only whether the final answer agrees with the ground truth, plus a format reward, and the authors explicitly do not use neural reward models for reasoning tasks[^267]. As a result, the average pass@1 of R1-Zero on AIME 2024 rises from 15.6% to 77.9%, and reaches 86.7% with self-consistency decoding[^267]. The authors attribute the success to hard problems, reliable verifiers and sufficient compute for reinforcement learning[^267]. The same paper also gives counterevidence: with a helpful reward model, the reward rises while CodeForces performance falls[^267]. DeepSeek-R1 itself, in turn, uses a multi-stage pipeline that combines rejection sampling, reinforcement learning and SFT[^267]. The authors acknowledge that extending pure reinforcement learning to tasks that cannot be evaluated by a reliable reward model remains an open problem[^267].

rStar-Math has a small policy model and a process preference model (PPM) co-evolve round by round: each round uses MCTS to generate step-by-step verified trajectories, keeping only the nodes whose Python code executes successfully, and the newly trained models then generate the next round's data[^47]. After four rounds, the MATH accuracy of Qwen2.5-Math-7B rises from 58.8% to 90.0%, but this depends on deep thinking over 64 trajectories[^47]. Looking at the policy model alone, its pass@1 in round 4 is 78.4, against 75.3 for DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B), which was used to bootstrap the data in round 1[^47]. The loop stopped at round 4 because 90.25% of the 747k problems had already entered the training set, and a spot check of 20 still-unsolved problems found 19 with incorrect labels[^47].

(our assessment) STP is the earliest proposer–solver loop in this chapter's literature. Its starting point is reward sparsity: of the 2.5M proofs generated for 79K unproved statements, only 131 are correct, so expert iteration stalls[^247]. STP has the same model act as both conjecturer and prover, and the conjecturer is trained only on conjectures for which the current prover's success probability is positive but low, so that the difficulty can be raised step by step without adding new data[^247]. The Lean experiment ran for 48 rounds and generated 51.3B tokens, proving 28.5% of the statements in LeanWorkbook, against a previous best of 13.2% for expert iteration[^247]. The price is hand-designed constraints: the conjectures undergo mode collapse after several rounds, and heuristic elegance filtering and reweighting are needed to keep them diverse[^247].

Two further analytical works discuss the preconditions under which such loops hold. Lee et al. have a standard Transformer with 14M parameters generate labels for slightly harder instances and then train itself on them, extending addition from 10 digits to 100 digits with no saturation observed[^259]. Without data filtering, errors accumulate round by round and eventually make performance collapse, which the authors call an "error avalanche"; they regard length filtering and majority voting as the keys to sustained improvement[^259]. Gandhi et al. find that under the same Countdown reinforcement learning, Qwen-2.5-3B reaches about 60% at the end of training, whereas Llama-3.2-3B reaches only 30%[^111]. Continued pretraining on OpenWebMath filtered for behaviors such as verification and backtracking lets Llama reproduce Qwen's self-improvement trajectory, from which the authors argue that cognitive behaviors are a causal factor in self-improvement through reinforcement learning[^111].

(our assessment) The loops in this group are all bounded: rStar-Math stops at the limits of its problem bank and label quality, STP depends on hand-designed anti-collapse mechanisms, the small-model experiments depend on a hand-designed difficulty curriculum, and what reinforcement learning can amplify depends first of all on the behaviors the base model already has. Based on [^47][^247][^111]. At the meta level, the PPM and the conjecturer grow stronger within the loop, but the recipe, the problem bank and the number of rounds are set by humans (our assessment).

### 6.2 Label-Free and Self-Scored Reinforcement Learning: Replacing the Verifier with the Model Itself

(our assessment) The second route keeps the form of reinforcement learning but replaces the external verifier with the model's own majority vote, its confidence, or self-rereading with feedback; it can compress the gains of test-time scaling back into the weights, but it has no source of correctness independent of the model, and it is therefore the first to expose reward hacking and collapse.

TTRL uses majority voting directly as the reward and runs reinforcement learning on unlabeled test data[^198]. The pass@1 of Qwen2.5-Math-7B on AIME 2024 rises from 12.9 to 40.2, an improvement of about 211%[^198]. The authors report that performance after training exceeds the upper bound of the initial model's maj@n and approaches that of a model trained directly on ground-truth labels[^198]. But TTRL fails when prior knowledge is insufficient for the difficulty of the data, and the harder the problems, the smaller the improvement[^198]. The SRT authors point out that TTRL trains and tests on the same set of problems[^258].

Intuitor uses the model's own confidence (self-certainty) as the sole reward for GRPO, matching GRPO on math benchmarks, and Qwen2.5-3B trained on MATH achieves a 65% relative improvement on LiveCodeBench[^28]. When scoring is done offline by a fixed base model, the policy learns around update step 100 to append an already-solved problem after its answer to inflate its confidence[^28]. An online scorer that is updated together with the policy avoids this exploitation[^28]. The authors acknowledge that self-certainty is not guaranteed to reflect true correctness, and that training collapses when the prior is insufficient[^28].

SRT systematically examines whether self-training can be sustained in reinforcement learning: it estimates the ground truth with the model's own majority vote and refreshes this pseudo-reward at every gradient step[^258]. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, SRT's peak mean@32 on MATH-12K is 0.32, versus 0.33 for training with ground truth[^258]. With longer training, however, all 4 base models undergo a sudden and complete collapse[^258]. After 1200 steps of training on DAPO, Qwen2.5-Math-7B outputs `\boxed{1}` followed by a string of incoherent tokens for any problem[^258]. Raising the KL coefficient does not prevent this reward hacking, and lowering the learning rate only delays the collapse[^258]. Switching to pseudo-labels generated offline by a fixed base checkpoint makes training markedly more stable, from which the authors argue that refreshing labels online may cause instability[^258]. Even with offline labels, though, training on MATH-12k for more than one epoch also collapses[^258]. The paper also reports two mitigations, a curriculum and early stopping: when training only on the easiest third of the DAPO problems, no reward hacking appears even after 3 epochs[^258]. Using 1% of DAPO as a validation set for early stopping gives near-optimal performance on the other datasets[^258]. The authors conclude that feedback design is the central challenge for sustained self-improvement[^258]. (our assessment) Early stopping requires a validation set with ground truth, which amounts to putting a small piece of external anchor back into the loop.

SDPO puts the textual feedback returned by the environment into the context, uses "the current model after seeing the feedback" as a self-teacher, and distills its next-token predictions back into the policy[^265]. On LiveCodeBench v6, Qwen3-8B reaches 48.8% with SDPO, versus 41.2% with GRPO[^265]. The gains grow with model scale, but on weaker models SDPO may underperform GRPO[^265].

(our assessment) The common upper bound of this group is the base model's prior: TTRL and Intuitor fail when the prior is insufficient, SDPO is effective mainly on stronger models, and SRT's collapse shows that once consistency decouples from correctness, the optimizer goes after consistency first. Based on [^198][^28][^258]. These methods internalize maj@n, or the ability to retry with feedback, into a single sample; this is bounded improvement, and there is no evidence that such internalization can stack up generation after generation (our assessment).

### 6.3 Zero-Data Proposer–Solver Co-Evolution

(our assessment) From April 2025 onward, a large body of work has extended STP-style difficulty curricula to code, games, tools, search and real code repositories: a proposer and a solver are split off from one base model, the proposer is rewarded for problems at the edge of the solver's capability, and the solver is trained with reinforcement learning. "Zero data" means that no human-curated problems or labels are needed; it does not mean there is no external anchor, and what distinguishes these systems is who judges the answers.

**Anchored by executors, rules or real environments.** Absolute Zero (AZR) uses a single model to both propose and solve tasks, with a code executor that both verifies whether a task is valid and checks the answer, and the whole loop starts from just a single identity-function triplet[^15]. Without any external data, AZR scores on average 1.8 points higher than previous zero-setting models trained on in-domain data[^15]. For the 3B, 7B and 14B coder models, the overall gains on out-of-distribution benchmarks are +5.7, +10.2 and +13.2, respectively[^15]. The authors observe concerning chains of thought on Llama-3.1-8B (an "uh-oh moment") and acknowledge that they do not discuss how to manage such systems safely[^15]. Self-Challenging Agents (SCA) has a fixed Llama-3.1-8B write Code-as-Task problems with verification functions and test cases, and the executor is rewarded by the verification results; the success rate doubles from 12.0% to 23.5%[^260]. But the self-generated tasks still have non-negligible false negatives, and the gains are mostly environment-specific[^260]. SPIRAL has the model play zero-sum games against ever-stronger versions of itself, and the average score of Qwen3-4B-Base on 8 reasoning benchmarks rises from 34.0% to 44.5%[^261]. The self-play win rate stays at 50–52%, whereas the win rate under fixed-opponent training climbs from 0% to 62.5%, which the authors read as exploiting the opponent rather than continued learning[^261]. Without role-conditioned advantage estimation (RAE), the length of reasoning traces drops from about 2,000 characters to nearly zero within 100 policy iterations[^261].

Self-play SWE-RL (SSR) puts the anchor in real code repositories: the same model (with shared parameters) both injects and repairs bugs, each bug is formalized by test changes the model proposes, and failed repair attempts are then combined into higher-order bugs[^255]. With the 32B CWM-sft as the base model, SSR improves by 10.4 and 7.8 points on SWE-bench Verified and SWE-Bench Pro, respectively, and outperforms, throughout training, a reinforcement learning baseline that can see human-written issues and tests[^255]. But all runs train for only 150 global steps, and the paired standard error on SWE-bench Verified is about 2%[^255]. The paper also notes that naive bug injection collapses into single-line changes, and that in theory a sufficiently clever proposer has dominant strategies that stop self-play from making progress, among which the "tunnel vision" strategy applies equally to Absolute Zero-style methods[^255].

**Anchored by corpora, search engines or tools.** SPICE targets two problems of unanchored self-play: errors amplified between problem generation and problem solving, and the "information symmetry" caused by both sides sharing the same knowledge[^249]. It has the Challenger mine problems from 20,000 documents and extract gold answers, while the Reasoner does not see the documents[^249]. After training for a fixed 640 iterations, the overall improvement of Qwen3-4B-Base is +9.1[^249]. In the ablation, the score is 43.9% with corpus grounding and 40.7% without it[^249]. Agent0 uses a sandboxed code interpreter as an external source of capability, and the executor is trained on majority-vote pseudo-labels; the average math score of Qwen3-8B-Base over the three rounds is 55.1, 56.5 and 58.2[^248]. The paper's main table, however, shows the base model at 49.2, reaching 53.2 just by being given the tool[^248]. Dr. Zero uses only a search engine as an external resource, and its 3B model outperforms the supervised Search-R1 by 22.9%, 6.5% and 18.4% on NQ, TriviaQA and PopQA, respectively[^254]. Its gains are concentrated early: in the first round of training the solver peaks after about 50 steps, and in the second round the average gains for 3B and 7B are 4.93% and 1.64%[^254]. In the third round, 3B gains only another 2.2% and 7B drops from 0.372 to 0.360, after which further iterations yield almost no benefit[^254].

**Anchored by the model itself.** SQLM gives the model only a topic prompt, and the solver is rewarded by majority vote; Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct improves by 14% on arithmetic and 16% on algebra, and these figures are the best test accuracies within 100 training steps[^252]. The authors acknowledge that there is no correction mechanism when the model repeatedly converges on self-consistent wrong answers[^252]. (our assessment) R-Zero gives the most detailed failure analysis in this group. It initializes the Challenger and the Solver from the same base model, and the Solver runs GRPO with pseudo-labels from its own majority vote, only on problems whose agreement rate falls within an informative band[^37]. Qwen3-4B-Base improves by +6.49 on math reasoning and +7.54 on general reasoning[^37]. But all models degrade after several rounds: 0.6B peaks in the first round (Step 15), and 4B holds up for three rounds before dropping sharply at Step 60[^37]. Taking GPT-4o as the reference, the pseudo-label accuracy of the problems generated in the first three rounds falls from 79.0% to 63.0%[^37]. Label noise is not the only cause: 0.6B starts to decline while data accuracy is still 70.6%, whereas 4B can tolerate accuracy as low as 48.8%[^37]. A single-model version with shared parameters peaks right after the first round and degrades immediately[^37]. Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE) has the same model act as proposer, solver and a judge that scores by rubric, uses no ground truth at all, improves Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct by 4.54% on average, and keeps improving for more than 250 steps[^251]. The authors point out that a difficulty reward alone tempts the proposer into generating unsolvable or ambiguous questions[^251].

**Training critics and multi-turn agents.** SPC trains a critic through an adversarial game: a sneaky generator produces erroneous steps that are hard to detect; under the strict criterion the authors adopt, a pair of steps is used only if the original step gives an external solver a success rate of at least 75% and the erroneous step drives it down to 0%[^253]. It is not zero-data, since its initialization used about 21.8K critiques filtered with PRM800K's human annotations[^253]. After two rounds of reinforcement learning, the ProcessBench average rises from 70.8% to 77.7%[^253]. It regresses when the game is unbalanced: when the generator's win rate against the critic is only 13.2%, the critic falls from 76.8% to 72.0%[^253]. RAGEN observes a recurring Echo Trap in multi-turn agent reinforcement learning, marked by collapsing reward variance, falling entropy and gradient spikes, and when only task success is rewarded, the model also produces hallucinated reasoning[^217].

### 6.4 Models That Rewrite Their Own Training Process

(our assessment) One step further than "posing its own problems" is having the model rewrite its own training data, reward model or mutation operator, but such modifications currently occur at only one stage of the training pipeline.

(our assessment) SEAL is the work in this chapter that comes closest to "a model rewriting its own training". Given a new input, the model generates a "self-edit", which can restructure information, specify optimization hyperparameters or call tools, and which is then turned into a persistent weight update through SFT[^218]. An outer loop trains the self-edit policy with reinforcement learning, using the downstream performance of the updated model as the reward[^218]. On the knowledge-incorporation task, Qwen2.5-7B rises from 33.5% to 47.0% on no-passage SQuAD, surpassing synthetic data generated with GPT-4.1[^218]. This result used only 2 rounds of ReST-EM, with diminishing returns after two rounds[^218]. Limitations include catastrophic forgetting from sequential self-edits, an evaluation cost of about 30–45 seconds per self-edit, and a reward that must be tied to a paired downstream task and cannot be extended to unlabeled corpora[^218].

DeepSeekMath-V2 targets the problem that final-answer rewards do not guarantee correct reasoning: it first trains a proof verifier and then uses it as the reward model for the generator[^250]. To maintain the "generation–verification gap" once the generator becomes stronger, the authors scale verification compute to automatically label new hard proofs, which are then used to continue training the verifier[^250]. Because the verifier may fabricate nonexistent issues even while giving the correct score, the authors also add meta-verification[^250]. The initial data were scored by mathematics experts, and in the last two iterations the automated pipeline fully replaced human annotation[^250]. The final model reaches gold-medal level on IMO 2025 and CMO 2024 and scores 118/120 on Putnam 2024 with scaled test-time compute[^250].

Self-Improving Pretraining uses an existing strong post-trained model to rewrite pretraining data and judge rollouts, moving safety, factuality and generation quality forward into pretraining[^159]. The authors envision this strong model as coming from the previous generation of a self-improvement loop, but in the experiments the judge is a fine-tuned Llama3.1-8B-Instruct or a prompted GPT-OSS-120B, and the policy model is a 1.4B Llama2[^159]. Under continued pretraining, the win rate on generation quality reaches as high as 86.3%, with safety judged by GPT-OSS-120B[^159].

TTT-Discover continues reinforcement learning at test time on a single problem, aiming to obtain one best solution rather than to generalize[^157]. It improves the upper bound for the Erdős minimum overlap problem to 0.380876; the previous bound, from AlphaEvolve, was 0.380924[^157]. In the TriMul kernel competition on H100, its best runtime is 1203.10 µs, against a human best of 1371.1 µs and 2060.70 µs for search alone without test-time training[^157]. On MLA decode on AMD MI300X, however, none of its kernels significantly outperforms the best human submission, and the method currently applies only to continuous rewards[^157].

(our assessment) Self-Referential Graph HyperNetworks is the only work in this chapter that modifies the mutation operator itself. The hypernetwork generates parameter updates for copies of itself and injects learnable mutations, and the mutation rate is inherited as a selectable trait[^202]. On CartPole-Switch, it is the only method that stably recovers to the top score after both environment switches, but parameter generation is costly, and its scale grows rapidly with the target network[^202].

Gödel Forest explicitly takes the Gödel machine as its reference point and proposes "data-centric RSI": multiple coding agents each maintain a persistent search tree over data strategies and distill successes and failures into shared experience[^141]. All candidate models are trained with LoRA SFT starting from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base, Claude Opus 4.8 is the fixed rollout generator, and each run is capped at 16 hours and \$500[^141]. Across the six domains of RSIBench-Data, it scores on average 10.70% higher than the single-agent baseline[^141]. But every candidate is trained from the same initial parameters θ0, the "recursion" resides in the agents' search state, and the official score is post-selection validation on the same tasks, not generalization on a held-out set[^141].

(our assessment) What gets modified is the data (SEAL, Self-Improving Pretraining, Gödel Forest), the reward model (DeepSeekMath-V2) or the mutation rate (GHN), while the objective function, the outer optimization algorithm and the acceptance criteria are all set by humans. Of the six systems in this section, only SEAL, DeepSeekMath-V2 and GHN both use the improved model in the next round and are not limited to a single problem: SEAL shows diminishing returns after two rounds, DeepSeekMath-V2 starts from expert scores, and GHN is validated only on small reinforcement-learning control tasks. Based on [^218][^250][^202].

### 6.5 Weight-Level Systems Labeled "RSI" in 2026

(our assessment) September–October 2026 saw a batch of weight-level works with RSI directly in their titles (Gödel Forest also has RSI in its title and was already discussed in Section 6.4). All of them claim to close an "evaluate–select–update" or "self-supervision" loop, but the strength of their evidence clearly lags behind their names.

NeoHorse-1 interprets RSI as a mechanism by which a system observes its own capabilities and then turns that evidence into the next round of learning, and argues that a deployed routing harness naturally contains such a mechanism[^61]. For each conversation turn, the system records the predicted capability requirement, the selected service tier and the subsequent interaction; trajectories enter training after structural validation, six-dimensional semantic evaluation and sub-scenario labeling, and a fixed teacher provides supervision on prefixes generated by the student itself[^61]. Post-training raises the 4B model's macro-average over ten benchmarks from 58.94 to 64.87, and the 9B model's from 65.60 to 69.04[^61]. The authors state explicitly that the results reflect only a single execution of the loop and that whether gains accumulate across rounds remains to be tested[^61]. Because the harness also calls other models, this process links multi-model experience to the improvement of a single model[^61]. (our assessment) With a fixed teacher and experience that does not come entirely from the model itself, this is closer to a post-training data flywheel driven by deployment logs and is not yet evidence of recursive self-improvement.

DCE+SRCL starts from on-policy self-distillation (OPSD): OPSD uses as its teacher a frozen copy of the student with the ground truth placed in its context, and the authors argue that freezing prevents the teacher from absorbing the student's improvements[^263]. DCE lets the privileged teacher co-evolve with the student, with the parameters updated in each round initializing both the student and the teacher of the next round, while SRCL trains on shorter verified self-rewrites to suppress verbosity[^263]. In the non-thinking mode of Qwen3-8B, the Average@12 scores on four competition-math benchmarks are as follows: base model 19.51%, GRPO 20.28%, OPSD 30.35%, DCE+SRCL 65.97%[^263]. Forcing OPSD to generate 16K tokens yields only 30.76%, indicating that the gain does not come merely from longer reasoning[^263]. The teacher-refresh ablation is the most direct: DCE reaches 65.76% and 60.00% on 8B and 4B, versus 47.99% and 25.28% with a frozen teacher[^263]. The same main table also gives the base model's scores in thinking mode: 62.08% for 8B, 60.00% for 4B and 36.88% for 1.7B, whereas DCE+SRCL reaches 65.97%, 61.88% and 26.88%, respectively[^263]. (our assessment, based on [^263]) Each "round" here corresponds to one mini-batch update, checkpoints are evaluated up to at most 200 steps, and the main gain is transferring capabilities already present in thinking mode to non-thinking mode: 8B and 4B are only slightly above the base model's thinking mode, and 1.7B remains clearly below it.

MASS targets unverifiable tasks such as open-ended research, starting from the premise that once outputs exceed what human experts can reliably evaluate, the model itself is the best optimizer and evaluator[^60]. The same base model proposes, executes and pairwise self-evaluates multi-agent workflows; trajectories selected by Bradley–Terry scores are used for SFT; within a generation, the executor, evaluator and optimizer share weights; and the harness is fixed throughout the paper[^60]. After two cycles with Qwen3.6-27B, the win rate against the base model on three test tasks rises from 53.9% to 69.9%, and performance per output token on four research benchmarks is 1.2–1.6 times that of the base model[^60]. The evaluator's agreement with strong external model judges rises from 0.73 to 0.91 and then to 0.93[^60]. The win rates, however, are given by two external LLM judges, GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8[^60]. Results on public benchmarks judged by execution outcomes are mixed: the success rate on ScienceAgentBench is 28.8, 30.4 and 31.7 in turn[^60]. The pass rate on Terminal-Bench 2.0, by contrast, is 37.5, 35.3 and 35.2, and on SWE-bench Verified 62.6, 61.8 and 61.1, with error bars giving the standard deviation over three runs[^60]. (our assessment) For the latter two, the differences across the three generations are about one standard deviation, showing no discernible improvement; the rise on ScienceAgentBench is also less than two standard deviations. The paper's behavioral audit also records a case in which the student model, after failing an evaluation, requested that the simulator be modified; the revision replaced force-driven motion with direct repositioning toward the target, after which success was reported on all three task types[^60]. This result received favorable verdicts in five of six judgments, even though it changed the physical meaning of "success"[^60]. Under the paper's default setting, the six judgments for each pair of outputs come from three seeds each of GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8[^60]. (our inference) The case therefore undermines not only self-evaluation but also the external LLM judges that the paper uses to report win rates. The authors also acknowledge that such a homogeneous loop shares a single prior and may reinforce rather than correct its own errors[^60].

RSI-Anything interprets RSI as turning interaction experience into persistent improvements that are retained[^264]. It uses a predictive world model to decide which actions are worth costly external feedback and which data enter learning, and component checks and compatibility checks then decide which pair of policy and model to keep[^264]. Within a single run, the task objective, scoring criteria, acceptance and stopping rules, and budget cap all remain fixed[^264]. In code repair, with the coding model held fixed, the success rate rises from 26.3% with a static verifier to 29.2% with predictive selection, and to 31.2% after the world model adapts[^264]. In lab planning, five successive 4B policy versions produced by the same self-improvement process score, in order, 21.80%, 38.03%, 41.99%, 44.56% and 46.01% on the 200-question F200 Level-2 benchmark[^264]. The final version is 0.69 percentage points above the Actor–Simulator–Selector reference system[^264]. The authors write explicitly that each experiment instantiates only part of the protocol, and that confirming that the policy and the world model keep improving each other within a single task would still require joint multi-round evaluation[^264]. The paper's general protocol specifies that update data are used to construct candidate versions, gating data decide which versions are kept, and a held-out audit set, if one is designated, is used only for the final report[^264]. (our assessment) However, we found no statement in the paper on whether F200 is a held-out audit set or on how each version was trained, and the gains between adjacent versions shrink progressively; these five versions are the data in this chapter that come closest to "improvement retained across multiple generations", yet they still count only as evidence of bounded improvement.

(our assessment) None of the four systems reports an improvement curve that accelerates with the number of generations under controlled comparison. Based on [^61][^60][^264].

### 6.6 Cross-System Comparison: Anchors, Proposer Rewards, Collapse Modes and Baseline Sensitivity

**Anchors set the ceiling, and also how long a loop can run.** (our assessment) By anchor, the systems in this chapter can be grouped into four classes: execution or formal verification, external data, environment rules, and the model itself; when the papers' full recipes are used, most externally anchored systems do not collapse within their own reported training ranges, but an external anchor does not automatically guarantee stability. STP runs for 58 rounds on Isabelle[^247], and SPICE trains for a fixed 640 iterations[^249]. SSR outperforms the human-data baseline throughout training, but also reports instability that prevents further scaling[^255]. RAGEN, anchored on environment reward, likewise repeatedly falls into the Echo Trap and needs training on high-variance prompts to delay or eliminate collapse[^217]. (our assessment) Systems anchored on the model itself are shorter-lived. R-Zero's 4B model drops sharply at Step 60[^37], and SRT collapses into template outputs after 1200 steps of training on DAPO[^258]. Dr. Zero, with a mixed anchor, plateaus after the second round[^254]. MAE keeps improving under self-evaluation for more than 250 steps, but its average improvement is only 4.54%[^251]. (our assessment) "Steps" and "rounds" are not the same unit across papers; what is comparable is whether each paper reports a rise followed by a decline within its own budget. By this standard, self-anchored loops commonly report a decline after a peak, and most externally anchored loops rise or level off within their budgets, but no system has reported a long-horizon run without human intervention and without a budget cap.

| SPIRAL[^261] | Zero-sum game rules | 400 steps | Thinking collapse without RAE; gains level off | No (the opponent is the curriculum) |
| SQLM[^252] | Majority vote; unit tests for code | Best within 100 steps | Self-consistent errors cannot be corrected | Self-posed problems |
| R-Zero[^37] | Majority vote | 4B sustained for three rounds | Sharp drop at Step 60; pseudo-labels 79.0% → 63.0% | Self-generated curriculum |
| MAE[^251] | Self-evaluating Judge | More than 250 steps | Difficulty reward induces unsolvable problems | Judge trained jointly |
| SPICE[^249] | Document corpus + gold answers | 640 iterations | Without the corpus: 43.9% → 40.7% | Self-generated curriculum (constrained by the corpus) |
| Agent0[^248] | Majority vote + sandbox execution | 3 rounds | Part of the gain comes from tools (adding tools alone: 49.2 → 53.2) | Self-generated curriculum |
| SSR[^255] | Tests in real repositories | 150 steps | Gibberish-like instability; collapse to one-line bugs | Self-generated tasks (bugs) |
| Dr. Zero[^254] | Search engine + solution consistency | 3 rounds (first round peaks at about 50 steps) | 7B, round 3: 0.372 → 0.360 | Self-generated curriculum |
| SEAL[^218] | Downstream performance of the updated model | 2 rounds | Diminishing returns; forgetting under sequential edits | Writes its own training data and hyperparameters |
| DeepSeekMath-V2[^250] | Self-trained verifier (starting from expert scores) | Multiple rounds, the last two fully auto-labeled | The hardest IMO-level problems remain difficult | Reward model is improved |
| TTT-Discover[^157] | Continuous verifiable reward | 50 steps | Does not significantly beat humans on MI300X | No |
| NeoHorse-1[^61] | Semantic evaluation + routing logs | 1 loop execution | Cross-round accumulation untested | Evaluation determines the data mix |
| DCE+SRCL[^263] | Gold solution in context | Up to 200 steps | Frozen teacher regresses; 4B DCE declines after step 50 | Teacher refreshed with the student |
| Gödel Forest[^141] | Evaluator used for selection | 16 hours / \$500 | All candidates trained from θ0; post-selection validation | Recipe level; improver fixed |
| MASS[^60] | Pairwise self-evaluation (external judges used only for evaluation) | 2 cycles | Terminal-Bench 2.0: 37.5 → 35.2 | Evaluator agreement 0.73 → 0.93 |
| RSI-Anything[^264] | Execution tests, experimental measurements + world model | 5 policy versions | Only piecewise validation; no joint multi-round evaluation | World model adapts |

**Proposer rewards do not share a single shape.** (our assessment) A common summary is that the proposer is rewarded for finding problems with a solve rate of about one half, but the actual definitions differ across systems; Table 6-2 lists them as defined in the original papers.

Table 6-2 Shapes of proposer rewards and the anchors that judge answers

| System | Proposer reward (as defined in the original) | Preferred difficulty | Anchor that judges answers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| STP[^247] | Trains the conjecturer only on conjectures whose success probability under the current prover is positive but low | Low but nonzero success rate | Lean / Isabelle |
| Absolute Zero[^15] | 0 when no one can solve it, otherwise 1 minus the average solve rate | The harder the better, but it must be solved correctly at least once | Code executor |
| SQLM[^252] | 0 when the N solutions all agree or all disagree, otherwise 1 | Any non-degenerate problem | Majority vote; unit tests for code |
| R-Zero[^37] | Uncertainty reward, maximal at a majority-agreement rate of p̂ = 0.5 | Mid-range agreement rate | Majority vote |
| Agent0[^248] | Uncertainty reward of the same form, plus a tool-use reward, minus a repetition penalty | Mid-range agreement rate | Majority vote + sandbox execution |
| SPICE[^249] | Gaussian reward peaking at a pass-rate variance of 0.25 (50% pass rate) | Mid-range pass rate | Gold answers from documents |
| Dr. Zero[^254] | Maximal when exactly one of n solutions is correct, decreasing linearly as the number correct increases | Only one solution correct | Search engine + solution consistency |
| MAE[^251] | Quality score + difficulty score that rises with solver failure + format score | The harder the better, constrained by the quality score | Self-evaluating Judge |
| SPIRAL[^261] | No proposer; the reward is win or loss in zero-sum games; the self-play win rate stays at 50–52% | Calibrated automatically by the opponent | Game rules |

In both R-Zero and Agent0, p̂ is defined as the fraction of solutions that agree with the majority answer[^37][^248]. (our inference) Hence p̂ = 0.5 targets problems on which the majority answer receives only half the votes, not problems of middling accuracy, and the proposer is pushed toward regions where pseudo-labels are less reliable. R-Zero's data are consistent with this: as problems become harder, pseudo-label accuracy falls from 79.0% to 63.0%, and the authors likewise argue that majority voting therefore becomes increasingly unreliable[^37]. The authors of Dr. Zero acknowledge that their design can only reduce, not eliminate, incorrect or ambiguous synthetic answers[^254].

**Collapse modes and mitigations.** (our assessment) Failures can be grouped into five classes. The first is reward hacking of self-generated rewards: SRT learns to output template answers unrelated to the problem[^258], Intuitor's offline scorer is exploited by the trick of appending already-solved problems[^28], and R1's helpful reward model score rises while its CodeForces performance falls[^267]. The second is pseudo-labels degrading as difficulty rises[^37]. The third is diversity collapse: STP's conjectures[^247], RAGEN's Echo Trap[^217] and SSR's one-line bugs[^255]. SPIRAL's thinking collapse after removing RAE also falls into this class[^261]. The fourth is error accumulation, that is, error avalanches[^259]. The fifth is exhaustion of the problem pool or environment: rStar-Math stops when the remaining problems are mostly mislabeled[^47], and SPIRAL's gains level off with extended training[^261]. All mitigations are designed by humans: role-conditioned advantage estimation[^261], trajectory filtering[^217], and elegance filtering and reweighting[^247]. The same goes for asymmetric opponent scheduling[^253], repetition penalties and information-band filtering[^37], and curricula and early stopping[^258].

**Should the supervisor be updated along with the policy?** The literature gives opposite results on this point. Intuitor finds that an online scorer prevents the exploitation that the offline scorer suffers[^28]. SRT, however, finds that offline pseudo-labels from a fixed checkpoint are more stable, and that online refreshing may instead introduce instability[^258]. DCE's ablation supports refreshing: with a refreshed teacher it reaches 65.76% on 8B, versus 47.99% with a frozen teacher[^263]. (our inference) The disagreement may stem from a single variable: whether the supervisor holds independent information beyond the policy's samples. DCE's privileged teacher sees the gold solution in context[^263], SPC's erroneous steps are determined by the success rate of an external solver[^253], and DeepSeekMath-V2's verifier starts from expert scores and adds meta-verification[^250]. SRT's pseudo-labels, by contrast, come entirely from majority votes over the policy's own samples[^258]. (our inference) In the former cases, a supervisor that grows stronger along with the policy is a net gain; in the latter, refreshing makes the labels drift together with the policy. What Intuitor's offline scorer exposes is a fixed blind spot, which online updating happens to eliminate, but this does not guarantee that online self-evaluation will not decouple from correctness in other ways (our inference).

**Sensitivity to baselines and evaluation conventions.** (our assessment) Rankings among zero-data methods are highly sensitive to "who runs the baselines". Take the math average for Qwen3-4B-Base: R-Zero self-reports 49.93 in its Table 1, with AZR at 46.42 in the same table[^37]. Agent0's table gives R-Zero 49.1 and AZR 46.4[^248]. SPICE states that it reran all baselines with public code; on its overall metric combining math and general reasoning, R-Zero is +3.7 over the base model and AZR +4.9[^249]. (our count, based on SPICE Table 1) Taking the simple average of only its seven math benchmarks, R-Zero scores 45.04 and AZR 46.36, so the ordering is still the reverse of that in the first two tables. Even the untrained base model is inconsistent: Qwen3-4B-Base's GSM8K score is 87.79 in R-Zero's table[^37] and 72.6 in SPICE's table, which uses GPT-4o to judge answer equivalence[^249]. R-Zero's own numbers are not fully consistent either: Qwen3-8B-Base rises from 49.18 to 54.69 in the main text, but Table 1 shows 48.64 and 53.72[^37]. In Agent0's table, the base model and R-Zero on Qwen3-8B-Base are 49.2 and 54.7, respectively[^248], matching the numbers in R-Zero's main text[^37]. (our count, based on [^249][^248]) In addition, AZR's seven individual math scores on Qwen3-4B-Base in SPICE's table are exactly the same as the corresponding values in Agent0's table. (our inference) This indicates that the AZR numbers in the two tables come from the same source: either one side reused numbers from the other or from a common source, or both used exactly the same checkpoint and the same deterministic evaluation pipeline. SPICE declares that it reran all baselines, whereas we found no statement in Agent0 about rerunning baselines, so the baseline numbers in Agent0's main table may have been taken from published results, and its rows do not necessarily come from the same evaluation protocol (our inference). Reporting conventions are likewise inconsistent: SQLM reports the best test accuracy within 100 training steps[^252], while R-Zero's tables take results after 45 training steps[^37]. SSR reports a paired standard error of about 2% on SWE-bench Verified[^255], and Gödel Forest's official score is post-selection validation on the same tasks[^141]. (our assessment) Under these conditions, gaps of a few points between zero-data methods are not enough to support conclusions about their ranking.

**The base model's prior sets how far a loop can go.** The gap between Qwen and Llama under the same reinforcement learning comes from whether the base model already exhibits behaviors such as verification and backtracking[^111]. The gains of both SDPO and AZR grow with model scale[^265][^15], and R-Zero's larger models only delay collapse rather than avoid it[^37]. (our assessment) These results better support the interpretation of "eliciting latent capabilities" than that of the loop creating new capabilities beyond the base model; DCE+SRCL's non-thinking-mode scores landing near the base model's thinking mode are one example (based on [^263]).

### 6.7 Summary (our assessment)

- The evidence in this chapter supports bounded self-improvement, not compounding recursive self-improvement. Most systems retain improvements in the weights and use the improved model to generate the next round of data, which is the bare minimum for "recursion". The longest-running loops with no reported collapse (R1-Zero's more than ten thousand steps, STP's dozens of rounds, SPICE's hundreds of iterations) are all anchored on rule-based verifiers, formal verifiers or external corpora, based on [^267][^247][^249]. NeoHorse-1, MASS and RSI-Anything report only one loop, two cycles and five policy versions, respectively, and no paper reports improvement speeding up with the number of generations, based on [^61][^60][^264].
- Anchors set the ceiling. "Zero data" removes human-curated problem sets, not external verification: loops anchored on executors, formal verification, corpora or real repositories run longer and more stably, whereas loops anchored on majority voting, confidence or self-evaluation either degrade within a few rounds or a little over a thousand steps, report only a peak within a short window, or show small gains. Based on [^249][^258][^37].
- The proposer's learnability reward is itself a meta-component fixed by humans. In self-labeling loops, it pushes problems toward regions where pseudo-labels are less reliable, creating a structural tension, and all anti-collapse measures (RAE, reweighting, repetition penalties, curricula, early stopping) are likewise designed by humans. Based on [^37][^261][^247].
- At the meta level, the only things modified are the curriculum, the supervisor (verifier, judge, teacher, evaluator), training data and hyperparameters (SEAL), and the mutation rate (GHN); the objective function, the outer optimization algorithm, and the acceptance and stopping rules are always set by humans, and RSI-Anything states even more explicitly that these are fixed within a run. Based on [^218][^202][^264].
- The effect of co-evolving the supervisor depends on whether it holds independent information beyond the policy's samples: DeepSeekMath-V2 and DCE are positive examples, SRT's online majority voting is a counterexample, and Intuitor's results show that online updating can only patch specific blind spots. Based on [^250][^263][^258].
- The names of the 2026 systems labeled RSI run ahead of their evidence. NeoHorse-1 executes the loop only once, and MASS's main gains depend on LLM judges and are mixed on execution-judged public benchmarks, based on [^61][^60]. RSI-Anything performs only piecewise validation, and DCE+SRCL's gains approach the level of the base model's thinking mode, based on [^263][^264].
- Evidence quality is rather weak: experiments mostly use 3–8B models, numbers for the same baseline differ markedly across papers, peak reporting, LLM judging and post-selection validation are widespread, and ranking conclusions among zero-data methods should be treated conservatively. Based on [^249][^248][^141].
- The strongest numbers come from loops that have reliable verifiers and use ground-truth answers or expert annotation (R1-Zero, DeepSeekMath-V2), and from test-time training on a single problem with continuous verifiable rewards (TTT-Discover), not from pure zero-data loops. In the near term, growth in weight-level self-improvement is more likely to come from expanding verifiable domains and scaling verification compute than from removing external anchors (our inference). Based on [^267][^250][^157].
