## 5. Weight-Level Self-Improvement (Part I): From Bootstrapping to Self-Rewarding (2017–2024)

This chapter covers weight-level self-improvement: a model generates data, which, after being filtered or scored by some signal, are used to update the weights of the same model (or its successor), and the process can be repeated over multiple rounds. Chronologically it runs from Expert Iteration and AlphaZero in 2017 to WebRL at the end of 2024, 30 papers in all (our count). Four questions run through the chapter: where the filtering signal comes from (rule and execution checks, gold-standard answers, learned reward models, model self-evaluation or humans); whether the improver itself (search expert, verifier, judge) is also improved; how many rounds a loop can run before it saturates or reverses; and which parts of the outermost layer always stay fixed. The overall judgment is that the evidence from this period strongly supports "bounded self-improvement", that is, stable gains over one to four rounds under the constraint of external anchors, while support for "recursive (compounding) self-improvement" comes essentially only from game environments with closed rules and rewards that the policy cannot exploit (our assessment).

### 5.1 Precursors of Search–Distillation Self-Play: ExIt and AlphaZero

Expert Iteration (ExIt) splits sequential decision-making into planning and generalization: tree search plans new policies, a deep network generalizes these plans, and the network policy then guides the search, raising the strength of new plans[^187]. In each iteration, positions are generated by self-play of the previous-generation apprentice network, a new apprentice is trained to imitate the search expert, and the new apprentice is then used to update the expert, i.e., "expert improvement"[^187]. Unlike methods that can only imitate a fixed initial expert, ExIt can build stronger experts for itself, so an expert is available throughout training[^187]. This is the earliest and cleanest meta-level instance in this chapter: the improver that produces the training targets (the search expert) grows stronger together with what it improves (our assessment).

Trained from scratch, ExIt beat MoHex 1.0, the most recent publicly released Olympiad champion program, at the board game Hex[^187]. With 10,000 search iterations, ExIt had a win rate of 75.3% against MoHex with the same number of iterations and 59.3% against MoHex with 100,000 iterations[^187]. The policy improved steadily in every iteration, later apprentices were clearly stronger than earlier experts[^187], and the training curves still showed no convergence when the results were reported[^187]. The authors also give an upper bound on the per-iteration gain: the gap between the apprentice and the improved expert sets the upper bound on the new apprentice's performance in each iteration[^187]. In implementation, ExIt redoes imitation learning from scratch at each step and discards the old dataset, whereas the online version instead aggregates all data collected so far[^187]; the network weights are reinitialized in each iteration and then trained on the new data[^187]. What accumulates across iterations is data and expert strength, not one and the same set of parameters (our assessment).

The ExIt authors regard AlphaGo Zero as an independently developed version of ExIt[^187]. AlphaZero, in turn, generalizes the approach of AlphaGo Zero into a single algorithm[^120]. Starting from random play and given no domain knowledge beyond the rules, it reached superhuman level in chess, shogi and Go within 24 hours, defeating a world-champion program in each[^120]. The training signal is the game outcome as determined by the rules[^120], and the updated parameters are used directly for subsequent self-play[^120]. It drops AlphaGo Zero's best-player gating (a new player had to beat the best player by a 55% margin to replace it) and maintains a single, continually updated network[^120]. AlphaZero surpassed Stockfish after 4 hours of training (300k steps), Elmo after less than 2 hours (110k steps) and AlphaGo Lee after 8 hours (165k steps)[^120]. The cost was 5,000 first-generation TPUs generating self-play games and 64 second-generation TPUs training the network[^120], and a separate instance was trained for each game[^120].

These two works established the template that later LLM self-training would borrow again and again: use search (or "sampling plus filtering") as the policy-improvement operator to produce targets better than the current policy, then distill them back into the network (our assessment). They also set out the conditions under which the template can keep compounding: the reward is given by the rules at the end of the game and cannot be "persuaded" by the policy; the training problems arise from self-play and automatically become harder as the opponent grows stronger (our assessment). Removing best-player gating means that every parameter update feeds directly into the next round of data generation, a practice that is safe only when the reward cannot be exploited by the policy (our inference). Most of the saturation and reversals in the LLM loops discussed later can be traced to one of these two conditions failing to hold (our inference).

### 5.2 Bootstrapping on Verifiable Self-Generated Data: From STaR to Qwen2.5-Math

STaR lists ExIt as a source of inspiration[^209]. In effect, it replaces the "search expert" with "sampling with answer checking" (our assessment). Its loop runs as follows: generate rationales for a large number of problems; for wrong answers, regenerate with the correct answer given as a hint (rationalization); fine-tune on all rationales that end in a correct answer; repeat[^209]. Each time a new batch of data is collected, training restarts from the original pretrained model to avoid overfitting[^209]. On GPT-J (6B), STaR performs comparably on CommonsenseQA to a fine-tuned model 30 times larger (72.5% vs. 73.0%)[^209]. On arithmetic, accuracy reaches 89.5% after 16 iterations[^209]. On GSM8K, direct fine-tuning scores 5.8 and STaR with rationalization 10.7[^209]; these results were obtained after 36 iterations without rationalization plus 10 iterations with rationalization[^209]. Its limitations foreshadow the limits of the answer-filtering methods that followed: the first round requires better-than-chance few-shot performance, and GPT-2 could not bootstrap even on arithmetic[^209]; data biases that are "useful" for solving problems get amplified[^209]; and without rationalization the loop eventually stops solving new problems, because problems it gets wrong provide no training signal[^209]. Although STaR runs many iterations, what it does in substance is extract, from a fixed problem set, the part of the capability that answer checking can cover (our assessment).

Inspired by AlphaZero, Haluptzok et al. have a model synthesize its own programming puzzles and solutions, filtered by a Python interpreter[^84], and fine-tuning on its own verified data more than doubles test accuracy[^84]. Pass@100 for Neo-2.7B after fine-tuning for 1 epoch is 7.5% for the baseline, 20.6% with self-generated and verified data, 21.5% with Codex-generated unverified data and 38.2% with Codex-generated and verified data[^84]. The authors attribute a considerable part of the gain to interpreter filtering[^84]; the gain from a second round was already small, and because of the cost they stopped after two rounds[^84]. The gain depends mainly on the filtering signal and the strength of the generator; whether the data are generated by the model "itself" is not critical (our assessment).

LMSI switches the signal to self-consistency: it samples multiple chains of thought for unlabeled questions, selects high-confidence paths by majority vote and then fine-tunes on them[^109]. PaLM-540B improves from 74.4% to 82.1% on GSM8K[^109], with gains of 1.1% to 7.7% across six datasets, all without ground-truth labels at any point[^109]. After self-improvement, however, the optimal sampling temperature is higher than the original 0.7, which the authors attribute to reduced output entropy[^109]. As far as we have read, the paper reports only one round of self-training; the reduced entropy also suggests that majority-vote self-labeling mainly sharpens the existing distribution (our assessment).

ReST casts "sample–filter–fine-tune" as offline RL with a Grow/Improve structure: Grow generates data from the current policy, and Improve fine-tunes on the filtered data[^177]. Each subsequent Improve step significantly raises the average reward[^177], and a second Grow step adds another 5.3 points on IWSLT 2014 De-En[^177]. But the reward model is only a proxy for human preferences: by human evaluation a single Grow step is best, even though the reward keeps rising with more Grow steps[^177]; the authors also warn that repeated Grow steps increase the risk of overfitting to the reward model[^177]. ReST-EM builds on ReST with modifications[^133]; its procedure is "sample, filter by binary feedback, fine-tune, repeat a few times"[^133], and each round fine-tunes from the base pretrained model to reduce drift[^133]. PaLM 2-L's test accuracy improves by 6.34% on MATH and 6.4% on APPS[^133], significantly more than fine-tuning on human data alone[^133]. But the test gain on MATH shrinks after the first round, and APPS already regresses in the second round[^133]. Training-set performance rises linearly with the number of rounds while test-set performance does not[^133]; the authors argue that returns diminish beyond a few rounds, suggesting possible overfitting on a small number of training problems[^133]; the method also needs a manually collected problem set and a reward function that is hand-designed or learned[^133].

V-STaR points out that the methods above use only correct solutions and discard the incorrect ones, which often make up a large share[^48]; instead, it uses both correct and incorrect solutions to train a verifier with DPO[^48], with labels from ground-truth answers or test cases[^48]. Iteration makes the generator better, and the generator in turn supplies harder negatives to the verifier[^48]; at inference time the verifier picks the best of multiple candidates[^48]. Over multiple rounds both the reasoner and the verifier gradually improve, with test accuracy 4% to 17% higher than existing methods[^48], and on MBPP the verifier's gains across rounds are even larger than the generator's[^48]. This is rare evidence in this chapter of "the improver being iteratively improved and contributing more" (our assessment). However, putting the verifier into the training loop to filter data brought no substantial gain[^48].

Iterative RPO targets the problem that iterative preference optimization yields little on reasoning tasks[^257]. In each round it builds preference pairs on the rule "correct answers win, incorrect answers lose", trains with DPO plus an NLL term, and continues training from the previous round's model[^257]; the authors consider the NLL term crucial[^257]. Llama-2-70B-Chat improves from 55.6% to 81.6% on GSM8K and from 12.5% to 20.8% on MATH[^257]. On GSM8K the round-by-round scores are 73.1, 78.0, 81.1 and 81.6[^257], and the authors also state that the gains in reasoning eventually saturate[^257].

ReST-MCTS\* and AlphaLLM bring ExIt/AlphaGo-style search back into LLM self-training (our assessment). ReST-MCTS\* criticizes outcome-only filtering: a correct answer does not mean the whole reasoning chain is correct[^14]. Given oracle final answers, it estimates the probability that each step leads to the correct answer and uses it as a process reward[^14]; these rewards are used both to keep training the process reward model and to select trajectories for the policy's self-training[^14]. At the same token budget, the iteratively trained value model reaches 48.5% on MATH when used for verification, above self-consistency's 42.5%[^14]. But on the three-benchmark average for LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, the round-0 zero-shot and few-shot baselines score 24.78 and 28.99, respectively, while ReST-MCTS\* scores 29.02 after two rounds[^14]; the authors state that evaluation is zero-shot only and the few-shot baseline serves only as a reference[^14]. AlphaLLM draws on AlphaGo, combining prompt synthesis, MCTS and three critic models[^262], and its headline results are GSM8K rising from 57.8 to 92.0 and MATH from 20.7 to 51.0[^262]. But these rely on ηMCTS decoding at inference time; with greedy decoding the scores are only 73.7 and 23.6, while Llama-2-70b after SFT scores 69.3 with greedy decoding[^262]. The critic models stay static within the framework[^262]. AlphaLLM describes itself as needing no additional annotations[^262], but the authors also state that it uses final-answer annotations[^262]. A considerable part of the gain from search is not distilled into the weights, which is the same issue as the upper bound set by the apprentice–expert gap in ExIt (our assessment).

Quiet-STaR generalizes STaR in another direction: it generates "thoughts" at every token to explain the text that follows, and uses REINFORCE to raise the probability of thoughts that help prediction[^203]. After continued pretraining of Mistral 7B on web text, zero-shot GSM8K rises from 5.9% to 10.9%[^203], without task-specific fine-tuning[^203]. Here the "verifier" is the likelihood of the corpus itself, so the approach can be extended to unlabeled text (our assessment). The cost is that many tokens have to be generated before each token is produced[^203].

Qwen2.5-Math assembles these practices into an industrial pipeline and describes self-improvement as running through pretraining, post-training and inference[^214]. The previous-generation Qwen2-Math-72B-Instruct synthesized math pretraining data, forming Qwen Math Corpus v2 of more than 1 trillion tokens[^214], whereas the previous version of the corpus had about 700 billion tokens[^214]. In post-training, the reward model drives iteration of the SFT data, and the stronger SFT model is in turn used to retrain the reward model[^214]; the RL reward is jointly composed of a rule-based verifier that checks against gold-standard answers and the reward model[^214]. Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct outperforms the previous-generation 72B model, scoring 83.6 and 85.3 on MATH in the CoT and TIR settings, respectively[^214]. The meta-level updates here are real: the reward model is retrained across rounds, and each generation's model writes corpus data for the next; but every step is anchored to labeled answers, and as far as we have read there is no stage-by-stage ablation (our assessment).

The methods in this section share a common structure: the training problems (or the seeds used to generate them) and the corpora are supplied by humans, the final basis for checking (gold-standard answers, an interpreter, a majority-vote rule, text likelihood or a fixed reward model) does not change over the loop, and the model is responsible only for generating candidates (our assessment). Although V-STaR, ReST-MCTS\* and Qwen2.5-Math retrain their verifiers or reward models, their signals remain anchored to ground-truth answers or to rule-based verifiers that check against gold-standard answers (our assessment, based on [^48][^14][^214]). Under this structure, improvement with external checking is reliable, its ceiling is set by the capabilities the problem set covers, and for most methods the gains narrow markedly after two to three rounds (our assessment).

### 5.3 Self-Generated Instructions and AI Feedback: Self-Instruct, Constitutional AI and Their Successors

Alignment and instruction following have no gold-standard answers, so the signal source shifts to heuristic filtering, human-written principles and AI labelers (our assessment).

Self-Instruct starts from 175 human-written seed tasks[^197], has an off-the-shelf LM generate new instructions and instances, filters out low-quality or similar ones and adds the rest back to the task pool[^197], and then uses this data to fine-tune the very GPT-3 that generated it[^197]. After fine-tuning on 52K self-generated instructions, GPT-3 improves by 33% absolute on SuperNI[^197], and its ROUGE-L rises from 6.8 to 39.9, close to the 40.8 of InstructGPT001[^197]. A review of a sample, however, shows that only 58% of the outputs are correct and acceptable, and 54% have all fields valid[^197]. Instruction backtranslation (Humpback) replaces the hand-written filtering rules with model self-evaluation: a seed model generates instructions for web documents (self-augmentation) and then selects high-quality examples (self-curation)[^245], after which the improved model re-curates the data and is trained again, for two rounds in total[^245]. The curator does get better: on a 250-example development set labeled by the authors, M1 has a precision and recall of 0.52 and 0.44, higher than M0's 0.44 and 0.09 but far below GPT-4's 0.88 and 0.92[^245]. The 65B Humpback scores 83.71 on the Alpaca leaderboard judged by GPT-4, above Guanaco's 71.80[^245]. The key negative result is that with self-augmented data alone and no self-curation, instruction following does not improve and even declines as more data is added[^245]; self-curated data used on its own also does worse than simply expanding the seed data, and yields a large improvement only when trained jointly with the seed data[^245].

Constitutional AI (CAI) compresses human oversight into a list of principles and uses no human labels identifying harmful outputs[^27]. The supervised stage fine-tunes on responses revised after self-critique[^27]; in the RL stage the model compares two samples, and these AI preferences are used to train a preference model that serves as the reward, i.e., RLAIF[^27]. The preference model is mixed, however, as helpfulness still relies on human labels[^27]. RL-CAI is significantly more harmless than RLHF[^27], but overtraining produces Goodharting, which shows up as overly harsh or boilerplate responses[^27]. The authors envision that AI supervision could in future be used for iterated "online" training[^27]. From our reading, the paper itself does not carry out such iteration (our assessment). SELF-ALIGN reuses the Self-Instruct mechanism and introduces principles[^246], fine-tuning the base model on the responses the model generates according to those principles[^246]. Dromedary, obtained with fewer than 300 lines of human annotation (including 16 principles), surpasses Text-Davinci-003 on several benchmarks[^246]; the costs are that the Verbose Cloning step hurts multiple-choice benchmarks[^246] and that the model occasionally produces hallucinations that violate the principles[^246].

RLAIF vs. RLHF shows that AI labeling can match human labeling on summarization and dialogue tasks[^266]; in harmless dialogue, the harmless rate of RLAIF is 88% versus 76% for RLHF[^266]. In the main experiments, however, the labeler model is far larger than the policy being trained[^266]. RLAIF with a labeler of the same size is preferred over SFT 68% of the time, and the authors stress that this is not strict self-improvement, because the labeler and the initial policy are not the same checkpoint[^266]. d-RLAIF, whose feedback model is the same checkpoint as the initial policy, has a 66% win rate against SFT on helpful dialogue, which the authors call strict self-improvement[^266]. In all settings the labeler is never updated (our assessment). Both RLAIF and RLHF policies tend to generate longer responses[^266], and summaries also often end with extraneous symbols, possibly a result of reward hacking[^266].

Toolformer uses "whether it can lower the loss on subsequent tokens" as a self-supervised filter for the API calls that the model itself samples and executes, and then fine-tunes itself on the augmented data[^228]. With this, the 6.7B GPT-J outperforms the larger GPT-3 on several downstream tasks[^228], but the smallest models cannot benefit[^228], and sample efficiency is also very low[^228]. The authors put forward applying the method iteratively as one possible way to improve sample efficiency[^228]. From our reading, the paper itself does only a single pass (our assessment). MART has an adversarial LLM and a target LLM co-evolve: each round, the former generates stronger attacks against the updated target, and the latter undergoes safety fine-tuning[^88], with the target model assessed by feedback from two reward models, one for safety and one for helpfulness[^88]. After 4 rounds, the violation rate falls by up to 84.7% as judged by the reward models and by 53.7% as judged by humans[^88]; the final round converges on the generated adversarial prompts[^88], and about 2,400 manually curated seed prompts are still needed[^88]. OMNI uses a foundation model as a "model of interestingness" to choose the next task for an RL agent[^308], which the authors see as AI selecting its own learning tasks and thereby fostering self-improvement[^308]. In Crafter, the OMNI agent learns 82 tasks, versus 55 for a baseline that uses learning progress alone[^308]. The authors expect, however, that by Goodhart's law the model of interestingness will expose pathologies once it becomes an optimization target[^308].

The work in this section compresses human input from item-by-item labeling into seeds, principles and fixed judge models, but does not eliminate it; apart from Humpback's curator, the labelers of CAI and RLAIF and the reward models of MART are not updated during training (our assessment, based on [^27][^266][^88]). The RLAIF authors estimate that LLM labeling is more than 10 times cheaper than human labeling[^266]. In this period, the main contribution of AI feedback was to lower labeling costs, not to build a self-reinforcing signal source (our assessment).

### 5.4 The Model as Its Own Judge: Self-Rewarding, Meta-Rewarding and Bootstrapping Evaluators

Self-rewarding methods have the same model both generate responses and score them[^66][^256]. The judge is the very model being trained and therefore changes along with training; if the judge's ability improves along with it, the meta level is opened up (our assessment). The evidence in this section shows that this opening is real but shallow, and that it reverses when there is no external anchor (our assessment).

The starting point of Self-Rewarding Language Models is that reward models trained from human preferences may be bottlenecked by the level of human performance and, once frozen, cannot keep improving during training[^66]. It has the model score its own candidate responses with LLM-as-a-Judge prompting[^66], and each subsequent round is initialized from the previous round's model and trained with DPO[^66]. The seed is Llama 2 70B fine-tuned on Open Assistant[^66], and the instruction seed set consists of 3200 human examples[^66]. The AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate against GPT-4 Turbo is 9.94%, 15.38% and 20.44% over the three rounds[^66]. The judge itself also improves: its pairwise accuracy in agreement with human rankings rises from 78.7% to 80.4% and then to 81.7%[^66]. The authors' caveats matter just as much: the effect likely saturates in realistic scenarios[^66]; only three rounds were run, in a single setting[^66]; generation length grows with iterations[^66]; both the training reward and the final evaluation (GPT-4) are given by language models, which calls for deeper analysis[^66]; and on most NLP benchmarks the results are on par with the baseline[^66].

Meta-Rewarding argues that existing methods mainly improve responses rather than judgments, which makes iterative training saturate quickly[^134]; when judging ability does not improve, training of the actor may saturate quickly or even overfit the reward signal (reward hacking)[^134]. It introduces a third role, the "meta-judge", to evaluate the model's own judgments[^134], and, to curb length bias, picks the shorter response when scores are close[^134]. The length-controlled win rate of Llama-3-8B-Instruct on AlpacaEval 2 rises from 22.92% to 39.44%[^134]. On Arena-Hard, Self-Rewarding with length control scores 23.2%, 26.3%, 28.2% and 27.3% over four rounds, falling back in round 4, while Meta-Rewarding scores 25.1%, 27.4%, 27.6% and 29.1%[^134]. Compared with Self-Rewarding at the same round, its judge's agreement with GPT-4 judgments is up to +12.34% higher (Self-Chosen Pairs, round 2)[^134]. The limits are equally clear: without human annotation, judge quality can only be measured by correlation with gpt-4-1106-preview[^134]; the 5-point scale pushes responses ever closer to full marks, making improvements hard to detect[^134]; the meta-judge's positional bias hindered improvement in round 3, and score inflation by the judge accelerated saturation[^134]; and the judge's evaluation of responses it did not generate itself improves only to a limited extent[^134].

Self-Taught Evaluators directly makes the evaluator the object of improvement and uses only synthetic data[^137]. It builds contrasting pairs by treating a high-quality response to a modified, related instruction as the "worse" response[^137], trains the judge to output reasoning and a verdict, keeps only the samples whose verdicts are correct, and repeats this round after round[^137]. The RewardBench score of Llama3-70B-Instruct rises from 75.4 to 88.3, whereas training from the same seed on the labeled HelpSteer2 reaches only 85.6[^137]. The overall scores by round are 83.9, 86.0, 87.5, 87.7 and 88.3, while the easier Chat subset falls from 97.6 to 96.6[^137]; on MT-Bench, agreement with human judgments changes only from the seed's 77.8 to 78.9[^137]. The method also depends on a seed model that is already fairly well aligned with human preferences[^137]. After the third round, the gain in the overall score narrows markedly while human agreement barely moves, indicating that the evaluator's progress is concentrated on the kind of hard contrasts that RewardBench covers (our assessment).

CREAM gives the clearest reversal curve in this chapter (our assessment). The authors observe that on models of about 7B, the gains from self-rewarding may in some cases diminish after a few rounds, and hypothesize that the cause is bias accumulated in the reward system[^256]. CREAM argues that prompt-based scoring may suit only larger models such as Llama-70B[^256], so it switches to the implicit DPO reward[^256] and regularizes training with the consistency between the previous round's reward-model ranking and the current ranking[^256]. In six-round experiments on Llama-3 (averaged over five benchmarks), the score after SFT is 80.24[^256]. Unregularized SRLM falls round by round from 80.99 at M2 to 68.59 at M6[^256]. CREAM reaches 83.65 at M4 and then slips slightly to 82.73[^256]. On this basis the authors conclude that CREAM converges in round 4, while SRLM starts to degrade from round 2 onward[^256]. P-SRLM, which uses prompt-based scoring, scores only 77.62 at M2, below SFT[^256], and the authors state that prompt-based scoring is ineffective for small models, with performance declining from the first round[^256]. Because SRLM and P-SRLM are listed separately, the former should be using the implicit reward, as CREAM does, so the six-round degradation is not unique to prompt-based judges (our inference). The Oracle variant, which replaces the reward model with an external InternLM2 and draws on downstream-task labels, is the best overall[^256], from which the authors conclude that unreliable rewards are the key challenge for such methods[^256]; and overall ranking accuracy, CREAM included, does not exceed 70%[^256].

SPIN and SPPO represent another route: self-play, but with the anchor outside the model (our assessment). SPIN has the new model distinguish the previous round's self-generated responses from human-annotated responses[^8], and the global optimum of its objective is attained only when the policy matches the human data distribution[^8]. The Open LLM Leaderboard average of zephyr-7b-sft-full rises from 58.14 to 63.16, with per-round gains of +2.66, +1.32, +0.85 and +0.19[^8]. Continuing SFT on the same SFT dataset, Ultrachat200k, plateaus or even declines, whereas SPIN improves round after round using only a 50k subset of it[^8]; but the authors acknowledge that the fixed human target distribution puts a ceiling on performance[^8]. SPPO treats alignment as a constant-sum game and approximates the Nash equilibrium through iterative self-play[^152], using only 60k prompts and PairRM, a preference model with 0.4B parameters[^152]. The length-controlled win rate of Mistral-7B over three rounds is 24.79, 26.89 and 28.53, versus 23.81, 24.23 and 22.30 for iterative DPO in the same table[^152]. On the Open LLM Leaderboard, however, DPO, IPO and SPPO all decline after the first or second round, which the authors attribute to the alignment tax[^152].

Three lines of work do open up the meta level: Self-Rewarding's judge raises its pairwise accuracy by about three percentage points within three rounds, Meta-Rewarding specifically trains the judge, and Self-Taught Evaluator takes the evaluator itself as its object (our assessment, based on [^66][^134][^137]). They share three limitations (our assessment). First, the judge's improvement is concentrated on the model's own distribution, while agreement with humans stays nearly flat (our assessment, based on [^134][^137]). Second, response quality is mostly judged by GPT-4-class models, which belong to the same class as the training signal, raising the risk of circular validation (our assessment, based on [^66][^152]). Third, on small models without an external anchor, self-rewarding reverses after a few rounds, and an external reward model remains the upper bound (our assessment, based on [^256]). SPIN and SPPO do not collapse because their anchor does not drift with the model, at the cost that the ceiling is also set by the anchor (our assessment, based on [^8][^152]).

### 5.5 Learning to Self-Improve: Self-Correction and Self-Generated Curricula

Another "meta" approach is not to improve the judge but to turn improvement itself into a skill the model can learn, or to let the model's failures generate the tasks for the next stage (our assessment).

RISE (Recursive Introspection) starts from the observation that even the strongest closed-source LLMs, once told they have made a mistake, cannot keep improving their own responses[^38]. It recasts a single-turn "prompt–oracle answer" dataset as a multi-turn MDP[^38], in which k−1 turns rolled out by the current model are followed by an improved response, which comes from self-distillation (the best of several samples from the model itself) or from a stronger model[^38]. When iterating, restarting from the base model and training on the data from all rounds works better than continuing from the previous round's checkpoint[^38]. On GSM8K, using entirely their own data, LLaMa3-8B improves by 8.2% and Mistral-7B by 6.6%[^38]. Without an oracle, the five-turn introspection gains of LLaMa2 on GSM8K after training rounds 1 and 2 are 15.1% and 17.7%, respectively[^38]. Five-turn introspection improves Mistral-7B by 23.9%, but GPT-3.5 by only 4.6%[^38]. The SCoRe authors take a dimmer view of this: RISE relies mainly on oracle teacher supervision, and the preliminary results of its self-correction training show only small improvements within five turns[^132]. Before collecting on-policy data, RISE also usually first runs a round of supervised fine-tuning on multi-turn trajectories generated by a stronger model, which the authors call the "knowledge boosting" stage[^38]. RISE is trained for no more than two rounds, and for no more than one when the supervision comes from the model itself[^38]. The pure self-distillation version does not exceed the fine-tuned model's first-turn majority-vote performance (m5@t1)[^38], and the authors argue that it must first catch up with best-of-N sampling before it can possibly surpass it[^38]. For comparison, prompting-only Self-Refine lowers the performance of all models when there are no oracle hints or human feedback[^38].

SCoRe argues that self-correction has so far been largely ineffective in modern LLMs[^132] and proposes multi-turn online RL that uses entirely self-generated data[^132], while still assuming access to a reward function that evaluates outputs[^132]. Its second stage rewards "progress toward self-correction" rather than final correctness[^132]. The self-correction of Gemini 1.0 Pro and 1.5 Flash improves by 15.6% and 9.1% on MATH and HumanEval, respectively[^132]. SFT on offline correction traces either suffers from distribution mismatch or collapses into a single, ineffective mode of correction[^132]; SCoRe trains for only one round of self-correction, and subsequent rounds are not necessarily as effective[^132]. These two works move the improver from a judge outside the loop into the reasoning process, where it can be reused over multiple steps at inference time; but training still requires oracle answers or a reward function, and only one to two rounds are trained, so there is as yet no evidence of compounding at the training level (our assessment, based on [^38][^132]).

WebRL uses a self-evolving curriculum to train web agents built on open-source LLMs[^180], targeting the problem that DigiRL updates its policy only on a fixed task set[^180]. It generates new tasks from failed attempts, combined with an outcome-supervised reward model (ORM) and adaptive RL[^180]. The new tasks are generated by GPT-4o, and in each phase 500 instructions that meet the filtering criteria are selected from them[^180]. "Self-evolving" here means using the agent's own failures as seeds; the proposer is not the agent itself (our assessment). A new task is kept only if the critic's score falls between 0.05 and 0.75[^180], and infeasible tasks, as summarized from human review, are excluded by prompting GPT-4o[^180]. To curb drift, it constrains the current phase's policy not to deviate too far from the previous phase's[^180], and the replay buffer stores only successful trajectories from each phase[^180]. On WebArena-Lite, the success rate of Llama-3.1-8B rises from 4.8% to 42.4%, exceeding GPT-4-Turbo's 17.6%[^180]. In the training curves broken down by website, every site except Map shows a clear upward trend, while Map first rises and then falls[^180]. The ORM's accuracy is about 80%[^180]; the authors acknowledge that continued improvement depends heavily on the ORM[^180], and that the distribution drift brought about by online exploration may lead to forgetting and degradation[^180]. What WebRL improves is the task distribution; the paper shows no sign of the ORM being updated along with the curriculum (our assessment). Read together with OMNI, "setting one's own tasks" is safer than "grading oneself", because success and failure are still decided by the environment or a fixed reward; but in both cases the evaluator is frozen outside the loop (our assessment, based on [^308][^180]).

### 5.6 Cross-Cutting Findings: Saturation, Reversal, Anchors and "Data Compounding"

First, saturation is the norm, typically setting in between the second and fourth rounds (our assessment). In programming self-play, the gain in the second round is already small[^84]; ReST-EM regresses on APPS in the second round[^133]; and for ReST, a single Grow step is best as measured by human evaluation[^177]. SPIN's per-round gain falls from +2.66 to +0.19[^8], and SPPO's from +7.69 to +1.64[^152]; after the third round, Self-Taught Evaluator's RewardBench score rises only from 87.5 to 88.3[^137]; and MART converges in its final round[^88]. There are three kinds of exceptions that do not saturate within two to four rounds (our assessment). The first is closed, rule-governed games: when ExIt reported its results, the training curves had still not converged[^187]. The second is STaR-style loops, which retrain from the original model every round and repeat on a fixed problem set until performance plateaus[^209]. The third is WebRL's online curriculum, whose prompt-comparison experiment trained for eight phases[^180]; in the curves broken down by website, most sites keep rising, while Map first rises and then falls[^180]. The signals of the latter two remain anchored by gold-standard answers or an environment reward function (our assessment).

Second, when the judge is the model itself and is left unconstrained, saturation turns into reversal (our assessment). In CREAM's six-round experiments, unregularized self-rewarding drops from 80.99 at M2 to 68.59 at M6[^256]; the Self-Rewarding baseline reported by Meta-Rewarding falls back from 28.2% to 27.3% on Arena-Hard in round 4[^134]. Externally learned reward models show a similar phenomenon (our assessment). ReST's reward rises with the number of Grow steps, yet by human evaluation a single Grow step is best[^177]; CAI exhibits Goodharting after overtraining[^27]; and RLAIF policies append extraneous symbols to the end of summaries[^266].

Third, what truly stays fixed is the anchor (our assessment). Reasoning loops depend on human-collected problem sets and correct answers, which the authors of both ReST-EM and Iterative RPO list as a prerequisite[^133][^257], and the RL reward of Qwen2.5-Math also includes a rule-based verifier that compares against gold-standard answers[^214]. The anchors of alignment loops are human-written seeds and principles: the 175 seed tasks of Self-Instruct[^197], the 16 principles of SELF-ALIGN[^246] and the roughly 2,400 seed prompts of MART[^88]. SPIN takes human SFT data as its target distribution[^8], and SPPO uses PairRM, trained on human preferences, as its judge[^152]. There are also implicit anchors on the evaluation side: the instruction-following results of Humpback and SPPO are scored by GPT-4[^245][^152]. No work lets the model modify its own training algorithm, loss function or loop structure; changes at the meta level stop at the scorer and the task distribution (our assessment).

Fourth, for a substantial share of the methods, what accumulates across rounds is data rather than weights (our assessment). STaR and ReST-EM return to the original or base model every round[^209][^133], and RISE retrains from the base model while accumulating the data from all rounds[^38]. V-STaR's generator starts from the pretrained model each round and is fine-tuned on the accumulated correct solutions[^48], and ExIt reinitializes its network weights every round[^187]. The methods that carry weights forward include AlphaZero's single continually updated network[^120] and Iterative RPO, though the latter, conversely, does not reuse the data collected in earlier rounds[^257]. Self-Rewarding and Meta-Rewarding also continue training from the previous round's model[^66][^134], while WebRL needs a KL constraint and a replay buffer to guard against drift[^180]. Within this chapter's literature, the evidence of sustained compounding at the parameter level comes mainly from game self-play (our assessment).

### 5.7 Summary (our assessment)

1. The evidence for bounded self-improvement is sufficient: as long as an external anchor exists (rules, interpreters, gold-standard answers, unit tests), training on self-generated data delivers stable gains within one to four rounds, and these gains can be measured with metrics that do not depend on LLM judges. Based on [^84][^133][^257].
2. Compounding improvement is visible only in closed games. ExIt's unconverged training curves and AlphaZero's rise from random play to superhuman level depend on rule-based rewards that the policy cannot manipulate and on a problem distribution that automatically grows harder as the opponent grows stronger; not one of the LLM loops covered in this chapter meets both conditions at once. Based on [^187][^120].
3. LLM self-training loops generally saturate within two to four rounds; the longest self-evaluation loop in this chapter (six rounds) degrades significantly without regularization, and even with regularization it merely converges in the fourth round and then slips slightly, rather than continuing to rise. Based on [^256][^8][^152].
4. The meta level has been opened, but only shallowly: curators, verifiers, process reward models and LLM judges all have instances of being improved iteratively, but the improvements are limited in size, concentrated on the model's own distribution, and mostly measured by GPT-4 judgments or synthetically constructed labels; most of these experiments run for only two to five rounds, among which Self-Taught Evaluator already levels off after the third round. Based on [^134][^137][^48]. See also [^66][^14][^245].
5. No work modifies the training algorithm, the loss function or the loop structure itself; the outermost layer (seeds, principles, answers or reward functions, external evaluators) is always fixed by humans, and the "recursion" stops at the scorer and the task distribution. Based on [^197][^27][^214].
6. The signal source determines both the ceiling and the risk, and "who scores" matters more than "who generates": verified self-generated data and unverified data from a stronger model have similar effects, learned reward models are decoupled from human evaluation, and model self-evaluation reverses on small models. Based on [^84][^177][^256].
7. The carrier of cross-round compounding is often data rather than parameters: STaR, ReST-EM, RISE and others return to the base model every round and carry only the data, and the generator that produced it, into the next round; among the loops that carry weights forward, WebRL relies on KL and replay, and CREAM on a cross-round consistency constraint, to resist drift or the accumulation of bias. Based on [^209][^38][^180]. See also [^133][^256].
8. Self-correction and self-generated curricula turn the improver into a skill the model can learn or into a task distribution, offering another path toward recursive self-improvement; but RISE and SCoRe train for only one to two rounds, and the evaluators of WebRL and OMNI remain frozen, which is not yet enough to demonstrate compounding. Based on [^38][^132][^308].
