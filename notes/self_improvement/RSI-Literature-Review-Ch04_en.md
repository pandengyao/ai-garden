## 4. Theoretical Foundations: Formal Models, Definitions and Limit Theorems

This chapter reviews the theoretical literature on recursive self-improvement (RSI): definitions, theorems that give guarantees or set ceilings, and growth models that predict an "intelligence explosion." The 41 works it draws on run from OOPS in 2002 to the impossibility theorems of 2026-10, and 30 of them were published in 2025–2026 (our count). The chapter answers two questions. First, under what conditions can self-modification move from bounded self-improvement (converging under a fixed evaluator) to recursive (compounding) self-improvement (the improver itself is improved, and the successor improver is stronger under a comparable budget)? Second, can existing theory determine whether such a transition happens, and how fast? Throughout, the chapter follows two strands: the meta level (whether the improver is modified, and what is fixed at the outermost level) and the signal source (proof, execution-based verification, environment reward, model self-evaluation or human input).

### 4.1 From the Gödel Machine and OOPS to Self-Referential Learners

The formal lineage traced in this chapter begins with two IDSIA technical reports by Schmidhuber[^173][^7]. OOPS (Optimal Ordered Problem Solver) makes the programs of already solved tasks non-writable, and candidate programs for later tasks can copy, edit and execute this frozen code[^173]. The guarantee the author gives is n-bias-optimality, where n is a small constant, for example 8: if reusing old code is faster, OOPS will discover this, and otherwise it will not be harmed by the old solutions[^173]. In experiments, OOPS found a prefix that temporarily rewrites the distribution over its own suffixes, accelerating universal search by a factor of 1000, which the author calls self-improvement and "metasearching"[^173]. But this acceleration depends on an externally given ordered task sequence; given a single task, OOPS does not by itself invent subtasks ordered from easy to hard, and it has theoretical guarantees only in resettable environments[^173].

The Gödel machine pushes the scope of what can be modified to the limit: as soon as it finds a proof that some rewrite is useful, it rewrites any part of its own code, with the utility function, the hardware and the entire initial code all written as axioms in the proof searcher[^7]. Because the code must first prove that continuing to search for other rewrites is not more useful, the self-rewrite that is executed is globally optimal[^7]. A proof of the target theorem covers all later self-modifications affected by it, so all "meta-levels" collapse into one, and the utility function is rewritten only if the rewrite is provably useful according to the current utility function[^7]. The cost also comes from proof: even with unlimited resources, the machine must forgo improvements whose effectiveness it cannot prove, and with limited resources it can exploit only improvements that are provable within its time and space constraints[^7]. Schaul later stated bluntly that past designs for self-referential systems were "not (intended to be) practical"[^44].

AERA (Nivel et al., 2013, with Schmidhuber among the coauthors), presented under the title "Bounded Recursive Self-Improvement," explicitly contrasts itself with this line of work[^5]. In AERA, the utility of an improvement is established not by expensive search and formal proof but by observing its effect on the performance of the system[^5]. The system continually adds and removes very small parts, and what ultimately survives is "the best available" rather than "the best possible"[^5]. The authors state that this self-improvement can be sustained autonomously after the system leaves the lab, but always within the boundaries set by its designers[^5]. After about 20 hours of observation, the prototype S1 autonomously acquired the skill of real-time multimodal dialogue with humans, with no errors in its subsequent application[^5]. The authors also acknowledge that with insufficient input or an adversarial environment the rewrites would be useless, and that the scalability needed to support a general claim cannot yet be assessed[^5].

In 2022, Irie et al., as well as Kirsch and Schmidhuber, applied the self-referential idea to modern neural networks[^146][^70]. SRWM (self-referential weight matrix, ICML 2022) revisits Schmidhuber's self-referential weight matrix of the '90s with modern techniques and treats the weight matrix as the program of the network: only the initial weights are trained by gradient descent, and at run time all weights can be modified by the network itself; the authors say that in principle it can achieve "meta-meta-learning" in the sense of recursive self-improvement[^146]. In a multi-task setting with six ProcGen environments and 300M steps, SRWM's normalized test score is 20.0, slightly above DeltaNet's 18.6, while a control version without self-modification scores 15.3[^146]. The authors note that their first set of experiments did not specifically measure the capacity for self-improvement, and that there is no guarantee that self-modification preserves the original goal[^146]. FME (fitness monotonic execution) goes further and eliminates the explicit meta-optimizer: every change to the model is a self-modification, and the better a solution performs, the more often it is executed[^70]. On a two-armed bandit, a solution that always picks the high-payoff arm emerges after about 40 self-modifications and selections[^70]. On Cartpole, the self-modifications improve not only the policy but also future improvements, and hill climbing with tuned noise cannot reach the same rate of improvement[^70]. The authors also admit that the empirical evaluation is still brief[^70].

This lineage supplies the template for almost all later RSI mechanisms: accepted modifications are retained, and a fixed outermost layer adjudicates, for example the axioms and utility function of the Gödel machine, the task order of OOPS, the designer-set boundaries of AERA, the outer gradient descent of SRWM and the selection rule of FME (our assessment, based on [^7][^173][^5]). The stronger the adjudicating signal, the stronger the guarantee and the higher the cost of implementation: proof gives global optimality but is hard to implement, while observation and selection are easy to implement but can retain only local improvements (our assessment, based on [^7][^5]). As for compounding, the evidence from this period is weak: the 1000-fold speedup of OOPS comes from an external task sequence and is a one-off, SRWM did not specifically measure self-improvement, and only FME, on a toy task, observed "improving future improvements" (our assessment, based on [^173][^146][^70]).

### 4.2 Definitions and Taxonomies: From "Better at Improving Itself" to Structural and Effective Recursion

The core of the early definitions is "becoming better at improving itself." Yampolskiy (2015) requires each new generation of software to be better than the original algorithm at optimizing future versions; linear scaling from merely adding resources does not count, because the system does not become better at improving itself[^126]. He also gives a convergence argument: an optimizing compiler applied to itself might improve by 20% the first time and by 3% the second, and after a few rounds converge to a fixed point of zero improvement[^126]. He further relays Mahoney's proof (the algorithmic complexity of the program at the nth iteration is no greater than O(log n)) and acknowledges that RSI was then still a purely theoretical concept[^126]. Wang (2018) formalizes a class of RSI systems as a sequence of programs: each program generates the next, and a candidate replaces the current program only if it is better according to a score function[^106]. Simulations show a logarithmic relation between running time and the number of programs, but the score function in them is precomputed, which takes longer than direct enumeration[^106]. Schaul (2024) gives a broader definition: if outputs affect future inputs and performance improves, that is self-improvement; it is recursive when the input and output spaces are compatible, and when that space is language it is called "Socratic learning"[^44].

Definitional work in 2026 splits "recursion" more finely (our assessment). Duan et al. require experience and feedback to be turned into persistent changes to the system itself (model parameters, harness or improvement policies), such that these changes can affect the generation, evaluation, selection and consolidation of subsequent self-improvements[^168]. Their autonomy levels run from executing prescribed improvements (L1) to improving the mechanisms that govern subsequent improvement (L5)[^168]. They point out that improved task performance does not by itself show that the improvement mechanism has been revised, retained and reused, and they distinguish "structural recursion" (a revised improvement mechanism governs a later round) from "effective recursion" (that mechanism produces stronger successors under comparable budgets and independent evaluation)[^168]. Lu subsequently pointed out that both kinds of recursion can occur within a single verification class[^236]. Tang et al.'s Generalized Agent Iteration (GAI) unifies iterative policy improvement (GPI) and RSI as a single paradigm and uses two "dials" to distinguish instances: whether the improvement mechanism belongs to the agent draws the line between GPI and RSI, and whether the evaluation standard is anchored outside the agent determines whether the system is anchored, goal-drifting or fully self-referential[^235]. Koch distinguishes four regimes by the depth of the modification target, the deepest being revision of the evaluation criterion, but holds that current systems do not yet support the conclusion that they "can endogenously revise their evaluation criteria"[^233].

Other work sets thresholds of varying strictness. The survey by Zhou et al. requires only that the system modify itself, retain the changes, and use the updated system to carry out subsequent tasks and further self-modifications[^124]. Zong et al., by contrast, require that the improvement procedure itself also be improved, and use this to distinguish recursive from iterative self-improvement[^119]. The Physical RSI framework for robots reserves L5 for independently verified recursive gain and stresses that improved task performance does not prove that the improvement procedure has become more effective[^65]. A survey of self-evolving coding agents likewise points out that passing tests does not mean the agent has become better; evidence is also needed that the retained changes benefit later decisions[^216]. Usage is broadest on the growth-model side: Ord applies the term RSI to the whole range of cases, from human-led to entirely AI-run, from rewriting itself to building a new system, and from intelligence "exploding" to quickly fizzling out[^12]. Cunningham et al. argue that concepts such as self-sustaining acceleration and intelligence explosion are often conflated with the term "RSI," and therefore turn instead to deriving the conditions under which self-sustaining acceleration arises[^35]. Burtsev, for his part, regards RSI as a property of an AI–R&D system rather than of an isolated agent[^75].

Synthesizing these definitions yields three progressively stricter conditions: the modification is retained and reused in later tasks; the modification reaches the improvement mechanism itself (structural recursion); and the successor improver is genuinely stronger under comparable budgets and independent evaluation (effective recursion) (our assessment, based on [^124][^168][^235]). The definitions of Duan et al. and Zong et al. explicitly require the first two, and Tang et al. draw the line by whether the improver belongs to the agent, which has a similar meaning; it is the third condition that is the key to distinguishing bounded from compounding improvement, and it is precisely the one that is hardest to guarantee in theory and least often measured in experiments (our assessment, based on [^168][^119][^235]). Another point of divergence is the system boundary: if the boundary is drawn around a single agent, human-designed loops and external verifiers often fall outside it; if it is drawn around the AI–R&D system, human researchers are also part of the improver, so "recursion" holds more easily but is harder to attribute to AI (our assessment).

### 4.3 Limits and Impossibility Theorems: What Is Fixed and What Is Ruled Out

Most of the theoretical results from 2025–2026 do not prove that RSI is feasible; instead, they answer how far self-modification can go at most when certain parts are held fixed (our assessment). Table 4-2 summarizes these results by "what is fixed".

Moser et al. ask whether automated "gates" such as reward models or automated reviewers can reliably decide whether to accept a self-update[^147]. They model agents as programs, with "improvement" defined by a semantic score that need not be computable, while the gate must be a terminating, computable procedure[^147]. The main theorem states that, in the fully general setting, no terminating program can be simultaneously reliable (never accepting a non-improvement) and non-conservative (never missing an available improvement); self-referential constructions can force any gate or searcher to fail, and the same holds for randomized gates with bounded error[^147]. The authors stress that this obstruction is structural rather than statistical: the proposer can exploit the evaluator as a predictable part of the environment[^147]. In the decidable regime of bounded evaluation and a finite proposal budget, exhaustive simulation can restore the guarantees, but a chain of accepted updates may still raise the bounded score in every round while the true score falls[^147]. The authors also note that these are worst-case results and do not imply that self-improvement will not occur[^147].

Two papers by Lu give analogous limits for the case in which the improver itself can also be modified[^169][^236]. In a relative-computability model, the solver, evaluator and improver are all mutable, yet every total decision procedure that effective self-revision produces over resource A still falls within the class C(A) of problems Turing-reducible to A[^169]. A learned solver can stop relying on the "teacher" on every input and yet remain in the same computability layer[^169]. Capability internalization amounts to a strict ascent only when the successor preserves the predecessor's resource and makes tasks outside the predecessor's layer available, and the theory offers neither improvement rates nor a general procedure for identifying beneficial revisions[^169].

Lu's other paper starts from verification complexity and first points out that a performance score cannot distinguish whether a system searched longer, received additional support or changed its proposal and verification mechanisms[^236]. It distinguishes "native reach" (inputs that have an accepted solution under default support) from the "closure frontier" (inputs that have an accepted solution under any support the interface already admits)[^236]. Making a few more attempts changes neither set; making optional feedback routine can enlarge native reach within the old frontier, while admitting previously forbidden interaction transcripts can enlarge the frontier itself[^236]. Its conclusion for RSI is that, under a common sound interpreter and a fixed verification protocol, uniformly bounded self-modification stays within the same verification class, and escape requires a failure of semantic authority or of exposure inclusion[^236]. It also constructs a family of examples in which the ratio of search success rates can grow without bound while the accepted languages remain unchanged[^236]. The author notes that these results allow empirical gains within the frontier, do not cover unbounded processes and have not been validated on large deployed systems[^236].

The "stationarity dichotomy" of Bobadilla-Suarez et al. gives an auditable version: when the reachable set of edits stays fixed, iterative self-modification runs into strict diminishing returns, and only an expansion of that set allows escape[^2]. Rewriting harness components such as tools, verifiers and task decomposition can expand the reachable range without touching the weights, so checking only whether the weights are frozen audits the wrong object[^2]. Empirically, best-of-k orchestration can only reach the ceiling of the best worker, and a majority of 30 same-family workers fail together on 23/55 (42%) of the tasks[^2]. On SWE-bench, per-round improvement tends toward zero, and code churn across 401 production sessions decays geometrically, with the same shape as a pre-AI human baseline[^2]. The authors acknowledge that the data cannot distinguish whether the cause is a genuinely fixed reachable class or the ordinary economics of editing[^2].

Cai et al. study harness self-evolution in which the base model is fixed and only prompts, code, tools and orchestration change, and prove that the performance ceiling of reliable self-evolution is determined by the costs of verification and evaluation; the closer the reward gets to its upper bound, the higher the evaluation cost that reliably validating smaller gains may require[^239]. In the DS-1000 example, a substantial share of candidates met the audit target, yet in the end only two passed validation and were adopted; the authors also caution that a ceiling imposed by the validation rule does not mean that further improvement is impossible[^239].

Tang et al. point out that the classical policy-iteration guarantees require the improvement step to be cheap and monotone, and both the objective and the evaluation instrument to lie outside the agent[^235]. Once the evaluator becomes a component of the agent, the object being evaluated and the instrument of evaluation coincide, and a self-consistent system may report improvement while having moved the reference standard[^235]. The empirical evidence they cite shows that 58.33% of settings already improve on the first valid attempt, but 78.26% of searches that continue past the best score end with a lower score[^235].

Another group of results gives positive but bounded guarantees (our assessment). The guarantees that Fu et al. give for self-rewarding language models show that a single update step depends heavily on the quality of the initial model, that under full iteration performance improves at a rate of Õ(1/√n), and that the dependence on the initial model decays exponentially with the number of iterations, although the analysis is limited to tractable settings such as linear models[^234]. Liu et al. (ICML 2026) model each round of self-improvement as maximum-likelihood fine-tuning on self-generated data filtered by an external verifier and reveal a feedback loop: a better model gets more data accepted in each round, which both sustains continued improvement and explains eventual saturation[^238]. In the experiments, Pass@1 often improves more slowly after t=2 and levels off clearly below 1, and when tasks are too easy or too hard relative to the model, improvement is limited or performance even degrades[^238]. Qiu et al. interpret debate, bootstrap and internal coherence maximization all as "coherence optimization" (debate is an exact instance, and the other two are closely related to it), prove that it is equivalent to description-length regularization, and give the condition under which self-improvement succeeds: the pretrained prior closely approximates the data-generating distribution[^237]. They run in-context learning on GSM8K with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct: at γ=0.25, mean training accuracy rises from 24.86% to 35.58%, with most of the gain occurring within about the first 20 rounds, after which it saturates; moreover, the results are in-sample[^237].

An empirical survey gives an ordering consistent with these theorems. Chen et al. review 1,250 arXiv papers from 2024–2026 and arrange signals into a verification hierarchy, from formal verifiers (strongest) to intrinsic self-evaluation (weakest)[^62]. They observe that the strength of demonstrated self-improvement tracks this hierarchy and that typical failures all stem from violations of it, and they state that this is a qualitative pattern rather than a measured law[^62]. The positive guarantees in this section fit the same pattern: when the signal comes from the model itself, the conclusions all depend on the quality of the initial model or the pretrained prior, and with an external verifier, what is proven is likewise improvement that saturates (our assessment, based on [^234][^237][^238]).

These results differ in form but point to the same "conservation" structure: as long as the verifier, oracle, reachable class of edits or validation rule stays fixed, self-modification cannot take the system certifiably beyond that class (our assessment, based on [^147][^169][^236]). "Modifying the improver" is not in itself enough to produce compounding: Lu's model allows the improver to be revised, and the conclusion is still that the system stays within C(A) (our assessment, based on [^169]). What can raise the ceiling is external expansion that the system itself cannot certify: new oracle access, new support, harnesses that extend the reachable range, and larger evaluation budgets (our assessment, based on [^236][^2][^239]). Yet the fixed evaluator is precisely where the guarantees come from; if it too is handed over to the system for modification, the ceiling and the guarantees disappear together, and GAI's "object and instrument coincide" and Moser et al.'s "proposer exploits the evaluator" describe the same phenomenon (our assessment, based on [^235][^147]). Most of these theorems are worst-case or class-membership results that say nothing about the speed of improvement within a class; they can neither rule out rapid progress in practice nor prove that progress will sustain itself (our assessment).

### 4.4 Growth Dynamics and Explosion Conditions

The previous section dealt with "whether it can be certified"; growth models deal with "how fast it will go". As early as 2012, Hutter distinguished a speed explosion from an intelligence explosion: a society of agents simulated on computers whose compute grows hyperbolically must undergo a speed explosion in physical time, but not necessarily an intelligence explosion[^52]. He also pointed out that the upper bound on intelligence in universal intelligence theory is "not necessarily" truly binding, and that a monotone transformation of the intelligence measure such as 1/(1−I) suffices to create a mathematical singularity[^52]. Most later models treat AI as R&D labor and describe progress with a scalar A; what they discuss is recursion at the level of the AI–R&D system rather than of a single agent (our assessment).

As for empirical inputs, Ho et al., drawing on evaluations of more than 200 language models from 2012–2023, estimate that the compute needed to reach a fixed level of performance halves about every 8 months (95% confidence interval about 5 to 14 months), but a Shapley analysis attributes 60–95% of performance gains to compute scaling, with algorithms accounting for only 5–40%[^95]. MacAskill and Moorhouse call the core mechanism the "software feedback loop" and stress that it is not inevitable, because most new technologies are at most a one-off multiplier on researcher productivity; they also introduce two diminishing-returns effects, "fishing out" and "stepping on toes"[^86]. By the estimates they cite, the chance that the software feedback loop produces acceleration is about fifty-fifty[^86]. They estimate that a century of progress in a decade would require research input to grow more than 600-fold over that decade, whereas AI's effective cognitive labor is currently growing more than twentyfold per year[^86].

Whitfill and Wu prove that an intelligence explosion has two necessary conditions: compute and cognitive labor must be elastically substitutable, and self-improvement must outpace the rate at which new ideas become harder to find[^105]. If the elasticity of substitution σ<1, a purely software-driven explosion occurs only when φ>1, which the authors consider quite implausible[^105]. Using panel data from OpenAI, DeepMind, Anthropic and DeepSeek for 2014–2024, they estimate σ = 2.58 under the "compute CES" specification and σ = −.10 under the "frontier experiments" specification, an estimate statistically indistinguishable from zero[^105]. They therefore argue that whether a purely software-driven explosion is feasible is highly sensitive to the structure of the research production function[^105].

Ord distinguishes superexponential growth from a singularity[^12]. In differential-equation models, superexponential growth only requires Ȧ to grow superlinearly in A, whereas a singularity requires Ȧ to grow slightly faster than every function of the form A·log(A)·log(log(A))…[^12]. For example, Ȧ = A·log(A) gives double-exponential growth but no singularity[^12]. His key variable is generation time, the time needed to go once around the feedback loop[^12]. Under time-embedded difference equations, a singularity occurs if and only if generation time satisfies the "Zeno condition" (infinitely many steps can be completed in finite time) and the increments satisfy the "unboundedness condition"; with a fixed generation time, no vertical asymptote appears however fast the improvements grow[^12]. Under this more realistic model, superexponential but non-singular growth looks more likely[^12]. The conclusion for measurement is negative: the singularity condition is a global property of the relationship between Ȧ and A, not a local property such as "elasticity > 1", so without strong assumptions about the functional form, local measurements can neither confirm nor rule out an explosion[^12]. He argues that requiring frontier labs to report the generation times of pretraining and RLVR post-training may be good policy, and stresses that the greater difficulty of singular growth does not mean that RSI is safe[^12].

Burtsev's delayed-feedback model comprises baseline research productivity, a recursive gain a, an end-to-end delay τ from a capability gain to the resulting rise in research productivity, the fraction χ of the gain that makes it through the development pipeline (called operational closure) and the frontier hardness σ[^75]. This yields the recursive reproduction number R_AI = χa/σ; when it exceeds 1, incremental improvements are amplified across development cycles[^75]. The critical boundary does not depend on baseline research throughput, so rapid capability growth and recursive self-amplification are two different things, and a system may already be supercritical before acceleration becomes visible[^75]. Frontier hardening, in turn, may pull a supercritical system back into the subcritical regime, making the amplification only temporary[^75]. In an example with the delay fixed at τ = 0.5 years, the rapid-transition scenario compresses the interval from AGI to ASI from 72 years to less than half a year[^75]. The author notes that a, χ and frontier hardness cannot be identified from existing evidence and can only serve as scenario parameters, and that crossing R_AI = 1 is only a local amplification condition[^75].

Cunningham et al. (whose authors include Whitfill and Wu) define "self-sustaining acceleration" as AI being sufficient to accelerate capability progress without any growth in exogenous inputs, and separately define "intelligence explosion" as capabilities tending to infinity in finite time[^35]. They draw growth models as directed graphs and prove that the net acceleration of capabilities depends on the product of the elasticities along each feedback loop[^35]. In the baseline model, self-sustaining acceleration occurs when ε_{A,A} > 1, that is, when a 1% increase in the stock of ideas raises the discovery of new ideas by more than 1%, whereas in traditional industries this elasticity is consistently well below 1[^35]. Calibrated with the Epoch Capabilities Index, the condition requires each one-unit increase in capability to raise AI R&D productivity by at least 15%[^35]. A rough calculation based on engineer uplift puts this return at about 9% since the launch of coding agents, below the threshold, though it has been rising recently[^35]. And even this 9% is obtained by assuming that a 4x uplift, which the authors consider "likely an overestimate", holds true[^35]. Burtsev also notes that Cunningham et al. independently arrived at a unit-threshold condition closely related to R_AI[^75].

Chan et al. compile the evidence for a "software-driven intelligence explosion", whose mechanism is that AI enlarges the effective R&D workforce, and this workforce in turn builds better AI[^210]. The key quantity is the returns to research effort r; when r > 1, the growth of the R&D workforce outpaces diminishing returns and brings acceleration[^210]. They cite central estimates by Ho and Whitfill for three AI subfields, with r between 1.2 and 1.9; averaging gives λ = 1.40 and β = 1.01, in which case each doubling takes about 76% as long as the previous one[^210]. If r holds and there are no other bottlenecks, the pace of AI progress would rise tenfold within about 1.5 years, by which point a year of progress at today's pace would take only about five weeks[^210]. The automation metric they cite shows that the share of R&D work completed autonomously with only high-level supervision rose from 1% to 26% between March and August 2026[^210]. Chan et al. argue that the productivity gains from R&D automation have not yet reached the threshold for triggering an intelligence explosion, but that the gains from new systems are likely approaching it[^210]. They also list four frictions: diminishing returns, compute and data, tasks that are hard to automate, and time-consuming R&D processes[^210]. Limited data indicate that if the compute needed for experiments grows in proportion to the scale of frontier training, a software-driven explosion would be impossible, and the estimates of r rely on imperfect proxy variables[^210].

Li et al. take the feedback model in Chan et al.'s working paper as a starting point and randomize it into a "Good process": improvements arrive at a rate proportional to A^ε, each multiplying A by (1+H), with H independent and identically distributed and strictly positive[^130]. Randomness does not change the explosion threshold but turns the explosion date into a distribution, and even when the parameters are known, the relative uncertainty about the remaining time does not vanish as the explosion approaches[^130]. In the delayed-feedback models they specify, a fixed deployment delay can prevent the explosion[^130]. The model excludes compute and data frictions, and the authors also state that the numerical examples are not forecasts[^130]. Harris has AIs design their descendants on a design tree while humans control fitness: without additional assumptions, fitness need not rise, and only under the assumptions of bounded fitness and η-locking does it concentrate on the maximum reachable value[^53]. If deceiving the evaluators can raise fitness further, evolution will select for both capability and deception[^53]. Researchers' views are similarly divided: of the 25 researchers interviewed by Field et al., 20 list automated AI research as one of the most severe and most urgent AI risks, while academic researchers are more skeptical of explosive growth[^189].

These models separate three things that are often conflated: local self-sustaining acceleration (elasticity > 1, r > 1, R_AI > 1), superexponential growth and finite-time singularity (our assessment, based on [^12][^75][^35]). Self-sustaining acceleration is a local condition that can hold for a time and then fail, whereas a singularity requires a global condition such as generation time rapidly approaching zero; even after entering the supercritical regime, therefore, the more likely form is superexponential but non-singular growth or temporary amplification (our assessment, based on [^12][^75]). Chan et al.'s r describes counterfactual dynamics after full automation, while Cunningham et al. measure the current R&D return per unit of capability; the two do not contradict each other, and both hold that the threshold has not yet been crossed (our assessment, based on [^210][^35]). Growth models treat capability as a scalar that can keep rising, whereas the theorems of the previous section show that certifiable capability under a fixed verifier is bounded; a bridge between the two bodies of literature is still missing (our assessment). Generation time and operational closure may be the entry point: an improvement counts only once it has been verified, integrated and passed on to a successor system, and verification cost is precisely the source of the ceiling on reliable self-evolution (our inference, based on [^12][^75][^239]).

### 4.5 Positions: Open-Endedness, Metacognition and Socratic Learning

Hughes et al. argue that open-ended, continually self-improving AI remains hard to achieve, but that the ingredients needed for open-endedness relative to a human observer are already in place, and that open-endedness is a necessary property of any superhuman intelligence (ASI)[^117]. Their definition is relative to an observer: a sequence of artifacts is open-ended only if it is both novel and learnable, so a foundation model trained on fixed data is not open-ended to an observer who can model its training set[^117]. They point out that self-improvement requires a scalable mechanism for evaluating one's own performance, identifying what can be improved and adjusting the learning process, and that the novelty of the AdA agent already begins to plateau after about 1 month of training[^117].

Schaul's "Socratic learning" concerns pure recursive self-improvement within a closed system: as long as the feedback is sufficiently informative and aligned with the goal, experience coverage is broad enough, and capacity and resources suffice, an agent can master any target capability, with the ceiling on improvement limited only by the resources invested[^44]. But the feedback cannot correct itself, preventing drift and collapse may be very difficult, and no current LLM training paradigm has a feedback mechanism adequate to support Socratic learning[^44]. He proposes "language games" with scalar scoring to provide both data generation and feedback, and envisions agents going on to generate games themselves, but this meta-game lacks a feedback mechanism as explicit as that of the inner games[^44]. He also argues that fully self-referential agents have the highest ceiling, because unfreezing fixed structures can only raise the asymptotic upper bound[^44].

Liu and van der Schaar contend that genuine self-improvement requires "intrinsic metacognitive learning", that is, an agent's ability to actively evaluate, reflect on and adapt its own learning process[^19]. They argue that existing systems rely mainly on fixed, human-designed extrinsic loops, which encode stationarity assumptions about the learning process and whose effectiveness declines once an agent's generative ability exceeds its self-evaluation ability[^19]. The authors also acknowledge that this route raises the problem of scalable and safe oversight[^19]. Behrouz et al.'s Nested Learning proposes a sequence model that modifies itself by learning its own update algorithm, but the authors note that catastrophic forgetting has not been solved in a general sense[^240].

Yang's PhD thesis characterizes "continual self-improvement" by three properties: writing new knowledge into parameters without catastrophic forgetting, generating one's own training signals and autonomously deciding the learning algorithm[^244]. For the third, the post-training recipe that execution-guided search found within ten rounds outperforms the GRPO baseline on a 1.5B math model (69.4% vs. 48.0%), and the pretraining recipe it found takes 19.7 minutes, versus 35.9 minutes for the nanoGPT baseline[^244]. The author acknowledges that these ideas were not tested for generalizability across scales, and that reinforcement learning with execution rewards raises the average reward rather than the maximum reward[^244]. Two peripheral works show that the implementation and verification capabilities of automated research are still weak. Zhu et al. argue that the fundamental bottleneck of AI scientists lies in the ability to execute the required verification procedures, citing the example that Claude 3.5 Sonnet scores only 1.8% on PaperBench[^243]. Xie et al.'s survey reports that o1-preview's medal rate on MLE-Bench is only 16.90%[^21].

The common direction of these positions is to turn the outermost layer of the improver from a loop fixed by humans into a learnable part inside the system (our assessment, based on [^19][^44][^244]). But all of them leave "who evaluates" as an open question, and once the evaluation standard is internalized, both the classical guarantees and faithfulness are lost (our assessment, based on [^235][^147]). What they argue for, therefore, is what recursive RSI requires, not that these requirements have been met, and the accompanying empirical results all remain within the range of bounded improvement (our assessment, based on [^117][^244][^70]).

### 4.6 Summary (our assessment)

- All self-improvement in this chapter's literature that can be formally guaranteed is bounded: from the axioms of the Gödel machine to the validation rule of harness evolution, the guarantees all come from a fixed outermost evaluator, which also sets the ceiling (based on [^7][^239][^147]).
- "Modifying the improver" is not the same as compounding. Even when the improver can be revised, effective, uniformly bounded self-modification still stays within the original computability layer or verification class; what can raise the ceiling is external expansion that the system cannot certify on its own (based on [^169][^236][^2]).
- Definitions have converged on three tiers, "retained, touches the improvement mechanism, stronger under comparable budgets and independent evaluation", but the third tier (effective recursion) has not been directly measured for any system in this chapter's literature; the closest is FME's observation on toy tasks (based on [^168][^65][^70]).
- Growth models distinguish self-sustaining acceleration, superexponential growth and finite-time singularity. A singularity requires generation time to approach zero rapidly; it is the strongest claim and the one with the least evidence (based on [^12][^75][^130]).
- On whether the acceleration threshold has been crossed, existing calibrations consistently give the verdict "not yet, but getting closer", while the parameter estimates depend on proxy variables and on how the production function is specified, so they should not be taken as forecasts (based on [^35][^210][^105]).
- Position papers advocate internalizing metacognition, judges and learning algorithms, yet the internalization of evaluation standards is precisely where the theoretical guarantees fail; until then, "recursion" is more likely to appear in AI–R&D systems that include humans and external verification than in a single autonomous agent (based on [^235][^19][^75]).
- For infrastructure readers, there are three testable practices: report generation times, compare successive improvement procedures under matched initial conditions and budgets, and audit the reachable set of edits rather than only the weights (based on [^12][^65][^2]).
