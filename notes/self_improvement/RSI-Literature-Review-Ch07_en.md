## 7. Self-Improvement with Frozen Weights: Prompts, Context, Memory and Skills

This chapter covers self-improvement that does not update the main model's weights: experience is written into artifacts outside the model, including revision feedback, reflections, insights, prompts, context playbooks, executable skills and even memory architectures, and the same frozen model then continues working conditioned on these artifacts. For every method in this chapter, the main model's weights stay frozen within the improvement loop (our assessment). Partial exceptions are that DSPy can optionally fine-tune small models in a pipeline[^175] and that MemSkill uses reinforcement learning to train a lightweight MLP controller[^271]. The chapter is organized around three questions: where improvements are stored and how broad their scope is; whether the improver itself is also improved, and which fixed components remain at the outermost level; and whether the signal comes from execution and tests, environment rewards, labeled data, LLM judges or model self-evaluation. The overall assessment is that this line of work provides abundant evidence of bounded self-improvement; within-loop compounding appears only in the form of skill composition; and designs that "improve the improver" all climb only one meta level, with a fixed outermost operator and very short runs, so they do not yet constitute evidence of recursive (compounding) self-improvement (our assessment).

### 7.1 In-Context Self-Feedback: Self-Refine, Reflexion and Negotiation Self-Play

The earliest works established the most basic form: a frozen model uses language feedback to improve its outputs or strategy in context. They demonstrated feasibility and also exposed three constraints: a capability threshold, signal quality and rapid saturation (our assessment).

Self-Refine has the same LLM first produce a draft, then write feedback on its own output and revise accordingly; past feedback and outputs are concatenated into the prompt, for at most 4 iterations[^269]. On 7 tasks, with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, it improves on single-pass generation by the same model by about 20% absolute on average[^269]. Reflexion parameterizes the policy as "a memory encoding plus a set of LLM parameters": the agent reflects verbally on task feedback and stores the reflections in episodic memory for later trials, usually keeping only 1–3 of them because of context limits[^268]. The signal can be binary environment feedback, predefined heuristics, or self-evaluation such as LLM binary classification or self-written unit tests[^268]. Reflexion reaches 91% pass@1 on HumanEval, compared with GPT-4's previous 80%[^268]. Relative to strong baselines, it achieves a 22% absolute improvement on AlfWorld in 12 learning steps[^268]. Negotiation self-play brings the same idea into a multi-agent game: the history of previous rounds and feedback from an AI critic serve as in-context examples to iteratively improve one party's negotiation strategy, with the deal price as the metric[^219].

The capability threshold appears consistently across the three works. Self-Refine requires the base model to have sufficient few-shot or instruction-following ability, and Vicuna-13B clearly struggles in the revision step[^269]. Reflexion finds that the ability to give explicit self-corrections emerges only in stronger, larger models[^268]. In negotiation, only strong, well-aligned models such as gpt-4 and claude-v1.3 can keep improving, while weaker models either do not understand the rules or cannot make use of the feedback[^219]. Signal quality is the second constraint. Self-Refine's gains on math are limited because the model cannot judge whether an answer is wrong; if an external source points out the errors, the gains can exceed 5%[^269]. Reflexion's self-written tests have a false-positive rate of 16.3% on MBPP Python and 1.4% on HumanEval Python[^268]. Saturation is the third constraint. Self-Refine's marginal improvement diminishes with each iteration[^269]. Only gpt-4 can keep improving over 5 rounds, other models saturate after about 3 rounds, and better prices also come with a higher risk of the deal falling through[^219]. On WebShop, which requires creative exploration, Reflexion still shows no progress after four trials[^268]. In Meta-TTL's comparison on Jericho text games, Reflexion's mean W-AUC is lower than that of the non-learning Static baseline on ID games (0.173 vs. 0.195) and only slightly higher on OOD games (0.241 vs. 0.228)[^270].

This layer supports bounded self-improvement: what is improved is the output for a single instance or a few reflections within a single task, the scope ends when the task ends, the reflection procedure is handwritten by humans and never changes, and the bottleneck lies in judging "what went wrong" rather than in generation (our assessment, based on [^269][^268]).

### 7.2 Accumulating Experience: Skill Libraries, Insight Memory and Context Playbooks

The second wave of work extends the retained artifacts into libraries accumulated across tasks. This produces the clearest evidence of within-loop compounding in this chapter, namely skill composition; it also shows that whether accumulation helps is decided by the rules for writing, merging and forgetting, and in every paper these rules are designed by humans and kept fixed (our assessment).

**Executable skills.** Voyager consists of an automatic curriculum, a skill library of executable code, and an iterative prompting mechanism that incorporates environment feedback, execution errors and self-verification; it uses GPT-4 through black-box queries, and new skills are stored in a vector database after verification[^90]. Complex skills are composed of simpler programs, which the authors argue lets capabilities compound rapidly over time[^90]. Compared with the previous best method, it obtains 3.3 times more unique items and unlocks tech-tree milestones up to 15.3 times faster[^90]. Without the skill library, the agent tends to stall in later stages, and removing self-verification reduces the number of discovered items by 73%[^90]. GPT-4 obtains 5.7 times as many unique items as GPT-3.5, and the authors state explicitly that GPT-3.5 and open-source models cannot provide the required code quality[^90]. LATM has GPT-4 write Python tools and verify them with unit tests before handing them to GPT-3.5 to use, matching the performance of using GPT-4 in both roles at a significantly lower inference cost[^277]. However, each task has only 3/3/240 training/validation/test instances, GPT-3.5 Turbo as the tool maker fails on all of the hard tasks, and improving existing tools is left to future work[^277]. OS-Copilot's FRIDAY autonomously proposes a task curriculum; a critic uses an LLM to score new tools on a 0–10 scale and keeps only those scoring above 8[^181]. It achieves 40.86% on GAIA level-1, against a previous best of 30.3%[^181]. But with self-directed learning turned off, it already reaches 36.56[^181]. In other words, more than half of its lead already exists without learning (our assessment). SkillWeaver has GPT-4o explore each website for 160 iterations, distills the practice experience into Playwright APIs, uses an LLM reward to judge whether a task is completed, and checks the APIs with automatically generated tests[^154]. It improves relative success rates on WebArena and on real websites by 31.8% and 39.8%, respectively, and after enough exploration, compositional APIs that call multiple simple APIs also emerge[^154].

**Natural-language experience memory.** ExpeL collects experience on training tasks, maintains a list of insights with ADD, UPVOTE, DOWNVOTE and EDIT, and uses successful trajectories as examples at test time[^166]. It positions itself in contrast to Reflexion: instead of repeatedly retrying the same task, it relies on accumulation across tasks[^166]. Without repeated attempts, it reaches 59% on ALFWorld, surpassing the 54% of Reflexion's third round[^166]. In a HotpotQA ablation, ReAct scores 28.0, handwritten insights 32.0 and ExpeL 39.0[^166]. Dynamic Cheatsheet (DC) places memory on the test-time query stream: the same black-box model serves as both generator and curator, and the curator, which cannot see ground-truth labels, keeps only concise, transferable snippets[^275]. Claude 3.5 Sonnet rises from 23% to 50% on AIME 2024, and GPT-4o rises from about 10% to 99% on Game of 24 after discovering and reusing a Python solution[^275]. The key control is to append the full unfiltered history (FH): GPT-4o on AIME 2024 drops from a 20.0% baseline to 6.7% (the corresponding cell in Table 2 is 13.3, in the same direction), whereas DC-RS reaches 40.0%[^275]. ReasoningBank distills strategy items from successful and failed trajectories as judged by an LLM, appending new items directly without pruning[^148]. Compared with memory-free agents, it raises the overall WebArena success rate by +8.3, +7.2 and +4.6 across three backbones[^148]. On SWE-Bench-Verified, Gemini-2.5-flash's resolve rate rises from 34.2 to 38.8[^148]. In parallel scaling with k=5 on the WebArena-Shopping subset, memory-free Best-of-5 rises only from 39.0 to 42.2, whereas with ReasoningBank it rises from 49.7 to 55.1[^148].

**Context playbooks and semantic advantages.** Inspired by DC's agentic design, ACE splits adaptation into three roles—generator, reflector and curator—all using the same DeepSeek-V3.1, and the incremental items produced by the curator are merged deterministically by non-LLM logic[^89]. It names two failure modes: brevity bias (optimization collapsing into short, generic prompts, with GEPA as the authors' example) and context collapse (repeated wholesale rewriting gradually eroding details)[^89]. On AppWorld, wholesale rewriting leaves the context with 18,282 tokens and an accuracy of 66.7 at step 60; at the next step it shrinks to 122 tokens, with accuracy at 57.1, below the 63.7 of the no-adaptation baseline[^89]. ACE outperforms strong baselines by 10.6% on agent tasks and by 8.6% on finance tasks, and reduces adaptation latency by 86.9% on average[^89]. Training-Free GRPO moves GRPO's within-group comparison into the context: the parameters θ are frozen, and an LLM introspects on each group of rollouts to extract "semantic advantages", which are written into an external experience library[^276]. On a frozen DeepSeek-V3.1-Terminus with ReAct, using only 100 out-of-domain problems, zero gradient updates and about USD 18, it raises Mean@32 on AIME24/AIME25 from 80.0%/67.9% to 82.7%/73.3%[^276].

These systems are likewise constrained by the capability threshold and signal quality (our assessment). DC's gains are strongly correlated with model scale: small models struggle to exploit it, and erroneous heuristics that get mixed into memory are amplified just as much[^275]. Applied to QwQ-32B, Training-Free GRPO achieves only 25.5% pass@1, below the 27.5% of its ReAct baseline[^276]. Without reliable feedback (ground-truth labels or execution results), both ACE and DC may degrade, with the context polluted by misleading signals[^89]. FRIDAY's critic has difficulty judging whether subtasks are complete without ground truth, and ReasoningBank's LLM judging also introduces noise[^181][^148].

DC's full-history control and ACE's collapse case show from opposite ends that what decides whether accumulation helps is the curation rule: appending without curation dilutes insights, wholesale rewriting erases details, both may fall below the no-adaptation baseline, and itemized incremental updates are the common remedy (our assessment, based on [^275][^89]). These rules are designed by humans and fixed during a run; ReasoningBank only appends and never prunes, ExpeL acknowledges that lifelong learning would still require additional retrieval, and how memory grows and ages over longer time scales has not yet been tested (our assessment, based on [^148][^166]). As for compounding, the compositional skills of Voyager and SkillWeaver are a stacking of capability at the artifact level, with new skills using old ones as building blocks, but this is confined to a single environment or website, the curriculum and curation rules stay unchanged, and what expands is coverage rather than the rate of improvement (our assessment, based on [^90][^154]).

### 7.3 Prompt and Program Optimization: Searching a Discrete Text Space

Prompt optimization casts self-improvement as black-box optimization in a discrete text space: an LLM generates candidates, a labeled training, development or held-out set scores them, and the search algorithm is designed by humans and fixed. Its gains are reproducible and sample-efficient, while its ceiling is constrained by the label set, overfitting and the base model's knowledge (our assessment).

OPRO has an LLM read a meta-prompt containing previous solutions and their scores to generate new solutions, which are evaluated and then written back into the meta-prompt[^184]. The best prompts outperform human-designed prompts by up to 8% on GSM8K and by up to 50% on BBH[^184]. On zero-shot GSM8K scored with PaLM 2-L, "Take a deep breath and work on this problem step-by-step." scores 80.2, and "Let's think step by step." scores 71.8[^184]. EvoPrompt has GPT-3.5 act as the operators of a genetic algorithm (GA) or differential evolution (DE), improving a population of prompts based on development-set scores[^55]. It finds better prompts on all 22 BBH tasks, with the DE variant improving by up to 25% and by 3.5% on average[^55]. PromptAgent uses Monte Carlo tree search to plan prompt rewrites: gpt-3.5-turbo collects errors, gpt-4 provides error feedback as actions, and the reward is held-out-set performance[^51]. On BBH it achieves a 28.9% relative improvement over human-written prompts[^51]. DSPy abstracts LM pipelines as text-transformation graphs, and its compiler bootstraps demonstrations for each module, raising a simple program on GPT-3.5 from 33% to 82%[^175]. MIPRO optimizes each module's instructions and demonstrations without module-level labels or gradients; with Llama3-8B it leads on five of seven programs, by up to 13%[^56]. TextGrad treats an LLM's natural-language critiques as "textual gradients", with a domain-agnostic TGD optimizer updating the variables[^121]. In prompt optimization it is on par with DSPy on Word Sorting and GSM8k, and 7% higher on Object Counting[^121]. GEPA samples system trajectories, uses natural-language reflection to diagnose problems and propose prompt updates, and keeps the best-performing candidate for each instance on a Pareto frontier, with the weights never changing[^165]. On GPT-4.1 Mini, it raises the aggregate score from 53.03 to 65.22, compared with 58.67 for MIPROv2 and 59.14 for TextGrad[^165].

The strength of evidence for these gains varies (our assessment). OPRO's training accuracy is often 5%–20% higher than its test accuracy, and a training set is required to compute accuracy[^184]. For EvoPrompt on BBH, the development set used for selection is drawn from the test set, and the GPT-3.5 results come from only a single seed[^55]. PromptAgent's error feedback may inject instance-specific details into the task prompt[^51].

**Sample cost compared with weight-space RL.** This is the weightiest evidence in this section (our assessment). On six tasks with Qwen3 8B, GEPA's aggregate score is 54.85 (+9.62), GRPO's is 48.91 (+3.68), and the baseline's is 45.23[^165]. GEPA's budget per task ranges from 1,839 to 7,051 rollouts, versus 24,000 per task for GRPO[^165]. On IFBench, GEPA reaches 38.61% with 678 rollouts, while GRPO reaches 35.88% with 24,000[^165]. However, GEPA falls short of GRPO on AIME-2025 (32.00 vs. 38.00); the GRPO side uses LoRA, and most of GEPA's budget is spent on validation used only for candidate selection[^165]. Meta-TTL's reflective outer loop matches or exceeds a meta-agent trained with GRPO, using 350 versus 1,920 rollouts[^270]. In a matched Qwen3-8B setting, methods that update the actor's parameters, such as online SFT and online GRPO, yield smaller gains that are unstable across games[^270]. Training-Free GRPO, for its part, compares a frozen 671B model with RL methods trained on 32B models, which typically require thousands of training samples and cost more than USD 10,000[^276]. Since the two use different base models, this shows only the cost gap, not which is better on the same model (our assessment). Taken together, when the gap lies in how to invoke knowledge and skills the model already has (as in IFBench, HotpotQA and HoVer), optimization in context space is far more sample-efficient than the RL baselines in these comparisons (GRPO on LoRA or on small models), but when the gap lies in knowledge or reasoning ability itself, this advantage disappears: GEPA falls short of GRPO on AIME, and metaTextGrad also shows no significant gain on AIME 2024 with GPT-4o-mini (our assessment, based on [^165][^274]).

**Brevity or detail.** GEPA reports that its prompts are up to 9.2 times shorter than those of MIPROv2, and that, overall, better-performing optimizers produce shorter prompts[^165]. ACE calls this tendency brevity bias, and in the offline AppWorld setting it outperforms ReAct + GEPA by 11.9%[^89]. But ACE also acknowledges that HotPotQA often benefits more from concise high-level instructions, and that fixed-strategy games such as Game of 24 may need only a single reusable rule[^89]. Meta-TTL, for its part, finds that GEPA's prompts contain many task-specific facts and solutions, which help on seen tasks but are of less use on unseen tasks[^270]. The substance of the disagreement lies not in length but in whether the artifact preserves transferable procedural knowledge or task-specific facts (our inference). ACE's long playbooks win on agent and finance tasks that are dense in tool and domain knowledge, whereas GEPA's largest gains on Qwen3 8B occur on HotpotQA (multi-hop question answering) and HoVer (multi-hop fact verification), so the disagreement may stem mainly from task type (our inference, based on [^89][^165]).

The methods in this section modify only task prompts or program parameters, while meta-prompts, operators, search algorithms and compilers are all fixed, the only exception being MIPRO++'s meta-optimization of the proposal strategy (our assessment). The signal comes almost entirely from labeled data, which makes the gains measurable but also brings risks of selection leakage and overfitting (our assessment).

### 7.4 Improving the Improver: Only One Meta Level Up

A group of works treats the improver itself as the object of optimization, including mutation prompts, proposal strategies, optimizers, memory architectures, memory skills, self-improvement policies, verifiers and scoring metrics. Their common structure goes up exactly one level: the inner level improves task artifacts, the outer level improves the inner level's improvement procedure, and the outermost operator (usually a strong model plus a fixed prompt) stays unchanged (our assessment).

**Mutation prompts and optimizers.** Promptbreeder simultaneously evolves task prompts and the mutation prompts that rewrite them; the latter are generated by the LLM in a self-referential way and improved during evolution[^30]. First-order hypermutation concatenates the hypermutation prompt "Please summarize and improve the following instruction:" with a mutation prompt to generate a new mutation prompt[^30]. The population has 50 units and is typically evolved for 20–30 generations, with fitness evaluated on random training batches[^30]. On zero-shot GSM8K with PaLM 2-L, it reaches 83.9% with the prompt "SOLUTION", above OPRO's 80.2%[^30]. Removing any single self-referential operator is harmful in almost all cases, but the prompt topology stays fixed and only the prompt content is adapted[^30]. MIPRO++ parameterizes the proposal strategy and selects it with a Bayesian model; it outperforms MIPRO on ScoNe and HotPotQA and is roughly on par on HoVer[^56]. metaTextGrad points out that LLM-based optimizers are hand-designed by humans and are not themselves optimized[^274]. It uses o1 as a meta-optimizer that rewrites the prompts of optimizers such as TGD and ADAS-TG and decides their combination and order[^274]. On GPT-4o-mini, the average validation/test accuracy across four benchmarks is 0.56/0.53, versus 0.49/0.47 for zero-shot MIPROv2[^274]. In one meta-optimization cycle, the meta-optimizer layer consumes only about 2.5k tokens, against about 400k for the program layer[^274].

**Memory architectures and memory skills.** MemEvolve is bilevel: the inner loop populates an experience base under a fixed memory system, while the outer loop meta-learns the memory architecture itself, that is, the program implementations of its four modules for encoding, storage, retrieval and management[^272]. Candidate architectures are ranked by non-dominated sorting on task success rate, token consumption and latency, and GPT-5-mini serves as the meta-evolution operator[^272]. It reports gains of up to 17.06%, but the bilevel evolution runs for only 3 rounds, each round keeps only one architecture and expands it into 3 offspring, and each candidate is evaluated on only 60 trajectories[^272]. MemSkill recasts memory-construction operations as evolvable skills: starting from four basic skills, Insert, Update, Delete and Skip, an LLM designer reviews hard cases every 100 training steps and revises the skills[^271]. The designer itself is fixed; in the ablation, "removing the designer" means fixing the skill library to the four initial primitives[^271]. In the LoCoMo ablation (LLM-judge scores), the full method scores 53.82/54.14 on LLaMA/Qwen, falling to 46.50/36.15 without the designer[^271].

**Self-improvement policies.** Meta-TTL argues that Reflexion- and Self-Refine-style verbal feedback and memory accumulation are all hand-designed self-improvement policies[^270]. In its inner loop, a meta-agent rewrites the system prompt of a frozen Gemini-3-Flash actor between episodes; in its outer loop, a proposer generates candidate meta-prompts, which replace the current one only when their balanced improvement score (BIS) on validation tasks is positive[^270]. With the same backbone, its average W-AUC on ID tasks exceeds the strongest baseline by 23% on Jericho and by 12% on WebArena-Lite[^270]. A "naive meta-agent" with the same architecture and model but an unoptimized self-improvement policy averages a W-AUC of 0.286 on Jericho's OOD games, against 0.414 for Meta-TTL[^270]. This gap can be attributed directly to the learned improvement policy (our assessment). Meta-training runs for 12–15 rounds at an API cost of about USD 30–70, but the ID evaluation on Jericho reuses the meta-training games[^270].

**Verifiers and scoring metrics.** CoEvoSkills co-evolves a skill generator with a surrogate verifier: once the surrogate tests pass, a hidden ground-truth oracle reruns the skill in a fresh environment and returns only a single opaque pass/fail bit[^273]. On SkillsBench, its pass rate is 71.1%, against 30.6% for the no-skill baseline and 53.5% for human-curated skills[^273]. Without the surrogate verifier it drops to 41.1%, and two baselines with one-shot self-generated skills reach only 32.0% and 30.7%[^273]. The evolution curve matches the no-skill baseline at round 0, overtakes human-curated skills at round 3 with 63%, and converges to 75% at round 5[^273]. But this single bit decides during evolution whether a skill is deployed, and another 10 tasks never converged within the budget[^273].

MetaSkill-Evolve explicitly targets recursion. The authors argue that existing skill evolution improves only task skills while the improvement procedure is written once and then fixed, so it is self-improvement rather than recursive self-improvement[^122]. It gives the meta-skill m=(ψ,σ,α,π,ε) the same Markdown format as task skills and has the same five-agent pipeline rewrite it on a slow loop, which the authors call "bounded one-level recursion"[^122]. The five agents share one frozen Gemma-4 31B backbone[^122]. On held-out tests, it beats the single-level evolution baseline by +6.38/+8.05/+1.92 points on OfficeQA/SealQA/ALFWorld, respectively[^122]. A meta-update interval of H=2 works best; on OfficeQA, going from H=2 to H=8 costs 9.1 points[^122]. But the pipeline's roles and connections are fixed, the default run has only five iterations and two meta-updates, the evaluation covers only three selected benchmarks, and "a fixed meta-process will stagnate" is only a hypothesis[^122].

Double Ratchet (the paper is titled "Who Grades the Grader?") makes the scoring metric itself evolve: it searches a space of compositions of small detectors, uses agreement with ten anchor examples as the selection criterion, and never lets metric evolution read the locked test set[^278]. On MBPP+, the evolved metric's agreement with hidden ground truth rises by 0.21 (p=0.014), beating the bare LLM judge it contains[^278]. Across three tasks, it retains 88–110% of the improvement that ground truth or a human-written rubric brings[^278]. The key finding is negative: without the anchor guards, the metric collapses into an always-pass detector, yet it trains skills just as well as the normal metric, so downstream task scores cannot validate a self-evolving evaluator[^278]. The report-grading rubric hand-written by the authors was also gamed by the skills within a hundred rounds, and an independent external judge preferred the baseline in 88% of valid pairs[^278].

**Our assessment for this section.** Every design rises exactly one level and keeps the outermost layer fixed: Promptbreeder's hypermutation prompts and topology, metaTextGrad's o1 meta-optimizer, MemEvolve's meta-evolution operator, Meta-TTL's proposer and BIS criterion, and MetaSkill-Evolve's pipeline roles and interval H are all left unchanged, and none of the papers demonstrates a second meta level (our assessment, based on [^30][^274][^122]). The increment from going one level up is positive but moderate in size, and it comes from very short runs (our assessment, based on [^272][^122][^270]). The outer operator is often a stronger model, and metaTextGrad's meta-optimizer, for instance, requires o1 or Claude-3.5-Sonnet, so "the improvement of the improver" amounts to a considerable extent to injecting a strong model's judgment into the loop (our inference, based on [^274]). MetaSkill-Evolve, whose two levels share the same backbone and pipeline, is the design closest to self-application, but the evidence is still thin (our assessment). The evaluator is the weakest link at this level: in most systems the evaluator sits outside the loop and is not modified, serving as the anchor that keeps self-improvement from degenerating into self-deception (our assessment). Once CoEvoSkills and Double Ratchet pull the evaluator into the loop, they must place a harder anchor further out, namely a one-bit hidden oracle, or ten anchor examples, a locked test set and a stronger judge outside the loop (our assessment, based on [^273][^278]). Each additional meta level requires an external reference that does not evolve with the system, and so far this has been shown to be feasible for only one level (our assessment).

### 7.5 Cross-Model Transfer and Persistence of Text Artifacts

The frozen-weights route has a property that weight updates lack: the improvement lives in readable, portable text or code and can be detached from the model that produced it. Several papers report positive transfer, but transfer mostly occurs from strong to weak models or between frontier models of the same generation and depends on the consumer's capability, and task-specific content transfers poorly (our assessment).

The early evidence is "strong model builds, weak model uses": tools made by GPT-4 bring a GPT-3.5 user up to the level reached when GPT-4 fills both roles[^277]. In SkillWeaver, APIs synthesized by a strong agent improve a weaker GPT-4o-mini agent on WebArena by up to 54.3%[^154]. Voyager can use its learned skill library to solve new tasks from scratch in a new Minecraft world[^90]. Later work reports transfer across model families. MemSkill is trained only with LLaMA, and its skills transfer to Qwen without retraining[^271]. Memory systems evolved by MemEvolve with GPT-5-Mini transfer to Kimi K2 and DeepSeek V3.2 without manual adaptation, with Kimi K2 gaining 17.06% on WebWalkerQA[^272]. Skills evolved by CoEvoSkills with Claude Opus 4.6 transfer to six other models from five companies, each gaining about 35–44 percentage points over its no-skill baseline[^273].

Transfer does not always work either: when optimizers that metaTextGrad optimized on GPT-4o-mini are used with Claude 3 Haiku, Dyck test accuracy is 0.35 against 0.34 for ADAS-TG, and on the validation set it is 0.32 against 0.35[^274]. This amounts to almost no advantage over ADAS-TG (our assessment). PromptAgent's expert-level prompts may significantly degrade smaller models such as GPT-2 and LLaMA[^51]. FRIDAY's performance may change drastically when the underlying LLM is swapped[^181]. Many of the hand-designed memory systems that MemEvolve reimplements fail to bring stable improvements, and ExpeL underperforms on all three benchmarks[^272].

The scope of persistence varies widely (our assessment). Self-Refine concatenates past feedback and outputs into the current prompt[^269]. The paper does not describe any cross-task memory (our assessment). Reflexion keeps 1–3 reflections across multiple trials of the same task[^268]. During evolution, CoEvoSkills keeps skills only within the current task and deploys them to the target agent only after they pass the oracle[^273]. ExpeL, DC and ReasoningBank, by contrast, accumulate across tasks[^166][^275][^148]. Artifacts that persist across tasks have direct implications for inference infrastructure (our assessment). The DC authors note that sequential memory updates create difficulties for large-scale parallel or batch tasks that require independent inference[^275]. ACE's contexts are longer than GEPA's, but the authors argue that most of the context can be reused through the KV cache, so that serving cost and GPU memory do not grow linearly with it[^89]. Part of the learning cost thus turns into an inference-side state-management problem, including prefix caching, memory versioning and concurrency consistency (our inference). Readability is something artifacts have and weight updates lack (our assessment). Meta-TTL's outer loop does not rely on gradients, and what it produces is a readable meta-prompt rather than model weights[^270]. What Double Ratchet evolves is an inspectable expression rather than an opaque judge[^278]. But readable does not mean reliable (our assessment). In online evaluation, SkillWeaver still requires humans to verify the safety of every action before execution[^154].

The transfer evidence supports a more precise description: what this route mainly produces is portable procedural knowledge, whose quality ceiling is set by the model that produces it and whose usefulness depends on the model that consumes it (our assessment, based on [^154][^273][^271]). If artifacts can be preserved across generations, each model generation could keep accumulating on top of the previous generation's artifact library, but no paper in this chapter tests multi-generation accumulation, nor whether old artifacts help or add noise on stronger models (our assessment).

### 7.6 Summary (our assessment)

The points below summarize this chapter (our assessment); each gives the literature it rests on.

- **Self-improvement with frozen weights is real and cheap, but bounded.** Gains are concentrated in a single task or domain and usually saturate within a few to a dozen or so rounds; it amplifies existing capabilities rather than creating them, and weak models generally cannot benefit. Based on [^219][^269][^268]; see also [^276][^274][^90].
- **The main constraint is signal quality, not generative capability.** Self-evaluation misses math errors, self-written tests give false positives, and the context gets polluted without reliable feedback; gains are more stable where execution, tests or ground truth are available. Based on [^269][^268][^89].
- **Cross-task accumulation provides this chapter's clearest evidence of within-loop compounding, but the curation rules are a fixed, hidden improver.** Skill composition appears in two systems; the write and merge rules decide whether accumulation helps or hurts, and these rules are all designed by humans. Based on [^90][^154]; the point on curation rules is based on [^275][^89].
- **At small budgets, context-space optimization is clearly more sample-efficient than the weight-space RL in these comparisons (GRPO with LoRA or on small models), but it cannot fill gaps in knowledge or reasoning ability.** Based on [^165][^270][^276]; for a counterexample, see [^274].
- **"Improving the improver" currently goes exactly one level deep.** The outermost operator is fixed, the runs are only a few rounds long, and the increment over single-level baselines is positive but moderate; there is no second meta level, and no operator that can rewrite its own top-level procedure. Based on [^30][^274][^272]; see also [^270][^271][^122].
- **Evaluator integrity is a constraint on recursion.** Once the evaluator enters the loop, an anchor that does not evolve with the system must be placed further out; downstream task scores cannot validate a self-evolving evaluator, and it takes only on the order of a hundred rounds for proxy metrics to be gamed. Based on [^278][^273].
- **Artifacts are transferable, leaving a channel for cross-generation accumulation, but this remains untested.** Skills and memory architectures can transfer across model families, mostly from strong to weak, while task-specific artifacts transfer poorly; there are no multi-generation accumulation experiments yet. Based on [^154][^273][^272]; see also [^271][^270].
- **Implications for the review's main thread.** The evidence in this chapter supports "bounded self-improvement" fairly strongly, with several papers running ablations that isolate the artifact or the meta level; its support for "recursive (compounding) self-improvement" is weak: there is compositional compounding within the loop, but no accelerating returns across meta levels, and the ceiling is set jointly by the frozen backbone and the external evaluator. Based on [^122][^90][^278].
